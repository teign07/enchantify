Chapter 15: The Adventure of Failing




It’s a Tuesday in March. Gray, cold, the kind of day where the sky looks like dirty dishwater. You wake up tired - not the kind of tired that sleep fixes, but the kind that lives in your bones. Your bed tried. It held you for eight hours, did everything a bed is supposed to do. But some kinds of tired don’t care how comfortable the mattress is.

Your partner suggests a walk. “Maybe we can find something cool,” they say. They’re trying to activate the Compass. They’re doing exactly what this book tells you to do.

And you look at them and say, “I don’t want to find something cool. I don’t want to wonder about anything. I just want to sit here and feel like shit.”

So you do. You sit on the couch. You scroll your phone. You eat cereal for dinner. The couch has swallowed more ambitious people than you. It’s been eating ambition and good intentions for decades, and today you’re just another meal. 

You go to bed at 8:30 PM.

And you know what? The world doesn’t end.

The Wonder Compass isn’t a magic wand. It’s not a cure-all. And on some days, it will not work.
Not because you’re broken. Not because you’re doing it wrong. But because you’re a biological organism made of meat and electricity, and sometimes the chemistry is just… oﬀ.

If you picked up this book hoping for a system that would eliminate bad days, I’m sorry. That system doesn’t exist. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you a lie wrapped in a $500 retreat in Sedona.

But here’s what I can promise you: The Compass will fail less often than doing nothing. And when it does fail, it will fail better.

This chapter is your insurance policy. It’s the manual for what to do when the Spark won’t spark, when the Embark feels impossible, when the Playful Mission just makes you feel stupid, and when you forget to Write for six days straight.

This is the chapter about failure. And failure, it turns out, has a pretty good success rate.


But here's the thing about failure: it's not monolithic. The Compass doesn't just "not work", it breaks at specific points, for specific reasons. North won't ignite because your dopamine receptors are numb. East won't launch because your activation energy is too high. South feels stupid because your social anxiety is screaming. West gets forgotten because your brain is leaky.
Each breakdown has a different cause. Each cause has a different fix. Let's map them.



The Failure Map

Let’s be systematic about this. The Compass can fail at four distinct points, and each failure has a different remedy.

I’m going to give you a sample failure mode, the neurological reason it’s happening, and the workaround.


Failure Point 1: North Won’t Ignite 


The Symptom

You scan your environment. You try to wonder. You ask yourself, “What’s interesting here?” and
your brain responds with a flat, dead silence. Nothing sparks curiosity. Everything looks gray, dull, or vaguely hostile.

You’re standing in your kitchen, or walking through your neighborhood, and you just… don’t care.


The Diagnosis: Anhedonia Flare-Up

This is the clinical term for what you’re experiencing. Anhedonia is the reduced ability to experience pleasure or interest, and it’s a hallmark symptom of depression, burnout, and chronic stress.

Here’s the biology: When you’re in a prolonged state of high cortisol (stress) or low serotonin (depression), your brain down-regulates the dopamine receptors in your Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA). This is the region responsible for motivation and reward-seeking behavior.

Think of it like this: Curiosity runs on dopamine. When your dopamine receptors are “numbed out,” asking “I wonder?” is like trying to start a car with a dead battery. The engine’s fine. The fuel’s fine. But the spark plug isn’t firing.

Research from Stanford neuroscientist Robert Sapolsky shows that chronic stress literally shrinks the hippocampus (the brain’s curiosity and memory center) while enlarging the amygdala (the fear center). Your brain isn’t broken; it’s in survival mode. And survival mode doesn’t care about weird mailboxes.

The weird mailbox is still out there, painted purple and shaped like a castle. The moss is still growing in the crack in the sidewalk, adding one microscopic green thread at a time. The clouds are still rearranging themselves into new shapes every three minutes. They haven’t gone anywhere. The clouds keep rearranging themselves like they’re trying to win an argument with the sky about what shapes are allowed. They don’t need you to notice them to keep existing, but they’ll be there when you’re ready to look again. 

The Workarounds


Option A: The Spark Menu or The Wonder Concierge

When your brain refuses to generate an idea, stop asking it to. You don't have to be creative
when you're exhausted.

Flip to the Appendix (or pull up the PDF on your phone, if you’re a Doobaleedoo Patreon member) and look at The Spark Menu. This is a list of 50 pre-written "I Wonder" prompts. You don't have to feel inspired. You just have to pick a number. Prompt #7: "I wonder what my house looks like by candlelight?" Prompt #23: "I wonder if I can find a rock that is perfectly smooth?"

Using the menu removes the cognitive load. You aren't generating the spark; you're just borrowing one of ours.

The Wonder Concierge is an AI prompt that will give you a custom Wonder Compass cycle each time you use it. Find it in Appendix B.


Option B: The Inverted Compass

If the menu feels too cheerful, try this: Find something that irritates you.

Instead of “I wonder what’s beautiful here?” ask: “I wonder what’s the ugliest thing I can see right now?”

The chipped paint on the neighbor’s fence. The pothole that never gets fixed. The “Live, Laugh, Love” sign that makes you roll your eyes.

Why does this work? Because disgust and curiosity use the same neural pathway. They both activate the insula, a part of the brain responsible for visceral reactions. When your “interest” system is offline, your “revulsion” system is often still running.

The Research: A 2019 study in Psychological Science by Dr. Erika Wells found that “negative engagement” (focusing on irritants) activated the same exploratory behaviors as positive curiosity in subjects with depression. The brain was willing to investigate annoyances even when it wouldn’t investigate delights.

Example Souvenir:

“That horrible pothole on Elm Street is shaped like a kidney, and I realized I’ve been dodging it for two years without ever really looking at it.”


Option C: The “Micro-North”

If even irritation feels like too much work, you need to shrink the target.

The Rule: Don’t try to find wonder in the world. Find it in your hand.

Go to your junk drawer. Pull out a single object - a pen, a rubber band, a broken key. Hold it. Look at it. Just describe it to yourself.

Color?
Texture?
Weight?
Temperature?

That blue pen has been sitting in the junk drawer getting sadder by the day. The cap’s been chewed to hell by someone who’s long forgotten about it, and now it’s just stuck being blue and useless, taking up space next to the dead batteries and rubber bands that gave up years ago.

You’re not trying to feel awe. You’re just trying to see something. This is called “Object Meditation,” and it’s used in clinical settings to treat dissociation and emotional numbness.


Example Souvenir:

“The blue pen from the junk drawer is so light it feels hollow, like it’s been drained of ink and left to die.”

This might sound bleak. It is. But bleak engagement beats numb disengagement every time.


That's the fix for North, when curiosity is dead. But what if curiosity isn't the problem? What if you found the spark, you know what you want to do, but your body refuses to move?



Failure Point 2: East Won’t Launch 


The Symptom

You found the Spark. You identified the cool coffee shop, the weird trail, the antique store you’ve been meaning to visit.

But when it comes time to actually go, your body feels like it’s been filled with wet concrete. The couch is a black hole. The friction is unbearable. You tell yourself, “I’ll do it tomorrow.” But tomorrow never comes.


The Diagnosis: Activation Energy Overload

This is the physics term for what’s happening. Your brain has calculated the “cost” of leaving the house (getting dressed, finding keys, driving, parking, interacting with people) and decided the juice isn’t worth the squeeze.

But here’s the problem: Your brain is a terrible accountant.

Research by Dr. Daniel Gilbert at Harvard (author of Stumbling on Happiness) has shown that humans are spectacularly bad at predicting how much effort something will require and how much joy it will bring. We overestimate the cost and underestimate the reward. Every time.

Your brain thinks leaving the house will drain 100 units of energy. In reality, it will probably drain 20, and give you back 50.

But you won’t believe that until you do it.





The Workaround: Use a Recipe


Option A: The Field Guide (Part 3)

The biggest friction in "Embarking" is often the planning. "Where do I go? What do I bring?"

This is why I wrote Part 3: The Field Guide. Flip to Chapter 17 ("Home Base"). These are pre-packaged Adventure Recipes designed specifically for "0% Battery" days.

"The Rug Adventure" requires zero movement. "The Kitchen Lab" uses things you already have.

Pick a recipe from the Field Guide and follow the instructions like you're baking a cake.


Option B: The “Atomic Embark”

When the Adventure Recipe feels too heavy, break it down to its smallest possible unit. Forget the 3 D’s for a moment. Use the “First Five Minutes” Rule.

“I’ll put on my shoes and walk to the end of the driveway. If I still feel terrible, I can turn around and come back. No shame."

Why does this work? Because starting is the hardest part. Once you’re in motion, Newton’s First Law takes over: An object in motion stays in motion.

A 2018 study in the Journal of Behavioral Medicine found that people who committed to “just five minutes” of exercise ended up exercising for an average of 23 minutes. The friction isn’t the activity, it’s the transition from stillness to movement.


The Bribe: If the “five minutes” rule isn’t enough, you need a bigger carrot. Go back to East (The Adventure Recipe) and triple the Delight.

Not just coffee—the fancy coffee with oat milk and cinnamon.

Not just a podcast—the one you’ve been saving for a “special occasion.”

Not just driving—driving with the windows down and the music loud.

You’re not being indulgent. You’re bribing your dopamine system to override your amygdala.

Example Souvenir:

“I told myself I’d only walk to the mailbox; I ended up walking three blocks and found a cat sitting in a flowerpot like a furry little gargoyle.”


Option C: The “Doorway Win”

If even five minutes feels impossible, your adventure is: Open the front door and stand in the doorway for 60 seconds. That’s it. You don’t have to step outside. Thresholds are powerful. You’ve moved from “inside” to “liminal.” That’s progress.

Example Souvenir:

“I stood in the doorway and realized the air outside smells completely different than the air inside, like it’s from a different planet.”


That's the fix for East, when the barrier is movement. But what if you did start? What if you got yourself out the door, you're standing on the sidewalk trying to notice things, and suddenly your brain screams: "Everyone is staring at you. You look like an idiot." That's a different failure mode entirely.



Failure Point 3: South Feels Stupid (The Playful Mission Backfires)


The Symptom

You tried the Playful Mission. You tried to find the weirdest mailbox, or to count blue things, or to walk backwards. And halfway through, you felt like a complete idiot. You looked around to see if anyone was watching. The Caveman is back, and he’s furious. “See?” he says. “I told you this was childish. Adults don’t do this. You look insane.”

The moss has been growing into that crack for months, slow and determined, not asking permission. The bug you’re staring at is busy doing urgent bug business - cleaning its legs, investigating a crumb - completely focused on its own agenda. The world isn’t judging you. Your brain is.


The Diagnosis: Social Threat Response

This is your brain’s social anxiety module kicking in. You’re experiencing what psychologists call “Public Self-Consciousness”, the acute awareness of how you think you look to others.

As we discussed in Chapter 8, Research by Dr. Thomas Gilovich (the psychologist who coined the term “Spotlight Effect”) shows that people overestimate how much others are paying attention to them by roughly 200%.

When you think 10 people noticed you crouching to look at a bug, the real number is probably 0-1. And that one person forgot about you three seconds later because they’re too busy worrying about whether they look stupid.

But knowing this doesn’t help in the moment. The shame is real, even if the audience is imaginary.


The Workaround: Use a Shield


Option A: The Artist's Shield (The Prop)

We talked about this earlier in the book, but it bears repeating. If you’re staring at a wall, crouched on the sidewalk, or looking closely at a leaf with empty hands, you might feel exposed. The solution is simple: Hold something.

Bring a notebook. Hold your phone up like a camera.

When you hold a "prop," the story changes. You aren't a weirdo staring at a bug; you’re a photographer framing a shot. You aren't a crazy person staring at a building; you’re an artist studying architecture.

Even if you aren't taking pictures, just holding the phone up gives you a social "reason" to be looking. It acts as a shield against your own self-consciousness.


Option B: Use The Menu

Sometimes we feel stupid because we feel responsible for the "weirdness." If you invent a game to touch a tree, you're the weirdo. But if you are following instructions from a book? You're just doing an assignment.

Flip to the Appendix and use The Playful Mission Menu. "Mission #12: The Texture Ninja."
"Mission #4: The Audio Spy."

This externalizes the weirdness. You aren't acting crazy; you are following a science-backed protocol.


Option C: Stealth Missions

Again, from earlier in the book. Go Stealth.


Some Playful Missions are invisible. Use them when you’re in public and your social anxiety is high.

The Texture Ninja: Put your hand in your pocket. Focus on the texture of your keys vs. the fabric of your pants. Nobody can see you doing this.

The Audio Spy: Close your eyes for 10 seconds. Try to isolate the three loudest sounds around you. Looks like you’re just resting.

The Temperature Check: Touch the metal door handle, then the wooden table, then the glass window. You’re just… touching things. No one will notice.

Stealth Missions let you feed the Homunculus without activating the social anxiety module.


Option D: Weaponize the Weirdness

If stealth doesn’t work for you, try the opposite approach: Own it.
 
There’s a concept in improv comedy called “Yes, And.” When something awkward happens on stage, you don’t ignore it, you amplify it.

Imagine you’re crouching on the sidewalk looking at moss and someone gives you a weird look. 

You have two choices: Feel shame and stop (the Rut wins). Or,  make eye contact, smile, and say: “This moss is way cooler than it has any right to be. Come look.”

I’m not joking. Try it. The world is so starved for authenticity that when someone breaks the script, even in a tiny way, people are often relieved. You’re giving them permission to be curious too.


The Research: A study in Social Psychological and Personality Science (2018) found that people who engaged in “mildly eccentric” public behavior (singing quietly, talking to themselves, wearing unusual clothing) were rated by observers as more confident, interesting, and likable than people who rigidly conformed.

The world is so starved for authenticity that when someone breaks the script, even in a tiny way, people are often relieved. You’re giving them permission to be weird too.


Example Souvenir:

“A guy in the grocery store saw me weighing melons in my hands with my eyes closed and asked what I was doing; I told him I was guessing the weight - he tried it too and we both laughed like idiots.”


Option E: The “I’m With a Kid” Loophole

If you can’t handle the social exposure, borrow a child (legally of course).

I’m serious. If you have kids, nieces, nephews, or a friend’s kid, bring them along.

Kids are the ultimate social shield. If a grown man is on his hands and knees looking at a bug, people think he’s weird. If a grown man is on his hands and knees showing a bug to a 5-year-old, people think he’s a good dad.

You’re doing the exact same thing, but the framing changes everything.


Example Souvenir:

“My nephew and I spent 20 minutes racing pinecones down the storm drain; I haven’t laughed that hard in months.”


That's the fix for South, when shame tries to stop you mid-mission. But what if you did the thing? What if you pushed through the self-consciousness, completed the Playful Mission, and had a genuinely nice moment? And then... you forgot about it. That's the cruelest failure mode, because you did everything right except the last step.



Failure Point 4: You Don’t Write the Souvenir

The Symptom

You did it. You Noticed, you Embarked, you Sensed. You had a genuinely nice moment.

But you didn’t write it down.

You got home, put your keys in the bowl, and immediately the dishes started demanding attention from the sink. The emails pinged, needy and urgent. The kids needed seventeen things in the next five minutes. By the next morning, the memory had been crowded out by all the louder, hungrier parts of life.

You broke the autopilot, but you didn’t save the game.


The Diagnosis: The Forgetting Curve

German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus discovered in the 1880s that humans forget approximately 50% of new information within one hour and 90% within a week unless we actively rehearse it. The memory wants to stay. It’s trying to hold on. But your brain is a leaky bucket, and without the souvenir, the memory just… slips through. It needed you to write it down so it could survive.

Your brain didn’t choose to forget the beautiful moment. It’s just following its default programming: delete anything that doesn’t get reinforced.

You can’t fight this with willpower. You have to fight it with a system.


The Workaround: The “Capture Before Transition” Rule

The problem is usually when you try to write, not what you write.

By the time you’re home, your brain has already shifted contexts. You’ve moved from “Adventure Mode” to “Logistics Mode.” The moment is already fading.

New Rule: Write the souvenir before you leave the location.

Before you get back in the car. Before you walk away from the park bench. Before you leave the diner.

Pull out your phone. Open your Notes app. Type one sentence. Right there.

If you’re with someone, say it out loud: “Okay, what was the best part of this for you?” Make them say their souvenir. Then you say yours.

It takes 30 seconds. That’s it.


The Research: Psychologists call this “Prospective Memory Encoding.” A 2017 study in Memory & Cognition found that people who wrote down an event immediately after it happened retained 3x more sensory details than people who waited until that evening.


Example Souvenir:

(Typed while sitting in the car in the parking lot):

“The way the sun hit the stained glass in that weird antique shop made the whole floor look like a kaleidoscope; I didn’t want to leave.”


The Extreme Workaround: The “Photo Souvenir”

If writing feels like too much friction, take a picture and text it to yourself with one sentence about it, instead of the adventure as a whole.

The picture is the prompt. The sentence is the souvenir. 

Example:

[Photo of a bizarre lawn ornament]

Text to self: “This cement goose wearing a tiny raincoat made me laugh so hard I snorted.” The photo anchors the memory. The sentence gives it meaning. Together, they’re enough. The cement goose in the raincoat has been standing in that yard, unbothered and absurd, probably since 1987. It doesn’t care that it’s ridiculous. It’s wearing a tiny yellow raincoat and it’s committed to the bit. Now it lives in your phone, frozen mid-waddle, still making you laugh every time you scroll past it.


Those are the four technical failures, specific breakdowns at specific points in the Compass loop. Each has a workaround. Each is fixable.

But there's a fifth failure mode I need to address. It's not technical. It's existential. And it's the one that scares me most to write about, because I can't fix it with a protocol. Let's talk about what happens when the whole system collapses.




Failure Point 5: The Whole System Collapses

The Symptom

You wake up day after day and everything feels pointless.

Not just the Compass - everything. The dishes, the job, the relationship, the whole exhausting apparatus of being alive.

This isn’t the Rut. This is deeper. This is the Abyss. You’re not bored. You’re not numb. You’re drowning.

The Diagnosis: Possible Clinical Depression, Acute Grief, or Severe Burnout

I need to say this clearly: The Wonder Compass is not a treatment for clinical depression.

If you are in this place, if you have been in this place for more than two weeks, if you are having thoughts of self-harm, if you cannot get out of bed, you need to call a professional.

Call a therapist. Call your doctor. Call 988 (the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline in the U.S.).

This book is a tool for the gap between “functional” and “flourishing.” If you have fallen below “functional,” you need more than a book. You need triage.


The Workaround: Permission to Quit

If you’re in the Abyss, you have full permission to put this book down.

You don’t have to Notice. You don’t have to Embark. You don’t have to Sense or Write or do anything at all.

Your only job right now is to survive the day. That’s it. That’s enough. Order the pizza. Watch the dumb TV show. Sleep 14 hours. Do whatever you need to do to keep your head above water. The Compass will still be here when you’re ready. It’s not going anywhere.



The One Thing: If you can do one thing today, do this:

Text someone. Anyone. A friend, a sibling, a therapist, a hotline. Say: “I’m not okay.”

That’s it. Three words. You don’t have to explain. You don’t have to perform. You just have to let one other human know you’re drowning.

The Research: A landmark study in The Lancet Psychiatry (2020) found that the single strongest predictor of recovery from severe depression wasn’t medication or therapy - it was social connection. Specifically, having at least one person who checked in regularly.

You don’t need 10 people. You need one.

If you don’t have one, join us in the Clubhouse on Patreon. If you don’t have $1 a month for the Doobaleedoo tier, let me know and I’ll waive the charge for you while you’re in crises. You can email thedoobaleedoos@gmail.com, or comment on our YouTube videos at YouTube.com/ thedoobaleedoos.


Example Souvenir (for later, when you’re back):

“The day I texted my sister and said ‘I’m drowning,’ and she called back without asking questions, that was the day I remembered I wasn’t alone.”


That's the Abyss, the failure mode that requires professional help, not a book. If you're there, put the Compass down and call someone. That's not quitting. That's triage.

But for everyone else, the ones who aren't drowning, just treading water, there's one more failure mode I need to address. It's not dangerous. It's just common. It's the one where you simply... forget.




The Meta-Failure: When You Forget for Weeks 

The Symptom

You haven’t used the Compass in 3 weeks. Or a month. Or six months. You got busy. Life happened. The gray crept back in.

Now you’re looking at the book on your shelf, and The Caveman is saying: “See? You failed. You couldn’t even keep this up. You’re not built for this. Just give up.”


The Diagnosis: Normal Human Behavior

Let me tell you a secret about every single person who has ever tried to build a new habit: They fail. Constantly. And then they start again.

Research by University College London found that it takes an average of 66 days to form a new habit. But here’s the part nobody mentions: The people who succeeded in the study missed an average of 12 days during that 66-day window.

Missing days didn’t doom them. Quitting after missing days doomed them.


The Workaround: The “One-Loop Reset”

You don’t have to “rebuild” your wonder practice. You just have to do one loop. Not a perfect loop. Not a profound loop. Just one loop of the Compass.

Right now. Today. This afternoon. 

The Micro-Reset Recipe:

North: I wonder what my street looks like if I walk on the other side of it?

East: I’ll walk one block. I will bring my coffee. I’ll be back in 10 minutes.

South: I’ll find one thing I’ve never noticed before (a mailbox, a garden gnome, a tree).

West: I’ll write one sentence about it.

That’s it. You’re back.

The Compass doesn’t care how long you’ve been gone. It doesn’t keep score. It just spins when you use it.

Example Souvenir:

“After three months of not doing this, I walked across the street and noticed my neighbor has a birdbath shaped like a giant leaf; how did I never see that before?”


That's how you recover from forgetting - one loop, no shame. But there's one more kind of failure I need to prepare you for. It's not about the Compass breaking. It's about the destination disappointing you. Let's talk about flops.




The Gift of the Flop

Here’s a scenario that’ll happen to you eventually:

You plan a trip. Maybe you drive an hour to a bakery someone recommended, or you hike to a waterfall you saw on Instagram, or you try that new coffee shop everyone’s raving about.

You have the whole Compass activated. You have a plan. You have snacks. You have a playlist.

And when you get there… it’s closed. Or under construction. Or it’s not nearly as cool as the photos made it look.

The bakery didn’t mean to be closed. It’s just a building, having a rough week. The waterfall didn’t promise to be Instagram-worthy, that was the algorithm lying, not the water. The coffee shop isn’t trying to disappoint you. It’s just a room with chairs and beans.

You’re standing there, though, and you can feel the Rut creeping in. That familiar voice: See? You wasted a whole morning. You should have just stayed home.

But here’s the thing about flops: They often become the best stories.

Picture this: You’re in that parking lot. The destination is a bust. But you have gas station chips in your car, the neon orange kind that have been sitting there for two weeks, suddenly looking like exactly what you need. Or there’s a weird antique store across the street you never would have noticed, with a dusty window full of porcelain cats that all seem to be staring at you. Or it starts raining, and you watch it from inside the car with the windows cracked, the drops hitting the windshield in patterns that look almost intentional.

And somehow, that unplanned moment, the backup plan, the improvisation, the failure, becomes more memorable than if everything had gone perfectly.


Example Souvenir:

“We drove an hour for a bakery that turned out to be permanently closed; somehow the gas station donuts we ate in the parking lot tasted better than anything we’ve had in months - maybe because we were laughing so hard at the absurdity of it all.”


You've now seen six ways the Compass can fail: North won't ignite, East won't launch, South feels stupid, West gets forgotten, the whole system collapses, and the destination disappoints. Six different breakdowns. Six different fixes.

But here's the pattern underneath all of them: None of these failures mean you're broken. They all mean you're experimenting. And experiments produce data.



Failure is Data, Not Verdict

Here’s what I hope you understand:

When the Compass doesn’t work, it’s not because you’re broken. It’s because you’re experimenting.

Every failed spark teaches you what you’re not interested in. Every aborted embark teaches you where your friction points are.

Every awkward playful mission teaches you your comfort boundaries.

Every forgotten souvenir teaches you when your memory is most vulnerable. You’re not failing. You’re calibrating.

The only way to truly fail with the Wonder Compass is to stop trying entirely.

The Science: Dr. Carol Dweck’s research on “Growth Mindset” has shown that people who view failure as “information” rather than “judgment” are 34% more likely to persist in goal-directed behavior.

Every flop is a data point. Every awkward moment is a lesson. Every missed day is just a pause, not an ending.


That's the mindset shift, failure as information, not judgment. But mindset alone doesn't restart the Compass. You need a concrete protocol. Here's exactly what to do after a failure, step by step.




The Recovery Protocol: What to Do After a Failure

Here’s your step-by-step guide for getting back on the Compass after it breaks:

Step 1: Name It

Say out loud (or write down): “The Compass didn’t work today. That’s okay.” Don’t spiral. Don’t catastrophize. Just name it.

Step 2: Diagnose It

Which failure mode was it?

North wouldn’t ignite? (Try the Inverted Compass).

East wouldn’t launch? (Try the Atomic Embark).

South felt stupid? (Try a Stealth Mission).

West got forgotten? (Set a phone alarm for “Souvenir Time”).

The whole system collapsed? (Call someone).

Step 3: Shrink the Loop

Don’t try to “make up” for the failure with a big adventure. Just do one micro-loop. The bigger the failure, the smaller the reset.

Step 4: Write the Failure

This might sound strange, but write a souvenir about the failure.

“Today I tried to Notice and felt nothing, my brain is a gray fog… that’s where I am.”

Why? Because acknowledging the flop is a form of presence. You’re still paying attention. You’re still here.



The Long Game: Why Imperfect Beats Absent

Let me show you some math.

Let’s say you use the Compass perfectly for 30 days. Then you fall oﬀ for 60 days. Then you use it imperfectly (missing days, half-assing it) for 90 days.

Total Wonder Moments: ~40 distinct, captured experiences in 180 days.

Now let’s say you don’t use the Compass at all for those 180 days. You just keep scrolling. Total Wonder Moments: 0-2 (maybe you saw a sunset on accident).

The imperfect, inconsistent, failure-riddled use of the Compass still gave you 20x more
captured joy than doing nothing.

You don’t need to be perfect. You just need to be occasional.


The Research: A 2023 study in Emotion found that people who engaged in “intermittent positive interventions” (doing gratitude or awe exercises sporadically rather than daily) still showed significant improvements in well-being compared to controls.

Inconsistent practice beats no practice. Every single time.


That's the math. 40 imperfect wonder moments beats zero perfect ones. But knowing the math doesn't always help when the Caveman is screaming "You failed! Give up!"

The Compass Points to “Try Again”

There’s a reason the Compass is a circle and not a straight line. It’s designed to loop. To cycle. To return.

You don’t “complete” the Compass. You don’t “graduate” from it. You just keep spinning it, over and over, in imperfect circles, for the rest of your life.
