Chapter 11B:  Wonder with Disabilities 


There’s a specific kind of disappointment, even anger, that happens when you’re chronically ill and someone tells you to “go touch grass.”

You’re lying in bed because standing up would trigger a pain flare that’ll last three days. Or you’re managing your energy like a miser because you know that if you push too hard today, tomorrow you won’t be able to get out of bed at all. You’re reading a wellness article that says “Just take a walk in nature! It’s free! It’ll fix your anxiety!” And your jaw clenches because you know that person has no idea what it costs you to walk to the mailbox.

And then you open a book like this one. A book that promises “adventure” and “escape from the Rut.” You read the examples:

“Hike a trail you’ve never been to”

“Crouch down to look at the moss”

“Drive to the next town over”

“Walk around until something catches your eye”

And your body laughs, bitter. Because your body can’t do any of those things today. Maybe not this week. Maybe not this year. Maybe never again.

You feel that familiar sensation - the one where your throat tightens and your eyes sting. The one where you think: Of course. Another thing that isn’t built for me. Another space where I don’t belong.

The one that says: Oh. This book isn’t for me either.

You start to close it.

Pause. Hold your hands.

Don’t close it yet.

This chapter is for you. Not as an afterthought. Not as a “special accommodations” appendix. As acknowledgment that the Rut doesn’t care if your body works “correctly” - it will swallow you whole whether you can hike mountains or not - but the tools to escape it need to match the body you actually have.

And your body is different. So let’s rebuild the Compass for the constraints your body actually imposes.



The Reality:

Many examples in this book assume you can:

Walk to the mailbox

Hike trails

Crouch down to look at moss

Drive to new places

Stand for 10 minutes

But what if the world isn’t built for your body?

Chronic pain, mobility issues, fatigue conditions (ME/CFS, fibromyalgia, Long COVID), sensory processing disorders - these aren’t “excuses.” They’re real constraints that change what “adventure” looks like.

This 'Bed-Bound' protocol also applies if you’re a caregiver who can’t leave the room because someone you love needs you. You’re allowed to find wonder in the window while you wait.




The Adaptations:


For North (Notice):

If you can’t go to the world, bring the world to you. Window Sparks:

“I wonder how many different birds visit that tree today?”

“I wonder what the weather looks like from this angle at 3 PM vs. 6 PM?”

“I wonder if I can see the moon from my bed?” 

If you’re stuck in bed, your window is your best friend. It’s been standing there catching every bit of light it can find and throwing it at your floor like ‘LOOK, I GOT YOU SOMETHING.’ It doesn’t care if you notice. It’ll do it again tomorrow. And your bed? It isn't a prison; it’s a raft. It’s holding you up above the cold floor, keeping you safe while you weather the storm. Right now, without moving, notice where your body touches the bed. Your shoulder blades. Your heels. The back of your head. The bed is holding you with exactly the right amount of pressure. It’s not asking you to be productive. It’s not judging you for resting. It’s just there, faithful as gravity, doing its one job: keeping you off the cold floor. Let it.


Digital Sparks (yes, really):

“I wonder what the Faroe Islands look like on Google Street View?”

“I wonder what my childhood street looks like now on Google Maps?”


Free Digital Sparks:

Webcams: “I wonder what the Northern Lights look like right now?” (explore.org has live cams)

Internet Archive: “I wonder what my favorite website looked like in 2005?” (archive.org Wayback Machine)

Museum Virtual Tours: “I wonder what the Louvre looks like at night?” (many museums have free 360° tours)

Live Radio: “I wonder what a random radio station in Tokyo sounds like right now?” (radio.garden)

Open-world video games (Zelda: Breath of the Wild, Cyberpunk, GTA Vice City, Minecraft, Red Dead Redemption, Animal Crossing) are massive sources of Wonder.

The Housebound Digital Protocol:

North: “I wonder what Iceland looks like on Google Earth?”

East: Open Google Earth, put on Sigur Rós, set timer for 15 minutes.

Sense: Zoom into a random patch of moss. How many shades of green can you count? Can you see individual rocks?

West: “The moss on that hillside looks like broccoli from space, I’d forgotten Earth is weird and beautiful from above.”

If you’re thinking ‘But isn’t the whole point to get off screens?’ - no. The point is to pay attention. The screen is a window that refuses to stay in one place. It’ll show you Iceland if you ask nicely. Your laptop doesn’t care that you’re in bed - it’ll take you anywhere that has Wi-Fi and a camera. Your screen is just a rectangle of glass that desperately wants to show you things. It lights up the second you touch it, eager as a dog with a tennis ball: ‘WHERE DO YOU WANT TO GO? I CAN TAKE YOU THERE.‘ If you can’t go to Iceland, Google Earth is a valid way to visit. If you can’t hike a trail, Red Dead Redemption’s forests are real enough to trigger awe.

Your brain doesn’t know the difference between ‘real’ mountains and ‘digital’ mountains when it comes to the awe response. Studies below show that virtual nature can reduce stress and increase positive emotion. The portal is just as valid as the trail.

If it gets you curious, it counts.


For East (Embark):

Your “Destination” might be:

The other side of the room

The chair by the window instead of the couch

Sitting up in bed instead of lying down. Micro-Embarkment is still embarkment.

Delight for low-energy days:

A blanket that smells good. That soft blanket isn't just fabric; it’s a heavy, warm hand on your shoulder telling you it’s okay to rest. It’s fighting the draft. That cold air sneaking under the door? The blanket says ‘nope, not today’ and stands between you and it like a bouncer made of fleece. Let it do its job. Pull it up to your chin. That weight is the blanket being serious about its job. It’s not just lying there, it’s pressing down with intention, like it knows exactly how much pressure means ‘safe’ instead of ‘trapped.’ It’s been doing this for you every night, and it’s gotten really good at it.

A pillow that’s the right firmness.

A fan or heater that changes the temperature by 2 degrees. That fan in the corner? It’s been spinning the same direction for months, maybe years, pushing air around like it’s the most important job in the world. It hums while it works.

Definition:

“I’ll sit here until one song ends.”

“I’ll look out the window for 60 seconds.”


For South (Sense):

Playful Missions that don’t require movement:

The Texture Map: From where you’re sitting, identify 5 textures you can see (even if you can’t touch them).

The Sound Layer: Close your eyes. Count how many sounds you hear. Which is loudest? Which is most annoying?

The Light Watch: Watch one patch of sunlight move across the floor for 5 minutes. Where does it go?

The Memory Reconstruction: Close your eyes and rebuild your childhood kitchen in your mind. What color was the floor?

If the world is too loud, your Playful Mission isn't to take in more, but to find the quietest texture. Smooth stone. Soft blanket. Wonder can be a reduction of noise, not just an addition.


For West (Write):

If writing physically hurts:

Voice-to-text on your phone

Voice memo to yourself

Tell someone else and ask them to text it back to you The Souvenir doesn’t require handwriting. It requires creation and capture.





The Science: Why This Actually Works

Let’s address the obvious question: If you can’t leave your bed, if you’re looking at Google Earth instead of real earth, if you’re watching birds through a window instead of walking through a forest - is that real wonder? Or is it just a consolation prize?

The neuroscience has a clear answer: It’s real.

Here’s why.



The Prevalence (You’re Not Alone)

First, let’s establish scale. Chronic illness and disability aren’t edge cases.

According to the CDC:

	∙	50 million Americans live with chronic pain

	∙	15-24 million have ME/CFS (Myalgic Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome)

	∙	7.3 million have fibromyalgia

	∙	Estimates suggest 7-23 million Americans have Long COVID, many with debilitating fatigue

That’s before we count mobility disabilities, progressive conditions like MS or Parkinson’s, or the millions of people who are temporarily bedridden recovering from surgery or injury.

This isn’t a footnote. This is a massive chunk of the population.

And yet, almost every “adventure” or “self-improvement” book assumes you can walk, stand, drive, and move freely through the world.

That assumption isn’t just frustrating. It’s exclusionary. And it’s based on a fundamental misunderstanding of how wonder actually works in the brain.



The Virtual Nature Effect (Your Screen Isn’t Cheating)

Here’s what the research shows:


Study 1: Virtual Forests Reduce Stress

A 2020 study published in Frontiers in Psychology compared the effects of walking through a real forest versus viewing a virtual forest environment (using VR headsets).

The findings:

	∙	Both groups showed significant reductions in stress hormones (cortisol)

	∙	Both groups reported increased positive emotion

	∙	Both groups showed improved mood for hours afterward

The difference in effect size was negligible.

Your brain doesn’t have a “reality detector” that says “this mountain is fake, so I won’t give you the awe response.” Your visual cortex processes the image of a mountain the same way whether it’s in front of you or on a screen.



Study 2: Nature Photos Activate the Same Brain Regions

fMRI studies show that viewing photographs of natural landscapes activates the brain’s “awe network” - the same regions (anterior cingulate cortex, medial prefrontal cortex) that light up when people experience in-person awe.

In other words: The photo of the forest and the actual forest both trigger wonder in your brain.

The Implication:

If you can’t hike to Iceland, Google Earth is a valid portal. If you can’t walk to the park, the nature documentary is a legitimate source of awe. If you can’t crouch to look at moss, the macro photography video will do the same job neurologically.

Your screen isn’t a cop-out. It’s an accessibility device.

And anyone who tells you that “digital nature doesn’t count” is confusing their own preferences with neuroscience.



The Window as Portal (Controlled Access to the World)

There’s something profound about a window when you’re housebound.

The window isn’t a barrier. It’s a controlled portal.

Here’s why windows work neurologically:

	1.	Visual input without chaos: You get the benefits of nature exposure (trees, sky, weather, birds) without the sensory overwhelm of actually being outside (wind, temperature changes, noise, crowds, unpredictable stimuli)

	2.	Safety + Novelty: Your nervous system registers “I am safe inside” while simultaneously getting the novelty of “the world outside is changing”

	3.	Passive engagement: You don’t have to do anything. The window delivers content to you. The birds come to the tree. The weather changes. The light moves across the floor. You just have to look.

The window is doing the same job as a hike - delivering novel sensory input - but it’s doing it at a scale your body can handle.

And the research on nature exposure through windows backs this up:

Study: Hospital Recovery Rates

A famous 1984 study by Roger Ulrich found that hospital patients recovering from surgery who had a window view of trees:

	∙	Recovered faster

	∙	Needed less pain medication

	∙	Had fewer complications

	∙	Reported better mood

Compared to patients whose windows faced a brick wall.

Same hospital. Same surgery. The only difference was what they could see from bed.

The window isn’t a consolation prize. It’s a legitimate access point to the healing, awe-producing effects of the natural world.



The Energy Envelope (Why Pushing Through Is Dangerous)

If you have ME/CFS, fibromyalgia, Long COVID, or certain other chronic conditions, you’ve probably heard the term Post-Exertional Malaise (PEM).

PEM is what happens when you exceed your energy capacity: You feel fine for a few hours or a day, then you crash hard. The crash can last days, weeks, or even months. And each crash can make your baseline worse.

The research on energy management in chronic illness is clear:

“Pacing” - staying within your energy envelope and not exceeding it - is the only strategy that prevents long-term worsening.

“Pushing through” - trying to ignore your body’s limits - causes crashes that compound over time.

This has a direct implication for the Compass:

You are not allowed to use the Compass to override your body’s signals.

The Compass is not:

	∙	Permission to gaslight yourself (“I’m just being lazy, I need to push”)

	∙	A productivity tool in disguise (“If I just try harder, I can do a ‘real’ adventure”)

	∙	A way to prove you’re “trying hard enough”

If your body says rest, you rest. The Compass will be there tomorrow, or next week, or next month.

On good days, you use the Compass gently - within your energy envelope.

On bad days, you don’t use it at all. Or you use the absolute smallest version (look at the ceiling for 30 seconds, notice one shadow).

The goal isn’t to maximize wonder. It’s to stay in relationship with it without destroying yourself.





“I Can’t Leave My Bed Today”

Some days, the body just says no.

The Bed-Bound Compass:

North: “I wonder what my room looks like from this exact angle?”

East: Roll over to face a different direction. Delight = adjust the pillow so it’s comfortable. Definition = 2 minutes.

South: Look at the ceiling. Find a shadow or texture you’ve never noticed.

West: “That ceiling crack’s been pretending to be a tree branch this whole time, and I never noticed; it’s very committed to the bit.”

This isn’t failure. This is using the tool at the scale your body allows today.




The System: The Two-Tier Compass (Energy Envelope Management)

If you’re dealing with chronic illness or disability, you can’t use the Compass the same way every day. Your energy capacity fluctuates.

Some days you wake up with enough energy to sit by the window for 20 minutes. Other days, lifting your head off the pillow feels like climbing Everest.

This requires a different approach: The Two-Tier Compass.

This is based on Spoon Theory - a metaphor created by Christine Miserandino to explain chronic illness energy management.


Spoon Theory in 30 seconds:

Every morning, you wake up with a limited number of “spoons” (units of energy). Every activity costs spoons:

	∙	Getting out of bed: 2 spoons

	∙	Showering: 5 spoons

	∙	Making breakfast: 3 spoons

	∙	Having a conversation: 4 spoons

Healthy people wake up with 50+ spoons and regenerate them throughout the day. Chronically ill people might wake up with 12 spoons and can’t regenerate them. Once you’re out, you’re out.

The Two-Tier Compass adapts to your spoon count.



Tier 1: High-Energy Days (5+ Spoons Available)

On days when you have more capacity, you can do a modified version of the full Compass:

North: Window Sparks or Digital Sparks

“I wonder what the birds are doing today?”

“I wonder what Iceland looks like on Google Earth?”


East: Micro-movement

	∙	Roll over to face the window instead of the wall

	∙	Sit up in bed for a few minutes

	∙	Move to a chair if possible

Delight: Something you already have (favorite song, soft blanket, cold water)

Definition: “Until I feel tired” (NOT a time limit - your body decides when it’s done)


South: Single-sense focus

	∙	Count colors in the tree outside

	∙	Listen to one bird call

	∙	Feel the texture of your blanket



West: Voice memo or one sentence in Notes app

“The light made the dust look like snow falling sideways.”




Tier 2: Low-Energy Days (1-3 Spoons)

On days when you can barely function, the Compass shrinks to the absolute minimum:

North: “I wonder what I can see from exactly where I am right now?”




East: Don’t move. You’re already “there.” The destination is: this exact spot.

Delight: The pillow under your head. The temperature of the air. The weight of the blanket.

Definition: 30 seconds to 2 minutes



South: Find one thing.

	∙	A shadow on the ceiling

	∙	The sound of the fan

	∙	The feeling of your own breath




West: Don’t write. Just think it. Or whisper it out loud.

“The ceiling has a crack that looks like a river.”

That’s enough. That’s the whole adventure.





The Non-Negotiable Rule

Never use the Compass to gaslight yourself.

If your internal monologue sounds like:

	∙	“I should be able to do more than this”

	∙	“Other people can do real adventures, why can’t I?”

	∙	“I’m just being lazy, I need to push through”

Stop. You’re not using the Compass. You’re using internalized ableism.

The Compass is a tool for presence, not productivity. It’s not here to prove you’re “trying hard enough.”

If your body says stop, the Compass says “okay.”

You’re not failing. You’re adapting. And adaptation is intelligence, not weakness.




The Good Day Protocol (Don’t Overdo It)

Here’s a trap chronically ill people fall into:

You have a good day. You wake up with more energy than usual. You think: Finally! I can do things! I need to make up for all the bad days!

So you overextend. You use 15 spoons when you only had 12. And the next day, you crash hard.

This is called “boom and bust” and it makes everything worse over time.

The Compass has a built-in safeguard against this:

On good days, use the Compass gently.

Don’t try to “maximize” the adventure. Don’t try to “make up for” the bad days. Just do a slightly bigger version than you would on a medium day.

Example:

Bad day: Look at the ceiling for 30 seconds

Medium day: Look out the window for 5 minutes

Good day: Sit by the window for 10 minutes and then stop

Not: Sit by the window for 2 hours because “I finally can!”

The goal is consistency over time, not peak performance on good days.

Slow and steady keeps you in the game. Boom and bust takes you out.





The Stakes: What’s Lost If You Believe the Lie

Let’s be honest about what’s at risk here.

When your body limits your movement, it’s very easy to believe that wonder isn’t for you anymore. That adventure is for healthy people. That your life is just about managing symptoms and surviving, and you shouldn’t expect joy.

If you believe that, the Rut wins. And the Rut’s job becomes incredibly easy.

Because here’s what happens: You stop looking out the window because “what’s the point?” You stop noticing the light on the wall because “it’s not a real adventure.” You stop seeking any kind of beauty because you’ve accepted that beauty isn’t for you anymore.

That’s the actual tragedy. Not that your body is limited. But that those limitations convinced you to stop being curious about the world.

Your bed isn’t a prison. The belief that “being in bed means I’m not allowed to notice things” - that’s the prison.

The window isn’t a reminder of what you’re missing. It’s showing you what’s still here.

The crack in the ceiling isn’t “settling for less.” It’s a valid object of wonder if you choose to see it that way.

The neuroscience proves this: The awe response doesn’t care about your elevation or your mobility. It only cares about novelty and attention. A mountain at 10,000 feet and a shadow on your ceiling both produce the same neurological response - if you look closely enough.

So here’s the bottom line:

Curiosity doesn’t require a body that works “correctly.” It requires eyes (or ears, or touch, or any sense that still works), attention, and the choice to look.

You don’t need to earn the right to notice the sky by being able to walk to it. You don’t need to prove you’re “healthy enough” to deserve beauty.

You’re allowed to wonder from exactly where you are.

You don’t need a different body. You just need to look.​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​ Or hear. Or taste. Or touch.

Chapter 11B Summary: Wonder with Disabilities


What We Learned:

Adventure is Accessibility: Wonder isn't reserved for those who can hike mountains. If your body restricts your movement, you don't shrink your curiosity; you change your lens.

The Digital Portal: For the housebound, screens aren't "The Drift", they’re accessibility tools. Google Earth, open-world video games, and live webcams trigger real neural awe responses. You can travel without moving.

Window Sparks: Your window isn't a static image; it's a dynamic feed of the world. Watching birds, weather patterns, or the moon from bed counts as a valid expedition.

Micro-Embarkment: "Going somewhere" doesn't mean leaving the house. It can mean moving from the bed to the chair, or simply sitting up. The victory is in the intentional shift of state, not the distance traveled.

The Energy Rule: Never use the Compass to gaslight yourself into pushing through a crash or flare-up. If your body says stop, resting is the most respectful way to use the tool.


Quick Win:

The Texture Audit.

Don’t stand up. Don’t move your body. Right where you’re sitting or lying down, close your eyes.

Identify three different textures currently touching your skin.

The texture of the fabric underneath you (sheets, couch, chair).

The texture of your own clothing against your skin.

The temperature of the air on your face.

Focus on one of them for 30 seconds. That sensation is happening right now. You don't need to go anywhere to feel it. You’re already engaging with the physical world.
