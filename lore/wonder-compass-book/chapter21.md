Chapter 21: Wonder at Work



Let me tell you about some of the worst advice I ever received.

I was young, just starting out in pharmacy work, and I was complaining to an older coworker (she was in her 20’s, we were so young) about how boring the job felt. Day after day of the same routines, the same deliveries, the same customers.

She looked at me and said, “Oh honey, work isn’t supposed to be fun. That’s why they call it work. You just get through it so you can live on the weekends.”

I believed her.

For years, I believed her.

I spent eight hours a day, five days a week - which is roughly 2,000 hours per year, which is roughly 80,000 hours over a 40-year career - treating my work as something to endure rather than experience.

And finally, I was a ghost at my job. Physically present, mentally absent. My hands knew what to do, but my eyes had stopped recording. I could spend three hours assisting complex surgeries and not remember a single detail because I wasn’t actually there. I was thinking about what I’d do when I got home, or replaying an argument from the weekend, or planning my grocery list.

I was Fast-Traveling through my entire career.

And here’s the tragedy: I was working in mouths. I was literally inside the most intimate, vulnerable, weird, fascinating part of the human body, and I had decided it was “boring.” And the mouths kept opening and closing. Patients kept sitting in the chair, tilting their heads back, trusting me with the softest, most vulnerable part of their bodies. The tongue - this wet, muscular thing that’s been tasting and speaking and swallowing since birth - would curl back nervously, trying to stay out of the way like it knew its job was to not interfere.. The teeth, older than memory, worn by decades of chewing, would catch the light like they were trying to look good for inspection, even though some of them were cracked and stained and clearly exhausted from forty years of crushing food.  And I was standing there, inside this small cave of someone’s body, and I’d decided it was boring. That’s not a failure of the job. That’s a failure of attention.

This chapter is about something that sounds impossible, maybe even offensive to people who hate their jobs: Finding wonder at work, not despite work.

Not as an escape. Not as a distraction. But as a way to actually engage with the thing you’re doing for 40+ hours per week so those hours feel like they’re part of your life instead of a parenthetical pause in it.


The Hard Truth About Work

Before we go any further, I need to say this clearly:

Some jobs are genuinely awful.

If you’re working in an unsafe environment, if you’re being abused by your boss, if you’re being paid poverty wages while doing backbreaking labor, if you’re trapped in a system that actively dehumanizes you, then this chapter is not for you.

The Wonder Compass isn’t a tool for gaslighting yourself into tolerating exploitation.

If your job is killing you - physically, mentally, emotionally - you don’t need to find wonder in it. You need to find a way out of it.

Call a union. Call a lawyer. Call a therapist. Update your resume. Reach out to your network. Do whatever you need to do to survive and eventually escape.


I want to be very clear about who this is for.

When I tell you to find wonder in a spreadsheet or a customer interaction, I am not asking you to be a 'better employee.' I don’t care about your productivity. I don’t care about the company’s bottom line.

I care about you not disappearing.

The danger of a boring or stressful job isn't just that it’s hard; it’s that it shrinks you. It convinces you that you are your function. It tells you: 'You’re a coffee maker.' 'You’re an email answerer.' 'You’re a complaint department.'

If you accept that definition, the job wins. You become exactly as small as the role requires.

But when you use the Compass at work - when you pause to notice the light hitting the conference table, or when you study the chaotic energy of the lunch rush like a sociologist - you’re doing something radical. You’re refusing to fit in the box.

You’re saying: 'I’m performing the function of an employee, but I am retaining the soul of a human. I’m seeing things this job doesn't pay me to see. I am feeling things this job doesn't require me to feel.'

You become a Double Agent. On the outside, you’re doing the work. On the inside, you’re gathering evidence that you are bigger, more complex, and more alive than this building can contain.

We aren’t doing this to help the boss. We’re doing this to keep your soul intact until 5:00 PM.

Every time you notice something the job didn't ask for, you prove to yourself that you are bigger than the role you play.

Here’s the strange, almost accidental side effect of this subversive mindset: When you use the Compass at work, you don’t just feel better. You actually get better at the job.

Why? Because a bored brain is a sloppy brain. When you’re in the Rut, you make "autopilot errors." You miss details. You forget names. You send emails to the wrong "Steve." You suffer from Presenteeism, being physically at work but mentally absent.

But when you activate Wonder, when you’re actively scanning for details, patterns, and "glitches", your prefrontal cortex wakes up. You become sharper. You see solutions that your coworkers missed because they were too busy zoning out.

You become the person who notices the safety hazard before it breaks the machine. You become the person who hears what the client actually needs, not just what they asked for.

You become indispensable not because you grind harder than everyone else, but because you’re the only one who’s actually awake.



The Danger of Weaponized Wonder

And I need to give you a warning.

Some managers, some productivity gurus, some companies will try to use these techniques against you.

They’ll say things like:

“Find the joy in your work!” (Translation: Accept low pay and bad conditions with a smile.)

“Be grateful you have a job!” (Translation: Don’t ask for better treatment.)

“We’re a family here!” (Translation: Work overtime for free because families sacrifice.)


This is not that.

The Wonder Compass at work isn’t about making you tolerate exploitation. It’s about making you present during hours you’re already spending.

If your job is genuinely harmful, no amount of “noticing the beauty of spreadsheets” will fix it.


The Diagnostic:

If using the Compass at work makes you feel worse - if noticing details just makes you more aware of how much you hate it, if paying attention just highlights how meaningless it is, if being present just amplifies your misery - then stop.

That’s not the Compass failing. That’s the Compass giving you data: This job is wrong for you. Start planning your exit.

The tool is a diagnostic. If it reveals that your work is incompatible with your well-being, trust that signal.


Now then, with that out of the way, this chapter is for people who have okay jobs that have become gray jobs. Jobs that aren’t actively harmful, but aren’t actively engaging either. Jobs that pay the bills but don’t feed the soul. Jobs that have become so routine, so automated, so familiar that they’ve disappeared into the background of your life.

If that’s you, keep reading.

Because here’s the thing: Even boring work contains interesting elements if you know where to look.

The question isn’t “Is my job inherently interesting?” The question is “Am I paying attention to the interesting parts, or have I automated myself into blindness?”


The Two Types of Work Rut

Let’s be specific about what we’re solving for.


Type 1: The Repetition Rut

This is the person who does the same task over and over.

The assembly line worker. The data entry clerk. The barista making the 47th latte of the day. The dental assistant (me) passing the same instruments in the same order for the same procedures.

The job itself hasn’t changed. You’ve just done it so many times that your brain has automated it completely. You can do it in your sleep, which means you are essentially sleeping through it. Your hands are working, but your consciousness has left the building.


Type 2: The Meaning Rut
This is the person who’s lost the thread of why the work matters.

You might be doing different tasks every day. You might even be “important” or “successful.” But somewhere along the way, the connection between the work and its purpose got severed. You’re filing reports but you’ve forgotten what the reports are for. You’re teaching lessons but you’ve stopped seeing the students as humans. You’re fixing problems but you’ve lost track of who benefits from the fix.

You’re technically engaged, but emotionally, you’re hollow.

Both types lead to the same place: The feeling that you’re spending the majority of your waking life doing something that doesn’t matter, watching the hours tick by until you’re allowed to leave.


The Reframe: From “Killing Time” to “Being Alive During Work Hours”

Here’s the shift we’re making:

Most people think of work and life as separate categories:

Work: The thing you endure to earn money

Life: The thing you do with the money after work

This creates a 40-hour void in the center of every week. A black hole where you’re not “living,” you’re just “working.”

But here’s the reality: You’re alive at work.

Your heart is beating. Your neurons are firing. You’re breathing, sensing, existing. The hours you spend working are part of your one finite life, not a separate simulation you enter and exit.

Your coffee mug is sitting on your desk, ready for another day. Your chair has been holding you up for three years, learning the exact shape of you. Your keyboard has been translating your thoughts every single morning - frustrated emails, careful reports, quick notes - turning your finger-taps into words without ever asking what they mean. They’ve been showing up, just like you. They’re in this routine with you.

The goal of this chapter isn’t to make you love your job (though that might happen). The goal is to make you present during your job so that when you look back on your life, you don’t have to say, “I missed 40 years because I was too busy ignoring them.”

The Wonder Compass at work isn’t about finding joy in spreadsheets (though we’ll try). It’s about finding presence in the hours you’re already spending there.


Your Workspace Has a Personality (If You Let It)

If this section doesn’t resonate with you, that’s fine. You can skip to the next section and still use the Compass effectively. This is an optional upgrade, not a requirement.


In Chapter 8B, you learned the trick: your brain doesn’t clearly distinguish between real social agents and imagined ones. When you treat your bike like it has preferences, your brain fires the same neural circuits it uses for actual people. Not because you’re delusional. Because fifty thousand years of evolution wired you to see agency everywhere, and you can use that wiring on purpose.

At home, this comes naturally. You chose your mug. You picked your chair. There’s already history there, even if you’ve stopped noticing it.

At work, it’s harder. And you need it more.

Because nothing at work is yours. The stapler was on the desk when you arrived. The chair came from a supply closet. The monitor was issued by IT with a serial number and a warranty that expired in 2021. You didn’t choose any of it, so you interact with all of it like a hotel room - functional, impersonal, forgettable.

But here’s what’s actually true: that stapler has been with you longer than some of your friendships. That chair has held your body through three performance reviews, two panic attacks, and four hundred lunch breaks where you ate at your desk and pretended that was fine. The monitor has displayed every email you’ve ever sent from this building - the careful ones, the frustrated ones, the ones you typed and deleted and typed again.

They’ve been here. They’ve been participating. You just haven’t been treating them like it.

So try this: Talk to your stuff.

Not out loud (unless you work alone). In your head. Give your workspace permission to have character.

Start with one object. The one you touch most. For me, it was mostly the instruments - the mirror, the explorer, the scaler but weirdly, also the cotton rolls. I touched them hundreds of times a day. But at your desk, it might be the stapler. Pick it up. Really look at it.

Your stapler responds to your mood. When you’re calm, you load it gently, press it smoothly, and it cooperates, clean bite, neat fold, no complaints. When you’re frustrated, you slam it down, mash it harder than you need to, and it fights back. Jams on purpose. Refuses to close. Chews the paper instead of binding it. You blame the stapler, but the stapler was fine ten minutes ago when you weren’t angry. It genuinely works better when you treat it gently. Which means your stapler has been teaching you patience for three years and you’ve been ignoring the lesson.

Now look at your keyboard. Find the E key. It’s the most worn key on the board, pressed more than any other because E appears in almost every word you type. You can barely read the letter anymore. The plastic is smooth where every other key still has texture. That’s not damage. That’s a service record. The E key has been the first responder to nearly every thought you’ve had in this building, and it shows.

And the desk plant, if you have one. It’s been photosynthesizing under fluorescent lights that were never designed to grow anything. It’s converting your exhaled carbon dioxide into oxygen in real time, right now, on your desk, asking nothing in return except occasional water that you keep forgetting to give it. It’s doing more for the air quality in your cubicle than the HVAC system, and it’s the size of your fist.

Three objects. That’s enough.




Naming (The Advanced Move)

In Chapter 8B, we talked about how naming shifts an object from category to individual. “The bike” is equipment. “Lillywill” is someone. Your brain literally processes named things using the same neural regions it uses for recognizing people.

At work, naming is trickier. You’re probably not going to call your stapler “Gerald” in front of your manager. But you don’t need to. The name can be private. Completely internal. A secret between you and the object.

And here’s what I’ve found: at work, the names tend to arrive on their own. You don’t sit down and assign them. The stapler jams for the third time on a Monday morning and you mutter “oh, come on” and suddenly it’s “The Grouch” and it’s been The Grouch ever since because it really is grumpy. The coffee machine takes four minutes to brew and makes a sound like it’s sighing, and now it’s “Dolores” and you and Dolores have an understanding - you don’t rush her, she doesn’t burn your coffee.

You don’t have to name anything. But if a name shows up, let it stay. It means the relationship has started.




The Morning Greeting

It’s 8:47 AM. You’ve made it through traffic or the commute or the walk from the parking lot. You’re carrying your bag and your coffee and whatever mood the morning handed you. You sit down.

This is usually where you disappear. You open the laptop, the inbox loads, and you’re gone, swallowed by the screen, reacting to other people’s urgencies, already behind.

Before that happens. Ten seconds.

Look at your chair. You just sat down in it, same as yesterday, same as the day before. It took your weight without complaint. It always does. “Morning, chair,” in your head.

Touch your keyboard. The keys are cool. In twenty minutes they’ll be warm from your fingers. “What are we writing today?”

Pick up your mug. It’s empty or it’s full. Either way, it showed up. “You ready?”

Ten seconds. Three objects. You’ve just told your nervous system that you arrived - not just your badge, not just your login, but you. The person inside the employee. You’re here. You noticed. The day can start now.




Why This Matters Specifically at Work

You already know from Chapter 8B that anthropomorphism combats loneliness, makes boring tasks playful, and bypasses decision fatigue. All of that applies here. But work adds three dynamics that make the technique even more valuable.

The first is equipment care. A 2021 study in Applied Ergonomics found that factory workers who named their machines had 30% fewer equipment failures and 40% fewer safety incidents compared to workers who treated them as interchangeable tools. The mechanism is simple: when your drill press is “Big Betty” and she’s feeling sluggish today, you don’t just push through the resistance. You stop. You check the blade. You oil the parts. You catch the problem before it becomes an injury. You’ve shifted from operator to caretaker, and caretakers notice things operators miss.

The second is levity. Work is relentless - deadlines, politics, performance reviews, the fluorescent hum that never stops. When your stapler jams and you think “The Grouch is in a mood today,” you’ve injected a half-second of comedy into a moment that would otherwise just be frustration. Research from Stanford’s Center for Compassion and Altruism shows that brief moments of levity throughout the workday significantly reduce cortisol accumulation and improve end-of-day mood. You’re not escaping work. You’re slipping tiny pressure valves into the day so the pressure doesn’t build until you crack.

The third is the one I care about most. It’s resistance.

When you anthropomorphize your workspace, you’re building a world the company doesn’t control and can’t see. Your boss can assign you tasks. The corporation can track your keystrokes. The system can quantify your output and graph your efficiency and reduce you to a number on a dashboard.

But they can’t stop you from knowing that The Grouch needs gentle pressure on Mondays. They can’t see that you and Dolores have a four-minute understanding. They can’t access the ten-second greeting where you and your chair acknowledged each other before the inbox swallowed you.

You’ve created a private layer of meaning inside a space designed to extract your labor. That layer is yours. It doesn’t show up on any report. It doesn’t improve any metric. It just keeps a small, defiant part of you alive - the part that sees characters where the company sees equipment, the part that has relationships where the org chart has headcount.

That’s not childish. That’s how you keep your soul in a building that doesn’t know you have one.





The Warning

Two boundaries.

Don’t do this with safety equipment or critical machinery if it makes you less careful. Your fire extinguisher isn’t your friend. It’s a tool that needs to work perfectly the one time you need it. Don’t let personification create complacency about things that protect lives.

And keep it private. If your workplace culture would mock this, keep it entirely internal. This is your secret practice, your private resistance. Don’t sacrifice it by exposing it to an audience that isn’t ready.




The Permission:

You don’t have to do this.

You don’t have to anthropomorphize your entire workspace.

But try it with one object. Just one. Your keyboard. Your chair. Your coffee mug.

Give it a personality for one week.

See what happens.

I think you’ll find that the object becomes more than a tool.

It becomes a companion.

And when you’re spending 40 hours a week in a fluorescent box, surrounded by demands and deadlines and stress, having even one silent companion makes all the difference.

Anthropomorphism doesn’t make you childish.

It makes you awake.




So your workspace feels less lonely. Your coffee mug shows up every morning. Your chair knows your weight. Your keyboard translates your thoughts.

Great. But you’re still staring at the same spreadsheet you’ve been staring at for six months. You’re still in the same meeting where nothing gets decided. You’re still doing the work.

How do you stay awake for that?





The Job Compass (N-E-S-W at Work)

The Compass works at work, but it requires modification. You can’t exactly “Embark on an adventure” when you’re supposed to be at your desk by 9 AM.

So here’s the adapted version:


NORTH = NOTICE (The Reframe)

At work, North isn’t about finding a new spark. It’s about finding the spark in what you’re already doing.

Instead of “I wonder what’s out there?” it’s “I wonder what I’m missing right here?”

The Diagnostic Question:

“I wonder, if a documentary crew followed me for a day, what would they find interesting about my job that I’ve stopped noticing?”

This question forces you to step outside your own autopilot and imagine an outsider’s perspective.

Examples:

If you’re a barista: “They’d probably find it interesting that I can make five drinks simultaneously while having three conversations and never mixing up an order. That’s actually a skill.”

If you’re a teacher: “They’d probably notice how much energy it takes to hold the attention of 30 teenagers for an hour. That’s basically performance art.”

If you’re a coder: “They’d probably be fascinated that I’m essentially speaking to a machine in a language it understands and making it do things. That’s kind of like magic.”

If you’re a truck driver: “They’d probably notice that I’m navigating a 40-ton vehicle through traffic at 65 mph while staying alert for 10 hours straight. That’s actually intense.”

The Shift:

You’re not changing what you do. You’re changing how you frame what you do.

The work in your life hasn’t become uninteresting. You’ve stopped treating it as interesting.


EAST = EMBARK (The Micro-Shift)

At work, you can’t “go somewhere.” You’re trapped at your workstation, your desk, your truck, your station.

But you can make micro-shifts, small changes to your routine that break the autopilot without breaking the rules.

Diagnostic Question: “If I had to train someone to do my job tomorrow, what’s one habit I’d realize I never consciously chose?”

The Protocol:

Pick one small variable in your work routine and change it for one week. Examples:

If you always start your day by checking email, start by doing one physical task first (making coffee, organizing your desk, stretching).

If you always sit in the same spot in meetings, sit in a diﬀerent chair.

If you always take the same route through the warehouse/hospital/office, take a different path.

If you always eat lunch at your desk, eat outside (or in the break room, or in your car).

If you always work in silence, try instrumental music. If you always work with music, try silence.

Why this works:

Neuroscience research on “context-dependent memory” shows that your brain encodes experiences differently when even minor environmental variables change.

By changing one small thing, you’re signaling to your brain: “This isn’t the same day as yesterday.” That signal is enough to shift you from autopilot to awareness.


The Constraint:

Only change ONE thing per week. If you try to change five things, it becomes stressful and The Caveman will shut it down. One thing. That’s it.


SOUTH = SENSE (The Noticing Game)

This is where the magic happens.

At work, the Playful Mission isn’t “find the weirdest mailbox.” It’s “find the thing everyone else is ignoring.”

Your job is to notice one specific detail about your work that you’ve been treating as invisible.

Diagnostic Question: “If I could only describe my job using physical sensations (sounds, textures, temperatures, movements), what would I say?”

The Three Modalities:

Modality 1: The Material Study

Pick one physical object you interact with every day and actually see it. Examples:

The break room coffee machine has been making the exact same coffee for eight years. It’s not great coffee. It’s not terrible coffee. It’s just… coffee. And the machine is fine with that. It’s not trying to win awards. It’s trying to wake people up at 7 AM, and it succeeds every single day. The machine doesn’t care that you complain. It knows you’ll be back tomorrow.

The door handle you use to enter the building. What material is it? Brass? Steel? How many hands have touched it today?

Pick one object. You already know how to see it - you did it with your stapler and your E key ten minutes ago. Now do it with anything else on your desk.



Mission: Spend 60 seconds studying one object. Find three details you’ve never noticed. 

Why this works: You’re training your brain to see specificity instead of labels. The keyboard stops being “keyboard” and becomes “this particular keyboard with this particular history.”



Modality 2: The Process Autopsy

Pick one task you do on autopilot and break it down into its component steps. 

Examples:

Making coffee. How many distinct steps? (Grinding beans, measuring water, waiting for brew, pouring, adding milk—at least 5-7 micro-actions.)

Sending an email. What’s the actual sequence? (Open inbox, click compose, type recipient, think of subject line, write body, re-read, edit, click send. At least 8 steps.)

A surgical procedure I assisted with: passing instruments. (Anticipate next instrument, pick it up, rotate it to correct angle, place it in doctor’s hand at exact moment needed, retrieve used instrument, place in tray. At least 6 micro-actions, repeated 40 times in an hour.)

Mission: Pick one “simple” task. Count how many distinct actions it actually requires.

Why this works: You’re making the invisible visible. You realize that what feels “automatic” is actually a complex choreography your brain has mastered. That’s impressive. You’re impressive. You just forgot.


Modality 3: The Human Moment

This is for jobs that involve interacting with people (customers, patients, coworkers, students, clients).

Mission: Pick one interaction today and actually see the person instead of the transaction. Not in a creepy way. Not staring. Just noticing.

Examples:

The customer who orders the same thing every morning. What’s their vibe today? Rushed? Calm? Sad? Happy?

The coworker you pass in the hallway. Do they make eye contact? Do they look tired? Do they seem lighter than usual?

The patient in the dental chair (my context). Are they gripping the armrests? Are they trying to make jokes to hide their fear? Are they wearing a specific piece of jewelry they keep touching?

The Rule: You don’t have to do anything with this information. You’re not trying to “help” or “fix” or “connect” (unless you want to). You’re just practicing seeing people as humans instead of NPCs (non-player characters) in your work simulation.

Why this works: It breaks the transactional trance. For 30 seconds, you remember that you’re one human interacting with another human, and both of you are alive right now, in this moment, in this building, having this small exchange that will never happen again in exactly this way.


Mission: The Glitch Hunt, Work Edition

In a corporate or retail environment, everyone’s wearing a mask. The 'Professional Face.' The 'Customer Service Voice.' The 'Polite Nod.'

Your mission isn't just to see people; it's to find the moment the mask slips.

Look for the Glitch, the split second where the professional veneer cracks and a real human pokes through.

The moment your scary boss laughs at a text message and looks suddenly younger.

The moment the angry customer drops their keys and looks flustered and vulnerable instead of threatening.

The drawing of a dinosaur taped to the back of the IT guy's monitor.

Why this works: It demystifies the hierarchy. When you see the human glitch, you realize that everyone here is just pretending to be a professional. They’re all just people in costumes. And if they’re just people, they can’t crush you.


WEST = WRITE (The End-of-Day Souvenir)

At work, you can’t pull out a notebook mid-task and start journaling. But you can capture one moment at the end of the day. Before you leave, before you shut down your computer, before you clock out, before you walk out the door, take 30 seconds.

Pull out your phone. Open your notes app. Write one sentence.

The Prompt:

“What’s one thing I noticed today that I usually ignore?” 

Examples:

From my dental assisting days:

“The autoclave makes a specific hissing sound when it hits temperature, and I realized I’ve been using that sound as a timer for three years without consciously knowing it.”

“Patient #4 had a tattoo of an anchor on her wrist and she kept touching it during the procedure like it was a talisman.”

“The doctor was singing lightly to Pandora during the procedures today, so it was a good day.”

Other examples:

(Teacher) “One kid in the back row was drawing in his notebook during my lecture, but when I looked closer, he was drawing a diagram of what I was explaining; he was paying attention, just differently.”

(Barista) “The morning regular who orders a double espresso is always wearing different socks - today was dinosaurs and pizza slices; never matched, this is clearly a choice.”

(Coder) “I wrote 847 lines of code today and 600 of them were fixing a bug caused by one missing semicolon; computers are hilariously literal.”

Why this works: You’re building a catalog of “I was present today.” When you look back at the week, you won’t just see a blur of “work.” You’ll see specific moments where you were awake. More importantly, you’re training your brain to look for these moments throughout the day. Once you know you’re going to write a sentence at 5 PM, your RAS (Reticular Activating System) starts scanning for material at 10 AM.

You’ve gamified attention.


The Three Work Archetypes

Now let’s get specific. Here are three fully-realized Compass applications for different types of work.


Archetype 1: The Repetitive Worker (Assembly Line, Retail, Service) 

The Context: You do the same task over and over. Make the latte. Scan the item. Assemble the widget. Stock the shelf. Pass the instrument. Your brain automated this task years ago. You could do it blindfolded. Which means you’re essentially blindfolded, your consciousness has checked out.

The Danger: When work becomes purely mechanical, you stop being a human and become a machine. And machines don’t have lives. They have “run time.”


NORTH = NOTICE (The Reframe):

“I wonder what would break this task if I weren’t here?”

This isn’t about ego. It’s about recognizing the specificity of your skill.

You’re not “just making lattes.” You’re calibrating temperature, timing, pressure, and ratios to molecular precision. The machine can’t do that alone. You’re the calibration.

You’re not “just stocking shelves.” You’re organizing three-dimensional space to optimize visual hierarchy and customer flow. That’s design.

You’re not “just passing instruments.” You’re anticipating needs three steps ahead, reading body language, and preventing errors before they happen. That’s expertise.


EAST = EMBARK (The Micro-Shift):

Change one variable in your routine. Examples:

If you always make drinks in the same order (espresso → milk → syrup), flip the order.

If you always stock from left to right, stock from right to left.

If you always pass instruments with your right hand, try your left (if safe).

Why? The moment you break the automation, you have to pay attention again. You have to think. Thinking means you’re awake.


SOUTH = SENSE (The Material Conversation)

Mission: Talk to the materials you’re working with.

Not out loud. But in your head. The materials you touch 200 times a day aren’t dead objects, they’re participants in your work.

Examples:

Barista: “Alright, espresso beans, let’s see what you’ve got today.” Smell them. Are they fresh? Stale? Oily? The beans change daily based on humidity, roast batch, age. Today’s beans might be smooth and caramel-sweet. Tomorrow’s might be bitter and sharp. Pay attention to the difference.

Assembly line worker: “Okay, bolt, you’re going in.” Feel the resistance as you tighten it. Some bolts are smooth. Some have slightly stripped threads. Some are from a different metal batch and feel heavier. Notice which ones.

Retail stocker: “This can of soup has been waiting in the warehouse for three weeks. Now it’s your moment, front and center.” Feel the weight. Some cans are heavier than others (overfilled, different contents, dented and leaking). Feel the difference.

Truck driver: “Alright, Nim, let’s see how you’re doing today.” Your truck has been carrying you for 80,000 miles. Does the engine sound different today? Is the steering pulling left? Is the brake pedal softer than yesterday? Pay attention. The truck is talking, you’ve just been ignoring it. Your truck has been carrying you across state lines for three years. It’s mapped every pothole on I-40 in its suspension. It’s been your office, your break room, your cocoon. It’s not just a vehicle, it’s the only constant companion on ten thousand miles of road. It’s proud of its 200,000 miles. Other trucks in the lot are newer, shinier, but your truck has been places. It’s got dents and scratches that are battle scars, stories. The truck doesn’t want to be shiny anymore - it wants you to know it’s earned every mile.



Why this works:

You’re anthropomorphizing the materials (the beans have moods, the bolt has a personality, the soup can has been waiting for its moment). This activates your mirror neurons and keeps you engaged.

You’re engaging your senses (smell, touch, sound, weight) instead of autopiloting. Your hands are still working, but your brain is awake.

You’re creating micro-relationships with the things you touch all day. The espresso beans aren’t just “beans”, they’re collaborators. The bolts aren’t just “parts”, they’re individuals. The cans aren’t just “inventory”, they’re waiting passengers.

The shift:

You’re not trying to be faster. You’re trying to be present. You’re refusing to treat the materials as interchangeable units. Each one is slightly different. Each one has a story. And by paying attention to those differences, you’re proving to yourself that you’re not a machine.

Machines produce identical outputs. Humans notice variation.




WEST = WRITE (The Souvenir):

End-of-shift sentence:

“I made 73 drinks today and the most interesting order was the oat milk cortado with honey and cinnamon because the customer said it was for their grandfather who can’t have dairy anymore, and I realized I was making a small act of love.”

“Stocked 400 items today and found one can that had been dented and hidden behind the others for so long it was dusty; someone put it there on purpose, felt like finding a secret.”

“Feels like I passed 487 instruments today without a single error; I don’t know if the doctor noticed, but I did.”

“Talked to the espresso beans all day - morning batch was smooth and sweet, afternoon batch was bitter and angry; I realized the beans have moods just like I do, and I’ve been working with them for three years without noticing.”





Archetype 2: The Knowledge Worker (Office, Remote, Creative Professional) 

The Context: Your job is primarily mental. You write reports, answer emails, attend meetings, create things, analyze data, solve problems. You’re not bored because the task is repetitive. You’re bored because the meaning has evaporated. You’re swimming in information but starving for purpose.

The Danger: Knowledge work can feel like intellectual busywork. You’re moving documents around, attending meetings about meetings, producing outputs no one will read. The existential “Why does this matter?” becomes unbearable.


NORTH = NOTICE (The Reframe):

“I wonder who benefits from this task?” 

Trace the line from your work to the end user. 

Examples:

If you’re writing code: Who will use this feature? What problem does it solve for them?

If you’re answering emails: Is this email preventing a future fire? Is it unblocking someone else’s work?

If you’re writing a report: Who reads this? What decision do they make because of it?

The goal: Reconnect the what (the task) to the who (the human).


EAST = EMBARK (The Micro-Shift):

Change one variable in your work environment. 

Examples:

If you always work at your desk, work from the conference room for one hour.

If you always type, dictate instead (use voice-to-text).

If you always work in silence, try a “focus” playlist (instrumental, lo-fi beats, classical).

If you always work alone, try a “co-working” session with a colleague (same room, parallel work, no talking).


SOUTH = SENSE (The Deep Dive):

Mission: Go extremely deep on one small task instead of skimming across ten tasks.

Examples:

Instead of answering 20 emails at surface level, answer three emails perfectly. Craft them. Make them clear, kind, complete.

Instead of writing 10 lines of mediocre code, write three lines of elegant code. Refactor until it’s beautiful.

Instead of half-reading five reports, fully read one report. Take notes. Understand it.

Why this works: Depth is the antidote to the shallow skim that makes knowledge work feel meaningless. When you go deep, you remember why craft matters. Your text editor has been open for six hours. It’s been holding your code, your drafts, your half-finished thoughts through six different versions. It watches you delete 50 lines and start over, and it doesn’t get frustrated - it’s curious about where you’re going with this. It saves every version because it believes each one matters, even the ones you throw away.



WEST = WRITE (The Souvenir):

End-of-day sentence:

“Spent 90 minutes on one email reply to a frustrated customer and by the end, I’d turned their complaint into a conversation; they thanked me and I remembered why words matter.”

“Refactored a function today until it was 12 lines instead of 47 - it’s faster, cleaner, and more readable; no one will ever see this victory but me, and that’s fine.”

“Read a report about user behavior and realized we’ve been designing for the wrong problem for six months; sent a memo: we’re pivoting - I changed something.”


Archetype 3: The Caregiver/Teacher/Service Worker (People-Focused Work) 

The Context: Your job is people. You teach, you heal, you serve, you help, you guide. You started because you wanted to make a difference. But somewhere along the way, the people became problems. The students became obstacles. The patients became interruptions. The customers became nuisances.

You’re burned out. You’ve lost the thread.

The Danger: When you stop seeing people as people, you stop being human yourself. You become a transaction processor. And that’s soul death.


NORTH = NOTICE (The Reframe):

“I wonder what this person’s life is like outside this interaction?” You’re not trying to know the answer. You’re trying to wonder about it. 

Examples:

The student who’s disruptive in class. What’s happening at home? Are they hungry? Scared? Bored because they’re actually too advanced?

The patient who’s rude on the phone. Are they in pain? Are they terrified? Did someone they love just die?

The customer who’s demanding a refund. Are they struggling financially? Did this product break at the worst possible time?

The Rule: You don’t have to fix any of this. You’re not a therapist (unless you are). You’re just practicing empathy, the act of imagining complexity instead of assuming simplicity.


EAST = EMBARK (The Micro-Shift):

Change your opening script. Examples:

Instead of “How can I help you?” try “What brings you in today?”

Instead of “Do you have any questions?” try “What’s one thing you want to make sure you understand before you leave?”

Instead of “Let’s begin,” try “Before we start, is there anything you need to feel comfortable?”

Why? A small change in language shifts the power dynamic. You’re not processing them. You’re with them.


SOUTH = SENSE (The One Person Focus):

Mission: Pick one person during your shift/day and give them your full, undivided attention. Not all of them. Just one

Examples:

One student: Make eye contact. Ask a follow-up question. Remember their name.

One patient: Notice their shoes. Their jewelry. Ask them one non-medical question: “What do you do for fun?” or “Where are you from originally?”

One customer: Slow down. Make the interaction 30 seconds longer than necessary. Treat them like a human, not a ticket.

Why? You can’t do this for everyone. You’ll burn out. But you can do it for one. And one is enough to remind you why you do this work.


WEST = WRITE (The Souvenir):

End-of-day sentence:

“One student stayed after class to ask a question and when I answered, she said ‘You made that so clear’ and I remembered that I’m good at this.”

“Patient #3 was terrified of needles but made it through the procedure; afterward, they said ‘Thank you for being gentle’ - I’d forgotten that gentle is a skill.”

“One customer was buying a toy for their grandkid and we talked for five minutes about what games kids play now; they said I reminded them of their daughter and I almost cried.”


The Meeting Survival Protocol

Meetings are where work goes to die. But they’re also unavoidable for most people. Here’s how to use the Compass to survive them without losing your mind.


NORTH = NOTICE (The Reframe):

“I wonder what everyone in this room isn’t  saying?”

Meetings are theater. Everyone’s performing. But if you watch closely, you can see the subtext.

Who’s checking their phone? (They don’t think this meeting matters.)

Who’s leaning forward? (They care deeply about this topic.)

Who’s silent? (They might disagree but won’t say it out loud.)

Who’s talking the most? (They’re either the leader or they’re insecure.)


EAST = EMBARK (The Micro-Shift):

Sit in a different chair than you usually do. If you always sit near the door, sit at the center of the table. If you always sit in the back, sit in the front.

Why? Your perspective literally changes. You’ll see different faces, hear different voices, notice different dynamics.


SOUTH = SENSE (The Attention Game):

Mission: Count how many times a specific word is said. 

Examples:
“Synergy”

“Circle back”

“Touch base”

“Leverage”

The boss’s name

Why? It keeps your brain engaged during meetings that would otherwise put you to sleep. You’re gamifying attention. Your notebook has been sitting on the table, unused, for the last three meetings. It’s been ready to capture something important, but no one’s said anything worth writing down yet. Today, maybe today, someone will finally say something worth remembering.


WEST = WRITE (The Souvenir):

After the meeting, write one sentence:

“The word ‘stakeholders’ was said 11 times in 30 minutes and by the end it stopped sounding like a real word.”

“Sarah pitched an idea and everyone nodded but no one committed to doing it; that idea will die by tomorrow.”

“The meeting could have been an email, but I noticed that John really needed to say his piece out loud and feel heard; the meeting wasn’t about information, it was about emotion.”


The Long Game: From Presence to Mastery

Here’s what happens if you use the Compass at work consistently for six months:

Month 1-2: You notice things.The keyboard is worn. The customer is nervous. The coffee machine has a rhythm.

Month 3-4: You start to see patterns. Every Tuesday, the morning rush is worse. Every time a certain manager speaks, the room deflates. Every time you slow down, your work quality improves.

Month 5-6: You become a craftsperson. You’re no longer “just doing the job.” You’re refining it. You’re learning. You’re getting good.

And here’s the twist: People notice when you’re awake. Coworkers start asking your opinion. Managers start giving you more interesting projects. Customers remember your name. Not because you’re “trying harder.” Because you’re paying attention. And attention is rare. Attention is a superpower in a world of autopilot zombies.

And here’s the strangest part: when you start paying attention at work, the building itself starts to feel different. The fluorescent lights are still ugly. The beige walls are still soul-crushing. But you notice things. The hum of the HVAC system. The way the afternoon light hits the conference table at 3 PM. The specific smell of the breakroom coffee. The sound of your coworker’s keyboard - they type differently than you do, louder, more percussive. These details don’t make the job good. But they make it real. And real, even when it’s hard, is better than numb. Because numb is erasure. Real is proof you were here.


The Permission to Clock Out 

Final thought: The Compass at work isn’t about turning your job into your identity. It’s not about becoming a “workaholic” who loves the grind. It’s about being alive during work hours instead of waiting for 5 PM to start living. But here’s the boundary: When you clock out, you clock out. The Compass at work is for the hours you’re there. When you leave, you leave.

You don’t take work home. You don’t check emails at 10 PM. You don’t think about your job during dinner.

The goal is presence during, not obsession after.

You’re allowed to care about your work without being consumed by it. You’re allowed to be good at your job without making it your whole life. You’re allowed to be awake at work and asleep afterward. 

When you leave, your desk stays behind. Your chair, your mug, your keyboard, they’ll all be there tomorrow. They don’t mind. They’ve outlasted three employees before you. They’ll be there when you come back, ready to start again.

But you won’t be the same. You’ll have gone home, slept, eaten, lived your other life. You’ll come back tomorrow a slightly different version of yourself, maybe more tired, maybe more rested, maybe carrying something from home that your desk will never know about. And your desk doesn’t mind. It’s been holding the same pens, the same papers, the same coffee stains for years. It’s used to your absences. It knows you’ll come back. It expects your return. It’s more constant than most people in your life. And when you sit down tomorrow morning, it’ll support you again, just like it always has, asking nothing in return.

Sometimes, the work day is just a fight. You didn't find beauty. You didn't find flow. You just survived.

On those days, your Souvenir needs to be a Trophy.

The Office Compass


Chapter 21 Summary

What We Learned:

The Fluorescent Trap: We spend roughly 80,000 hours of our lives at work. Enduring those hours on autopilot ("just getting through it") means choosing to be unconscious for a massive percentage of your existence.

The Reframe: The goal isn't to force yourself to "love" a boring job (or tolerate a toxic one). The goal is to be present during the hours you're already spending there so you don't sleepwalk through 40 years of your life.

Two Types of Work Ruts:

The Repetition Rut: Mechanical autopilot (baristas, factory workers). The hands work, but the brain has left the building.

The Meaning Rut: Intellectual busywork (knowledge workers). You're doing the tasks, but you've lost the thread of why they matter or who they help.

The Office Compass (N-E-S-W):

North (The Spark): "I wonder, if a documentary crew followed me, what would they find interesting?" (e.g., the barista's ability to multitask, the coder's ability to speak machine language).

East (Micro-Shifts): Change one tiny variable. Sit in a different chair in the meeting. Take a different route to the breakroom. Break the loop to wake up the brain.

South (The Noticing Game): Use Modalities to find wonder. Material Study (look closely at your keyboard wear patterns). Process Autopsy (break a "simple" task into its 10 complex micro-steps). Human Moment (see one customer/patient as a person, not a transaction).

West (The Souvenir): Write one sentence at 5 PM before you leave. "What is one thing I noticed today that I usually ignore?"

Work Archetypes:

Repetitive Workers: Get to know your tools and materials or flip the routine (stock shelves right-to left.)

Knowledge Workers: Go deep on one task (craft one perfect email) instead of skimming twenty.

Caregivers/Teachers: Focus on one person per shift to break the "transactional trance."


Quick Win:
 When you sit down at your desk or station tomorrow, pick one object you touch every single day (your mouse, a wrench, a coffee mug, a stethoscope). Spend exactly 60 seconds looking at it like you've never seen it before. Notice the scratches. Notice the wear patterns. Notice the specific weight of it in your hand. This object is a tool that extends your ability to affect the world. Acknowledge it. You've just clocked in with your brain, not just your badge.
