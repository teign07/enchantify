Chapter 14: Digital Wonder




I need to tell you something that might sound contradictory after everything you’ve read so far. Your phone isn’t the enemy.

I know, I know. I’ve spent this entire book calling it “The Scroll of Numbness.” I’ve told you to put it down, to look up, to touch grass (like an old man). And all of that is still true.

But here’s what I didn’t tell you: The problem isn’t the screen itself. The problem is what you’re
doing with it.

There’s a difference between:

Scrolling TikTok at 2 AM, watching 40 videos in a row and remembering none of them (The Drift)

Spending two hours in Minecraft building a cathedral with your friend in another state while talking about nothing and everything (Connection)

There’s a difference between:

Doomscrolling Twitter until your chest feels tight and your brain feels like static (Anxiety)

Spending an evening learning Blender to model a dragon, failing 15 times, and finally getting the scales right (Mastery)

There’s a difference between:

Passively consuming other people’s lives until you hate your own (Comparison)

Playing a co-op game where you and your partner have to communicate perfectly to solve puzzles (Collaboration)

Same device. Same screen. Completely different relationship.

This chapter is for the people who read Chapter 8 (“South = Sense”) and thought: “But… I DO feel wonder. When I’m coding. When I’m gaming. When I’m creating digital art. When I’m in Discord with my community at 11 PM laughing until my face hurts.”

You’re not wrong. And you’re not broken.

Digital wonder is real wonder.

Let me be honest about something: I’m Gen-X who grew up with dial-up internet, Atari and Nokia phones. Amanda and I didn’t get smartphones until our late 20s. We remember what life was like before.

But if you’re Gen Z or younger, you don’t. The digital world isn’t something you “go to.” It’s where you already are.

For you, Discord isn’t “escaping reality”, it’s where your real friends live. And as we discussed in Chapter 11, for those dealing with chronic illness or unsafe physical environments, the digital world isn't a distraction - it's the only accessible territory for adventure. Your Minecraft server isn’t “just a game”, it’s a place you built with your own hands, where you have history and inside jokes and shared experiences. My digital home is Guild Wars 2. Or it is, when my mini-pc isn’t broken.

The research backs this up. A 2023 study from the Oxford Internet Institute found that multiplayer gaming with friends showed the same cortisol-lowering, oxytocin-boosting effects as in-person social interaction.

You’re not “addicted to screens.” You’re living in a different ecosystem these days. And that ecosystem has its own moss, its own clouds, its own weird mailboxes.

But here’s where it gets complicated: The Drift exists online too. And it’s way more sophisticated than it is in the physical world.


The Three Types of Screen Time

Not all screen time is created equal. Let me show you the taxonomy.

Type 1: The Drift 

What it looks like:

Auto-playing videos you didn’t choose

Infinite scroll feeds designed by behavioral psychologists

Content that makes you feel worse (rage-bait, comparison traps, doomscrolling)

Passive consumption with zero interaction 


How it feels afterward:

Empty. Anxious. Tired. You look at the clock and can’t remember what you just watched. Your eyes hurt. Not the normal “I’ve been reading” hurt - a deeper ache, like your eyeballs are bruised from the inside. Your jaw is tight. When did you start clenching your teeth? Your thumb has a weird cramped feeling from swiping. You stand up and your body feels heavy, like you’ve been sitting in one position for three hours. Because you have. But you have nothing to show for it. No memory. No creation. Just a vague sense of lost time and a phone battery at 18%.

The biological signature:

High cortisol (stress), low dopamine satisfaction (the hit never quite lands), DMN on fire (ruminating while scrolling).

This is The Drift. This is the enemy. This is what I was warning you about.

And I need to be real with you: I wrote this book, and I still fall into this. Just today, I spent both my breaks at work on Reddit; Trump is claiming victory on his Greenland threats, apparently. I ignored my own Compass and spent a half hour total scrolling bullshit politics.


Type 2: The Tool (Neutral)

What it looks like:

Looking up a recipe

Checking directions

Answering a work email

Using your phone to identify a bird with iNaturalist 


How it feels afterward:

Neutral. Functional. You used the device for its intended purpose and moved on. 

The biological signature:

Task-positive network engaged briefly, then disengaged. No emotional charge either way.

This is fine. This is what smartphones were supposed to be: tools that make life easier without consuming it.


Type 3: The Flow (The Wonder)

Your code wants to run - it’s been sitting in the compiler like a runner in the blocks, coiled, ready, waiting for you to press go. Your Minecraft castle has been holding its shape all day, every block exactly where you placed it, the unfinished turret open to the sky like a tooth with a gap. Your half-edited video has been frozen mid-sentence for three days, the playhead parked at minute four, the waveform still spiking where someone laughed, the next clip already loaded in the timeline, pressed right up against the cut point, ready to play the moment you press the spacebar.

What it looks like:

Creating something (digital art, music production, video editing, coding)

Playing a game that requires skill and strategy (not mindless tapping)

Collaborating with others in real-time (multiplayer games, shared docs, co-creation)

Learning something challenging (tutorials, courses, deep research) 


How it feels afterward:

Energized. Satisfied. Time disappeared but you have something to show for it (a creation, a level completed, a skill gained, a conversation remembered). 

The biological signature:

This is Flow. Transient hypofrontality (The Caveman goes quiet). High dopamine from mastery. Oxytocin if you were with others. Low cortisol despite high focus.

This is digital wonder. And it’s real. Here’s what it looks like: You’re in Blender. You’ve been trying to get the dragon’s scales to look right for an hour. You’ve failed seventeen times. The eighteenth time, you change one setting, just one, and suddenly the light catches the scales perfectly. They look wet. They look real. You rotate the model slowly. The scales shift in the light, each one catching a slightly different angle, throwing back a slightly different color. You didn’t paint those reflections. The rendering engine calculated them from the material properties you set, and the result is better than what you imagined. That’s the thing about digital creation that nobody talks about: the software collaborates. You set the parameters, but the renderer does things with light that you didn’t plan and couldn’t predict. The dragon is partly yours and partly the machine’s, and the parts you didn’t control are the parts that look alive. You sit back. Your shoulders have been up around your ears for twenty minutes and you didn’t notice until now. Your coffee is cold, it’s been cold for an hour, sitting there getting room temperature, holding its disappointment quietly. You take a sip anyway. It tastes like victory. You just spent ninety minutes failing and the last thirty seconds made all of it worth it. Your heart is doing that thing where it beats slightly harder, not from fear but from the specific satisfaction of making something exist that didn’t exist before. That’s not screen addiction. That’s the oldest human feeling there is - I made this.

I know what you’re thinking: “But how do I know if I’m in Flow or if I’m just telling myself I am while I waste another three hours?” Fair question. The Drift is sneaky, it can disguise itself as productivity or connection. So here’s how to tell the difference in real-time, not in retrospect when it’s too late.



The Diagnostic: Which Screen Are You On?

Here’s how to tell the difference in real-time:

Ask yourself these three questions:


Am I choosing this, or is it choosing me?

If you opened the app with a specific intention (“I’m going to message Alex”) → Tool/Flow

If your hand moved to the app automatically and you don’t remember deciding → Drift. The app opened itself. It’s been waiting for you, like a casino at the edge of town with the lights always on.

Could I explain what I just did for the last 30 minutes?

If yes (built a redstone circuit, edited photos, had a conversation) → Flow

If no (scrolled… something… videos? tweets? can’t remember) → Drift


Do I feel more or less alive than when I started?

More (energized, connected, proud) → Flow

Less (tired, empty, anxious) → Drift


Same → Tool


If you’re in The Drift more than 70% of your screen time, the “touch grass” advice applies. Put the phone down. Go outside. Use the physical-world Compass.

But if you’re genuinely in Flow? You don’t need to apologize. You don’t need to “balance it with nature.” You’re already doing the work.

You’re just doing it in a different medium.

So you’ve diagnosed where you are (Drift, Tool, or Flow). But what if you want to spend more time in Flow and less time in the Drift? What if you want to engineer digital wonder instead of stumbling into it by accident?

That’s what the Compass does in the digital world. Same four directions. Different terrain.



The Digital Compass (N-E-S-W for Screens)

So how do you use the Wonder Compass in the digital world?

The same way you use it in the physical world - you just change the territory.


NORTH = NOTICE (The Spark)

Physical World Spark: “I wonder what that weird mailbox looks like up close?”

Digital World Spark: “I wonder if I can recreate that effect I saw in Photoshop?” / “I wonder how that game mechanic actually works?” / “I wonder what happens if I combine these two mods?”

The key is active curiosity, not passive consumption. Bad North (Drift):

“I wonder what’s on TikTok right now?” (Opens to infinite feed)

“I wonder what drama is happening on Twitter?” (Opens to rage-bait) 


Good North (Flow):

Your half-finished Blender dragon has been sitting in that file, waiting. The code you wrote yesterday is curious about what you’ll do with it next. The empty Photoshop canvas isn’t judging you, it’s just ready.


“I wonder if I can beat this boss without taking damage?”

“I wonder what this code does if I change line 47?”

“I wonder if I can make my avatar look like a cyberpunk samurai?”


If you’re looking for a Spark on social media (Pinterest, Instagram, YouTube), you’re walking into a casino. The house is rigged to keep you there forever.

To survive, you need a hard stop. Try The Third-Scroll Rule.

When you open a feed to find inspiration, you get three swipes of the thumb.

Scroll 1: Curiosity.

Scroll 2: Checking.

Scroll 3: The Drift.

If you haven't found a Spark by the third scroll, it isn’t there. The algorithm hasn’t served you a wonder; it’s served you a sedative. The feed is patient. It will scroll forever if you let it. It has infinite inventory and zero shame. It doesn’t get tired. You do.

Close the app immediately. Don’t 'just check one more.' If the Spark didn't hit in three swipes, the environment is dead. Walk away.


That being said, I can talk about the Scroll of Numbness the whole book, but the honest truth is even I’m going to be scrolling every day. I need my Reddit. That being the case, we should at least make an attempt to make the best of it.

Most people think their social media feed is a reflection of the world. It isn’t. It’s a reflection of your lizard brain’s worst impulses, mirrored back to you by a machine designed to sell ads.

In the old days of social media, your feed was determined by who you followed. If you followed negative people, you saw negative things.

But today, we live in the era of Interest Media. Apps like TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts don’t really care who you follow. They care what you watch. They measure your attention in milliseconds.

If you pause for five seconds to watch a video of a "Karen" screaming in a Target, the algorithm takes notes. It thinks: "Okay. They like conflict. They like rage. Load up 50 more screaming videos."

You didn’t ask for rage. You just couldn’t look away from the car crash. But the robot doesn't know the difference between "horror" and "wonder." It only knows "engagement."

If you want to find Digital Wonder, you have to retrain the robot. You have to terraform your feed from a casino into a museum.

Here’s the protocol for priming your algorithm for Wonder:


Step 1: The Purge 
 Unfollow 5 accounts today. You know the ones. The influencers who make you feel poor. The news aggregators that make you feel hopeless. The people from high school who make you feel behind. Cut the cord.


Step 2: The Seed
 This is the most important step. You need to actively tell the algorithm what you want, or it will default to the lowest common denominator.

Right now, open your app of choice. Go to the search bar. Type in three of the following terms and watch at least three videos from each category all the way through. Like them. Save them.

The Wonder Seeding Menu:

For Texture: "Macro videography," "Slime mold time lapse," "Ink mixing," "Pottery glazing."

For Calm: "Studio Ghibli scenery," "Rain on window," "Cabin diaries," "Silent walking tours."

For Skill: "Watch restoration," "Wood turning," "Calligraphy ASMR," "Stop motion animation process."

For Perspective: "Deep sea creatures," "Hubble telescope images," "Electron microscope zoom."

By doing this, you are manually injecting a new signal into the noise. You are telling the butler: "No more trash. Bring me the art."


Step 3: The Hard Ignore
 The next time the algorithm tries to slip a piece of rage-bait into your feed - a political hot take, a public freakout, a "story time" about drama - don’t engage. Don’t comment. Don’t hate-watch.
Scroll past it instantly. Fast.

The algorithm is like a puppy with terrible taste. Right now, it keeps bringing you dead rats (rage-bait, drama, screaming videos) because you looked at one dead rat for five seconds and it thought you loved it. You need to train it. When it brings you a dead rat, ignore it immediately, don’t even look. When it brings you something beautiful (moss time-lapses, studio tours, woodworking), pet it and say ‘good dog.’ After a week, the puppy will stop bringing rats.

The Mechanic: Capture, Don't Just Consume
 Finally, the rule remains: If you see something wonderful, use the West (Write) step immediately.

If you watch a guy restore a rusted 1950s lighter and it makes your brain feel tingly and quiet, don’t just scroll to the next one. Screenshot it. Write it down.

"Watched a rusted lighter become shiny again; I love that things can be fixed."

If you scroll past it without capturing it, it wasn't wonder. It was just anesthetic.

You’ve found the Spark, the curiosity that pulls you in (“I wonder if I can build this?”). But curiosity without a plan becomes the Drift. You need boundaries, or you’ll be in that Discord server for six hours without remembering why you opened it.





EAST = EMBARK (The Plan)

Physical World Plan: Coffee, playlist, home by dinner.

Digital World Plan: Session boundaries, specific goal, definition of “done.”

The Recipe (3 D’s - Digital Edition):

Destination: What am I actually doing?

“I’m building the western tower of my castle in Minecraft”

“I’m editing the first 10 minutes of my video”

“I’m playing ranked matches until I win three”


Delight: What makes this session feel good?

The right playlist

A good snack

A friend in voice chat

A cozy setup (good lighting, comfortable chair)


Definition: When am I done?

“When the tower is finished”

“When the timer hits 90 minutes”

“When I win three or lose three, whichever comes first”

Why this matters: Without a Definition, you fall into “just one more match” syndrome. You play for 6 hours, win nothing, and feel worse. The Definition creates a clean exit.

You’ve set your boundaries, your Definition of “done,” your Delight to make it pleasant, your Destination to keep you focused. Now you sit down, open the app, and start. But here’s where most people lose it: they forget to stay present. They’re physically there, but mentally they’ve drifted into autopilot. That’s what South is for.




SOUTH = SENSE (The Playful Mission)

This is where digital wonder gets interesting.

In the physical world, you’re hunting for textures, sounds, colors. In the digital world, you’re hunting for emergent behavior, moments when the system surprises you.

There’s a physical phenomenon called 'Screen Apnea.' When we stare at a device, we tend to shallow-breathe or hold our breath entirely. Our shoulders collapse. We become floating heads disconnected from our bodies. The “Brain in a Jar” from Chapter 3.

You can’t experience Wonder if you’ve severed the connection to your own nervous system.

Before you start any digital mission, before you open the game, the coding terminal, or the design software, try these:

Hydrate: Put a glass of water next to the screen.

Posture: Fix your chair. Feet on the floor. Spine against the backrest.

Audio: Put on headphones. Control the soundscape so the world doesn't bleed in.

Your chair has been holding the shape of you for months. The cushion has compressed exactly where your weight lands; there’s a dip on the left side because you lean that way when you’re thinking. The armrests are slightly worn where your elbows rest during long sessions. The hydraulic cylinder has settled to the height you always use, the one that’s two inches too low because you never bothered to adjust it. Right now, before you sit down, fix it. Raise it. Put your feet flat. Give the chair the posture it’s been trying to give you; it’s been sinking under your weight for months and you keep overriding it. By preparing your physical body, you signal to your brain that this is an active sport, not a passive coma.

Examples of Digital Playful Missions:

In Minecraft:

“I’m going to build a house using only materials I find within 100 blocks of spawn”

“I’m going to dig straight down until I hit bedrock and see what biomes I pass through”

“I’m going to tame every wolf I see and create a pack”


In a Creative App (Photoshop, Blender, Procreate):


“I’m going to use only three colors”

“I’m going to draw without using the undo button”

“I’m going to make something ugly on purpose”


In a Coding Project:


“I’m going to make this function work using only one line”

“I’m going to break this on purpose and see what error message I get”

“I’m going to add one completely unnecessary feature just because it’s funny”


In a Multiplayer Game:

“I’m going to play support and see how many assists I can rack up without getting any kills”

“I’m going to use only the ‘bad’ weapon and see how far I get”

“I’m going to call out one good play by a teammate every round”


The mission gives you a lens. It transforms “playing a game” into “exploring what the game can do.”


Here’s what flow feels like in a game, and I mean real flow, not the numb autopilot of your fortieth match.

You’re in it. Your fingers know the controls so well that you’ve stopped thinking about which button does what, your thumbs are just translating intention into action with no conscious step in between. Your peripheral vision is tracking three things simultaneously. You hear the footsteps before you see the enemy. Your breathing has synced to the rhythm of the game without you deciding to do it.

And then the moment happens. The clutch play. The impossible shot. The split-second decision that somehow worked. Your team is screaming in the voice chat. Your hands are shaking slightly,  not from fear, but from adrenaline that has nowhere to go because you’re sitting in a chair. Your heart rate is probably 120. You’re grinning at a screen and you don’t care how that looks from the outside…

…Or it’s quieter than that. You’ve been exploring a map for an hour. No combat, no objectives, just walking. The game designers put a view at the top of this hill that nobody made you climb. The soundtrack swells at the exact moment the landscape opens up. You stop moving your character. You just look. The developers spent months on this vista, the way the light filters through the trees, the draw distance, the ambient sound design. Someone made this for the moment you’d stand here. And standing here, you feel the same chest-opening feeling you felt on your own version of the Two Cent Bridge, or at the top of a parking garage watching a sunset. The feeling doesn’t care whether the landscape is rendered in polygons or photons. Your nervous system responds to beauty. Period.

That’s digital wonder. It’s not lesser. It’s not a substitute. It’s the same brain, responding to the same triggers - novelty, mastery, beauty, connection - through a different medium.



You’ve been in the mission for an hour. Your shoulders dropped. Your breath synced to the rhythm. You built something, or learned something, or connected with someone. And now you’re about to close the app and move on. Don’t skip this step. This is where the experience becomes a memory instead of evaporating into the void.




WEST = WRITE (The Souvenir)

This is the step people skip online. They have the experience, they feel the flow, and then they close the app and… it evaporates.

The Digital Souvenir is no different from the physical one.

Physical souvenir: “The coffee shop smelled like cinnamon and rain.” 

Digital souvenir: One Sentence Souvenir or possible screenshot + sentence or clip + caption.




Examples:

After a Minecraft session:

Screenshot of your finished build + “Spent three hours on this tower and the sunrise through the windows is perfect”


After a creative session:

Screenshot of your work-in-progress + “This started as a dragon and ended up as a weird gremlin but I love him”

After a gaming session:

Clip of the final kill + “We were down 12-3 and somehow won in overtime, my heart is still racing”

After a coding session:

Screenshot of your terminal + “Finally got this function working after 2 hours of debugging, the error was a single missing semicolon and I’m not even mad”

The key: Capture the moment before you move on to the next thing.

Don’t just close the game and open Twitter. Don’t just finish the edit and immediately start doomscrolling.

Take 30 seconds. Screenshot it. Write the sentence. Save the clip.

Here’s what happens when you skip this step: You close the game. You’re still buzzing from the win. You open Twitter “just to check.” Fifteen minutes later, the buzz is gone. You’re reading about some celebrity drama you don’t care about. The victory you just earned? Already evaporating. The dopamine from the flow state is being drained by the Drift.

But if you take 30 seconds, screenshot, one sentence, save, you anchor it. You prove to your brain that what just happened was real and worth remembering. The screenshot is a stake in the ground that says: This counted.

You’re creating the same memory anchor you’d create from a physical adventure.

A screenshot is good, but it’s also easy to ignore. To truly anchor the memory, you might try to pull the experience out of the digital world and force it into the physical one.

If you built a castle in Minecraft: Don't just screenshot it. Sketch the outline of it on a napkin. The napkin takes the ink immediately - no loading time, no lag, no undo button. The lines bleed slightly into the fibers, which means your sketch of the Minecraft castle looks softer and rougher than the original, more like a memory than a blueprint. That’s the point. It crossed over. It exists on paper now, and paper doesn’t have a delete key.

If you found a meme that made you laugh: Don't just save it. Describe it out loud to a friend or write the caption in your notebook.

If you had a deep conversation on Discord: Write the most important quote on a Post-it note and stick it to your monitor.

When you translate digital code into physical ink or sound, you break the spell of the simulation. You prove to your brain that the joy was real, because it crossed the border into real life.


That’s the full Digital Compass. North to find the spark. East to set boundaries. South to stay present. West to capture the memory.

But digital wonder isn’t one-size-fits-all. Some people are isolated creatives who need solo flow. Others are socially starved and need connection. Others are intellectually bored and need to learn something hard.

Here are three levels, each targeting a different kind of hunger. Use whichever one fits your current void.

The Symptom: Creative Starvation

And here’s the secret: Even “bad” creation feels better than “good” consumption.


That’s the fix for creative starvation - making something alone, even if it’s ugly. But what if your hunger isn’t creative? What if it’s social? What if you’re surrounded by people online but still feel invisible?



Level 2: The Guild (Multiplayer Connection)

Delight: Have a specific thing to share (a Souvenir, a meme, a question, a weird observation)

And here's what usually happens: When you post something specific and real, the lurkers notice. They've been waiting for someone to go first. Your post doesn't just break your own silence - it gives other people permission to break theirs.

And then there’s the specific, private, almost embarrassing joy of code that runs.

You’ve been staring at the same twenty lines for forty minutes. The error message is the same one you’ve seen nine times. You’ve Googled it. You’ve asked ChatGPT. You’ve read three Stack Overflow threads. You’ve added print statements everywhere to figure out where it breaks. Your eyes are doing that thing where they slide over the same characters without reading them because you’ve looked at this function so many times your brain has stopped actually seeing it.

And then you see it. The missing bracket. The wrong variable name. The semicolon that should be a colon. It’s always something tiny. Something your eyes skipped over because it was so small your brain autocorrected it.

You fix it. You press run. The terminal fills with the output you wanted. It works. It actually works.
The feeling is… there’s no word for it exactly. It’s not triumph. It’s not relief. It’s more like: the universe just agreed with you. You said ‘do this thing’ in a language made of symbols and logic, and the machine said ‘okay,’ and the thing happened. You spoke and something listened. For a moment, you were fluent in a language that makes things happen.

That moment, that exact moment when the output matches the intention, is one of the purest forms of wonder available on a screen. You didn’t find it. You built it. And it works.


WEST = WRITE (The Souvenir)

Screenshot your completed project (the basic cube you modeled, the “Hello World” output, the 8-second beat that loops).

Example Souvenir: “Made my first Python script and it actually ran without crashing; it doesn’t do anything useful but I understand what a variable is now and that feels like learning a new language.”

Why This Works:

When you learn something, even something small, you get a dopamine hit from competence.
 You prove to yourself that you’re not just consuming content. You’re building skills.

And here’s the long game: Three months from now, you won’t remember the 400 TikToks you watched. But you will remember that you can code “Hello World.” Or edit a video. Or make a beat.

Skills stack. Scrolling doesn’t.


Those are the three bright-side uses of the digital world: creating alone, connecting with others, learning something hard. But there’s a fourth use case I need to address. It’s uncomfortable, and I’ve been avoiding it until now. Let’s talk about the dark spaces.



The Shadow Digital Wonder

Let’s talk about something uncomfortable.

What about the digital spaces where people go when they’re not okay?

The subreddits for depression, addiction, grief

The Discord servers for people who can’t sleep at 3 AM

The forums where people share their worst thoughts because they can’t say them out loud

Are those “The Drift”? Or are they something else? 

Here’s my take: They’re both.

If you’re doomscrolling r/depression and comparing your misery to others’ misery and spiraling deeper, that’s The Drift.

But if you’re posting, “I’m struggling and I don’t know what to do,” and someone replies, “Hey, I’ve been there, here’s what helped me,” and you feel less alone, that’s connection.

The digital world has a unique superpower: It can connect you to people who understand what you’re going through when no one in your physical life does.

If you’re queer in a rural town, or neurodivergent in a neurotypical family, or grieving something no one around you understands, the internet might be the only place you can find your people. We touched on this in Chapter 11 regarding physical safety, but it applies to emotional safety too. The digital world removes the physical threats of the 'real' world, allowing you to find community without risking your body.

That’s not “screen addiction.” That’s survival.

But, and this is crucial, you can still use the Compass.

And sometimes, that’s enough.


That’s Shadow Wonder, the Compass applied to the hardest places. But not every digital adventure has to be that heavy. Sometimes the digital world and the physical world can play together, passing the ball back and forth, each making the other more interesting.

That’s the optimistic view - digital and physical working together, each enhancing the other. But I’d be lying if I didn’t acknowledge reality. The reason I’ve been so cautious about screen time throughout this book isn’t because screens are evil. It’s because the companies controlling the screens have weaponized your attention.




The Warning: The Algorithm Wants You

The reason I’ve been so harsh on “screen time” throughout this book isn’t because screens are evil. It’s because the algorithms running the screens are designed to exploit your biology.

TikTok, Instagram, Twitter, YouTube, they’re not neutral tools. They’re attention extraction machines optimized by AI to keep you scrolling as long as possible.

The research is unambiguous:

Infinite scroll feeds create the same dopamine loop as slot machines

Variable reward schedules (you never know if the next video will be good) create addiction patterns

Algorithmic rage-bait increases engagement by making you angry (anger = comments = “engagement”)

These systems are not designed for your wellbeing. They’re designed for ad impressions.

So when I say “the screen is the enemy,” what I mean is: The companies controlling the screen are the enemy.

But here’s the good news: You have power. 

The Resistance:

Use apps that don’t have algorithms: Minecraft, Blender, GarageBand, Guild Wars 2, Notion, Obsidian, these are tools, not feeds.

Disable autoplay: YouTube and Netflix autoplay are weaponized against you. Turn them oﬀ in settings.

Set time limits: Use Apple Screen Time or Android Digital Wellbeing to cap social apps at 30 minutes/day.

Follow creators, not trends: Curate your feed. Follow real people making real things. Unfollow accounts that make you feel bad.

Create more than you consume: If your screen time is 80% consumption, flip it. Make 80% creation (editing photos, writing, coding, building in Minecraft).

You don’t have to delete the apps. You just have to take control back.


Those are the tactical defenses, the settings you change, the apps you delete, the boundaries you enforce. But there’s one more tool, and it’s not about resisting the algorithm. It’s about remembering why you’re fighting to stay in Flow in the first place.



One final tool: The Screenshot Folder.

The 3D model you made in Blender that you’re proud of You’re building the same proof library you build with physical souvenirs.

You’re showing yourself: The internet isn’t just a wasteland. It’s also where you created things. Where you connected with people. Where you learned.

Open that folder right now. Even if it only has three things in it. Look at them. That Minecraft castle you built with your friend, the screenshot captured the sunset through the windows at the exact angle you placed them, the light still hitting the floor in orange squares. That meme you made,  the image file is 247 kilobytes, barely anything, taking up less space than a single song, but every time you open it the joke still lands. That kind comment from a stranger - the text hasn’t changed. Same words, same order, same meaning. Digital text doesn’t fade the way ink does. That’s not nothing. That’s a life.

But there’s also a short-term defense you need: how to exit a Flow session without immediately sliding into the Drift. The Re-Entry (Digital Edition)

Finally, let’s talk about digital re-entry, how to exit a good screen session without immediately falling into The Drift.

The Protocol:

The Clean Exit (2 minutes):

When you finish your session (gaming, creating, coding), don’t just close the app and open Twitter. Close everything. Stand up. Stretch. Look at something that’s not a screen for 60 seconds.

The Souvenir Moment (1 minute):

Take the screenshot. Write the sentence. Save the clip. Lock it in.

The Transition Task (5 minutes):

Do one physical task before you go back to any screen: Drink water, pet your cat, do 10 pushups, step outside, anything. You’re giving your brain a hard reset between “Flow State” and “Consumption State.”

Here’s what the transition feels like: You’ve been in the game for two hours. You’re in it - your brain is in flow, your senses are sharp, your reactions are fast. Now you close the laptop. For five seconds, the room feels weird. It’s been sitting here the whole time, waiting for you to remember it exists. It’s too quiet now because you’ve been inside a game that’s louder than real life. The room isn’t mad, it’s just patient. It’s been here, holding your chair, your desk, your coffee mug. It’s ready when you are. Your body remembers it has weight. Your eyes have to readjust to objects that aren’t glowing. This is the moment. This is where most people immediately open another screen to avoid the disorientation. Don’t. Stand up. Walk to the window. Look outside. Let your eyes focus on something 20 feet away. Feel your body come back online. You’re re-entering the physical world. Give yourself the landing gear.

Why this matters:

The trap isn’t gaming for 3 hours. The trap is gaming for 3 hours, then immediately scrolling TikTok for 2 more hours because your brain is still in “screen mode” and The Drift is easier than stopping.

The transition task breaks the loop. It forces a context switch.

You’re treating screen time like physical adventures: Clear start. Clear end. Clean transition.
