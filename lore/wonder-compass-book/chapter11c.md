Chapter 11C: The Safe Compass




I imagine there must be a specific kind of exhaustion that happens when you’re a woman reading travel advice written by men, like me.

You’re reading an article that says “Just wander! Get lost! Talk to strangers! That’s where the magic happens!” And you think: Have you ever been followed for six blocks? Have you ever had to fake a phone call because someone won’t leave you alone? Have you ever calculated which side of the street has better lighting in case you need to run?

Or you’re a person of color reading about “authentic local experiences” in rural areas, and you’re thinking: Do you know what it’s like to be the only brown face in a town where people stare? Have you ever had to plan your route based on which gas stations feel safest?

Or you’re visibly queer reading about “spontaneous adventures,” and you’re thinking: Do you know what it costs me to hold my partner’s hand in public? Have you ever had to assess whether a restaurant is safe before you walk in?

And then you open a book like this one. A book that promises “adventure” and “escape from the Rut.” You read the examples:

“Drive to a town you’ve never been to and just walk around”

“Wander down unfamiliar streets and see what you find”

“Strike up conversations with strangers”

“Get lost - that’s where the best stories happen”

And something in your chest goes cold. Because you know that “getting lost” in the wrong neighborhood at the wrong time isn’t an adventure. It’s a gamble. And the stakes aren’t “I had a boring afternoon.” The stakes are harassment, violence, or worse.

You feel that familiar sensation - the one where your jaw clenches and your shoulders rise. The one where you think: Of course. Another book that assumes the world is safe for everyone. Another set of instructions that could get me hurt.

The one that says: Oh. This book isn’t for me either.

You start to close it.

Stop. I thought about you, too. A lot.

Don’t close it yet.

This chapter is for you. Not as a disclaimer. Not as a “please be careful” afterthought. As acknowledgment that the Rut doesn’t care about your demographics - it will swallow you whole whether you’re safe or not - but the tools to escape it have to account for the fact that the world isn’t equally safe for all bodies.

Some people can wander freely. You can’t. So let’s rebuild the Compass for the terrain you’re actually navigating - one where curiosity and caution aren’t opposites, but partners.

But first, let’s be honest about why this matters.




The Science: Why Safety Determines Wonder

Let’s start with the biological reality that most adventure books ignore:

You can’t feel wonder if you don’t feel safe.

This isn’t a metaphor. This isn’t about “mindset.” This is neuroscience.

Here’s why.




The Nervous System Hierarchy (Polyvagal Theory)

In the 1990s, psychiatrist Stephen Porges developed Polyvagal Theory, which explains how your nervous system responds to safety and threat. The theory identifies three states:

Ventral Vagal (Safe & Social)

When your nervous system perceives safety, you enter this state. Your body is calm. Your heart rate is steady. Your breathing is easy. In this state, you can:

	∙	Feel curious

	∙	Notice beauty

	∙	Connect with others

	∙	Explore your environment

This is the state where wonder happens.



2. Sympathetic (Fight or Flight)

When your nervous system detects a potential threat, you shift into this state. Your body is alert. Your heart rate increases. Your muscles tense. Your attention narrows to scanning for danger. In this state, you:

	∙	Cannot feel awe (your brain is too busy assessing threat)

	∙	Cannot be playful (play requires safety)

	∙	Cannot notice beauty (all cognitive resources are devoted to survival)

You’re not “choosing” to be anxious. Your nervous system has decided you’re not safe enough to relax.




3. Dorsal Vagal (Shutdown)

When the threat feels overwhelming and inescapable, you enter this state. Your body freezes. You feel numb, disconnected, exhausted. This is the nervous system’s last-ditch survival strategy when fight and flight aren’t options.

In this state, wonder isn’t even on the table. You’re just trying to survive.




The Critical Point:

Wonder only happens in State 1 (Ventral Vagal). If your nervous system is stuck in State 2 (hypervigilance) or State 3 (shutdown), you physically can’t access curiosity.

It’s not a character flaw. It’s biology.

And here’s the problem: If you’re a woman walking alone at night, or a person of color in certain neighborhoods, or visibly queer in hostile spaces, your nervous system spends most of its time in State 2. You’re scanning. Assessing. Calculating escape routes.

Your amygdala (the brain’s threat detector) is running full-time, and it’s overriding the parts of your brain responsible for curiosity and awe.




The Amygdala Hijack

Your amygdala is a small almond-shaped structure deep in your brain. Its job is simple: detect threats and respond before you have time to think. The Caveman.

When your amygdala perceives danger (a dark street, a man following too closely, a hostile look), it triggers a cascade of physiological changes:

	∙	Adrenaline floods your system

	∙	Your heart rate spikes

	∙	Your peripheral vision narrows (tunnel vision focused on the threat)

	∙	Your prefrontal cortex (the part responsible for curiosity, creativity, and higher thinking) goes offline

This is called an “amygdala hijack.” And when it happens, you literally can’t notice the architecture of a building or the beauty of a sunset. Your brain has one priority: Get to safety.

The moss can wait. Your survival can’t.





Neuroception: Threat Detection Below Awareness

Here’s where it gets more complicated: Your nervous system is constantly assessing safety and threat below your conscious awareness.

Porges calls this “neuroception” - the subconscious detection of safety or danger through environmental cues.

Your nervous system is reading:

	∙	Facial expressions

	∙	Body language

	∙	Tone of voice

	∙	Environmental cues (lighting, number of exits, presence of other people)

	∙	Cultural and historical context (this neighborhood, this demographic, this time of day)

And it’s making split-second decisions about whether you’re safe before you consciously register why you feel uneasy.

This is why you might walk down a street and suddenly feel wrong without being able to articulate why. Your nervous system detected something (a person’s posture, the lack of streetlights, the absence of other pedestrians) and shifted you into hypervigilance.

You’re not being paranoid. You’re responding to real data your conscious mind hasn’t processed yet.

And when your nervous system says “not safe,” wonder shuts down. Not because you’re “doing it wrong.” Because your biology is prioritizing survival over scenery.





The Wandering Gap (Who Gets to Explore?)

Let’s look at the data on who actually feels safe wandering.

Gender Gap:

A 2021 UN Women survey found that 97% of women aged 18-24 in the UK had experienced sexual harassment in public spaces. 97%. That’s not a small minority. That’s nearly universal.

A 2023 study published in Urban Studies found that women’s mobility patterns are significantly constrained by fear of harassment and assault:

	∙	68% of women avoid certain streets or neighborhoods after dark

	∙	45% avoid public transportation during certain hours

	∙	34% avoid walking alone even during daylight in unfamiliar areas

Meanwhile, men’s mobility patterns show no such constraints.

The “Wandering Gap” isn’t about courage or adventurousness. It’s about who can afford to turn off their threat detection system.





Racial Gap:

A 2020 study in the Journal of Environmental Psychology found that Black Americans report significantly lower feelings of safety in predominantly white neighborhoods, parks, and recreational spaces.

The phenomenon of “hiking while Black” has been extensively documented: Black hikers, campers, and outdoor enthusiasts report being questioned, followed, or reported to authorities at rates far exceeding white outdoor enthusiasts.

For many people of color, “exploring an unfamiliar area” means calculating the risk of being perceived as “out of place” or “suspicious.”





LGBTQ+ Gap:

The Trevor Project’s 2023 survey found that 37% of LGBTQ+ youth avoid certain spaces or activities due to fear of discrimination or violence.

The decision of whether to hold a partner’s hand, wear certain clothing, or exist visibly in public spaces is a constant safety calculation.





The Cost of Hypervigilance:

Chronic hypervigilance has measurable health impacts:

	∙	Elevated cortisol (stress hormone) levels

	∙	Increased risk of anxiety and depression

	∙	Sleep disturbances

	∙	Cardiovascular strain

	∙	Cognitive fatigue

Living in State 2 (Fight or Flight) long-term isn’t sustainable. But for many people, it’s not optional. The world isn’t safe enough to relax.





The Implication for Wonder:

If your baseline is hypervigilance, you can’t “just wander” the way travel books suggest. Your nervous system won’t allow it.

But here’s the critical nuance: You don’t need perfect safety. You need “safe enough.”





The “Safe Enough” Threshold

Research on curiosity and exploration shows that humans (and animals) only explore when they feel “safe enough” - not perfectly safe, but safe enough that their nervous system can downregulate from hypervigilance to calm alertness.

What creates “safe enough”?

	∙	Predictability (you know the area, the time of day, the likely people you’ll encounter)

	∙	Presence of others (you’re not alone)

	∙	Easy escape routes (you can leave quickly if needed)

	∙	Control (you chose to be here, you can choose to leave)

	∙	Social camouflage (you don’t stand out; you look “normal” for the context)

You don’t need zero risk. You need manageable risk.

And that’s what this chapter is about: creating conditions where your nervous system can downregulate enough to allow curiosity, even if it can’t fully relax.




The Reality:

Many examples in this book assume you can:

Wander unfamiliar neighborhoods safely

Stop to look at things without being questioned

Explore without being perceived as a threat (or threatened)

Make “weird” choices in public without consequences 




If you feel actively unsafe:

Abort the Compass immediately. Your safety is more important than finishing the cycle.

Go to the nearest public place with people (store, cafe, library, police station).

Write the Souvenir later, from safety: “I trusted my gut and left when that guy
started following me; that counts as noticing, and it kept me alive.”





“I Don’t Feel Safe Anywhere”

If your baseline is chronic unsafety (domestic violence, neighborhood violence, systemic persecution):

The Compass might not work for you right now.

Not because you’re broken. Because Maslow’s Hierarchy is real - you can’t seek wonder when you’re in survival mode.

What to do instead:

Prioritize actual safety first. Hotlines, shelters, legal aid, escape plans. Those come before moss.

If you have micro-pockets of safety (a locked bathroom, a specific time of day, a friend’s house), use the Compass there.

Digital Wonder is valid. If the physical world is hostile, the digital world can be your territory:

North: “I wonder what Madagascar looks like on Google Earth?”

East: “Destination: Madagascar on Google Earth, Delight: Spotify too, Definition: A few songs.”

South: “I’ll zoom in close on this photo and find one detail I’ve never noticed.”

West: “The moss in that photo is so green it looks fake, like someone painted it for a movie set.”

This isn’t “lesser” wonder. This is survival adaptation.






The Adaptations:





For North (Notice):

Your Sparks need a safety filter.

Instead of: “I wonder what’s down that pretty sketchy alley?” Try: “I wonder what the park looks like at noon on Saturday when it’s full of people?”

Instead of: “I wonder what that abandoned building looks like inside?” Try: “I wonder what the public library’s architecture looks like from across the street?”

The Vibe Check Rule: If a location gives you a bad feeling, that’s North telling you something. “I wonder why this place feels wrong?” is a valid Spark. You don’t have to go there to answer it.





For East (Embark):

Build safety into the Adventure Recipe.

Destination: Only places with:

Good lighting

Other people around

Easy exits

Cell phone signal

Delight: Bring a safety item and a Delight:

Pepper spray in your pocket + a good playlist

A friend on the phone (even silent) + mocha latte

A dog (if you have one) + treats for the dog

Definition: “I’ll explore for 20 minutes or until I feel unsafe, then I leave immediately. No guilt.”





For South (Sense):

Playful Missions that keep you visible and “normal-looking” (see Chapter 8A for more on Stealth Playful Missions):

Instead of: Crouching to look at moss (looks suspicious) Try: Taking a photo of the moss with your phone (looks touristy)

Instead of: Walking backward (looks intoxicated/unstable) Try: Walking slowly while “on a phone call” (looks normal)

Instead of: Closing your eyes in public (vulnerable) Try: Closing your eyes for 10 seconds while sitting on a bench (less vulnerable)


The “Camouflage” Missions:

The Coffee Shop Anthropologist: Sit in a cafe with a book. Your mission: people-watch without staring. Invent backstories. That guy in the corner with the laptop? His coffee’s gone cold. The mug is just sitting there like ‘I tried, man. I was hot for a whole ten minutes and you ignored me.’ The steam gave up an hour ago.

The “I’m Waiting For Someone” Technique: Stand near a landmark acting like you’re waiting. Your mission: observe for 5 minutes, then leave.

The Photo Alibi: Hold your phone like you’re taking pictures of the sky or the buildings (avoiding people). Cameras are socially acceptable ways to stare at objects.





For West (Write):

Write your Souvenir before you leave the safe zone.

Don’t wait until you get home. Write it in the car, in the cafe, on the bench.

Why? Because if something did go wrong, the Souvenir captures that the adventure also had good parts:

“The park was beautiful until that guy started following me, but I did see a blue heron, and I’m proud I trusted my gut and left.”

This isn’t negativity. This is reality.






The "Safe Zone" Concept

Wonder is fractal. It exists at the macro level (a mountain range) and the micro level (a houseplant). If your physical environment limits how far you can wander, you don't go wide; you go deep.

This is establishing your Safe Zone.

The Porch Radius: Maybe your adventure is limited to your own front porch or balcony. Fine. Your mission is to find the weirdest bug on the railing, or to watch the specific way the wind hits the tree across the street. Your porch railing is the line in the sand. It says to the chaotic world: "You stop here." Lean on it. Feel how sturdy it is. It’s holding the line for you so you can watch the world without being threatened by it. Run your hand along it right now if you can reach it. Feel the wood grain, or the cool metal, or the rough concrete. It’s been standing here longer than your current crisis. It’s taken rain and snow and hundred-degree summers and it hasn’t quit yet. It’s stubborn. It refuses to collapse even though it probably should have by now. You can lean on it.

The Daylight Rule: Maybe you only explore between 10:00 AM and 2:00 PM. That counts.

The Buddy System: Maybe you never Embark alone. You bring a partner, a big dog, or a loud friend.

The Indoor Expedition: Maybe today, the world feels too chaotic to engage with at all. So the "Territory" becomes your living room rug. (We have a whole recipe for this in Chapter 17).

The Compass doesn't care how far you walk. It only cares how deeply you notice.

You don’t need to be a daring explorer conquering the unknown. You can be a quiet observer in a Safe Zone. You don’t need to risk your safety to save your soul.


When Your Territory Is Tiny

North: “I wonder which house on my block has the most interesting front door?”

East: Walk to the end of the block during daylight, with your phone visible (looks purposeful). Delight = your favorite song in headphones. Definition = one lap, 10 minutes max.

Sense: When you pass each door, count how many colors it has. Which one has the most? The fewest?

West: “The blue door at #47 has seven different paint chips showing through, someone has lived there a long time.”





The System: The Safety Audit Protocol

If you’re going to use the Compass in a world that isn’t equally safe for all bodies, you need a system for mapping your terrain.

This is the Safety Audit Protocol.




Step 1: Map Your Safe Times

Different times of day carry different levels of risk. For many people:

	∙	Early morning (6-9 AM): Relatively safe (people commuting, good visibility)

	∙	Midday (10 AM - 2 PM): Safest (high foot traffic, full daylight)

	∙	Late afternoon (3-5 PM): Moderately safe (still light, people around)

	∙	Evening (6-9 PM): Risk increases (getting dark, fewer people)

	∙	Night (10 PM+): Highest risk (darkness, isolation)

Your safety windows might be different based on your location and identity. The point is to identify them.

Action: Write down the time windows when you feel safest. Those are your “exploration hours.”





Step 2: Map Your Safe Zones

Not all spaces are equally safe. Identify your zones:

Green Zones (High Safety):

	∙	Your home

	∙	Your porch/balcony

	∙	Your immediate block (during safe times)

	∙	Well-lit parks with other people

	∙	Libraries, cafes, stores (public spaces with staff and customers)


Yellow Zones (Moderate Safety - Requires Vigilance):

	∙	Unfamiliar neighborhoods (during daylight)

	∙	Parks with few people

	∙	Public transportation

	∙	Anywhere you feel “watched” or “out of place”


Red Zones (Low Safety - Avoid or Extreme Caution):

	∙	Dark or isolated areas

	∙	Anywhere your gut says “wrong”

	∙	Spaces where you’ve previously felt threatened

	∙	Areas with hostile social dynamics toward your identity

Action: On a map (mental or actual), mark your zones. Your Compass expeditions happen in Green Zones, occasionally in Yellow Zones with precautions. Never in Red Zones.





Step 3: Build Your Safety Toolkit

Before any Compass expedition, assemble your safety toolkit:


Physical Tools:

	∙	Charged phone

	∙	Pepper spray or personal alarm (if legal and you’re comfortable)

	∙	Comfortable shoes you can run in if needed


Social Tools:

	∙	Friend on speed dial

	∙	Location sharing turned on (if you trust someone with that access)

	∙	Check-in system (“I’ll text you when I’m done”)


Psychological Tools:

	∙	Pre-planned escape routes

	∙	Cover story if questioned (“I’m waiting for someone”)

	∙	Permission to leave immediately if it feels wrong


Camouflage Tools:

	∙	Phone/camera (makes loitering look purposeful)

	∙	Headphones (discourages unwanted interaction)

	∙	Sunglasses (hides where you’re looking)

	∙	Notebook (makes you look like you belong)


Action: Before you Embark, mentally check: Do I have what I need to feel safe enough?






Step 4: The Three-Tier Escalation Plan

If something feels wrong during a Compass expedition, you need a pre-planned response. This is your Escalation Plan:


Tier 1: Mild Discomfort (Something feels slightly off, but no immediate threat)

Response:

	∙	Increase vigilance

	∙	Move toward more populated/lit areas

	∙	Stay in motion (don’t stop to observe)

	∙	Check in with your gut: Is this intuition or general anxiety?


Tier 2: Moderate Threat (Someone following you, hostile looks, gut screaming “leave”)

Response:

	∙	Abort the mission immediately

	∙	Move toward public space (store, cafe, gas station)

	∙	Call someone (even if you don’t speak, just having someone on the line helps)

	∙	Do NOT worry about “overreacting” - trust your nervous system


Tier 3: Immediate Danger (Active harassment, physical threat, no time to think)

Response:

	∙	Make noise (yell, scream, trigger personal alarm)

	∙	Run toward people/lights

	∙	Call 911 if appropriate (acknowledging this may not feel safe for everyone)

	∙	Your only priority is survival


The Rule: At any tier, you are allowed to leave. The Compass doesn’t care if you “finished.” Your safety is more important than any Souvenir.





Step 5: Write the Souvenir From Safety

If something went wrong during your expedition, the Souvenir still matters. But write it from a safe location.

Don’t wait until you get home if you’re shaken. Write it in the car, in the cafe, on the bus. Capture what happened, including the fact that you left.

Examples:

“The park was beautiful until I noticed someone following me; I left immediately and  I’m proud I trusted my gut - the red squirrel I saw before that still counts.”

“I only made it two blocks before the vibe felt wrong, turned around, no shame; I noticed the way the light hit the pavement, and that’s enough.”

“Spent the whole walk hypervigilant and couldn’t relax enough to notice anything and I guess that’s data about this neighborhood at this time -  I’ll try a different time/place.”


These are all valid Souvenirs. You’re not failing. You’re adapting.






The Long-Term Practice: Training Your Gut

Over time, this protocol helps you distinguish between:

	∙	Generalized anxiety (background noise)

	∙	Rational caution (real risk factors present)

	∙	Intuitive warning (your nervous system detected something before your conscious mind)

The more you practice the Safety Audit, the better you get at reading your own nervous system.

You learn:

	∙	Which areas genuinely feel unsafe vs. which ones just feel unfamiliar

	∙	Which times of day work for you

	∙	What level of precaution allows you to downregulate enough to notice beauty

This isn’t about “overcoming fear.” This is about building a relationship with your fear where it protects you without completely shutting down your curiosity.






The Stakes: What’s Lost When You Stop Looking

Let’s be honest about what’s at risk here.

The world is not equally safe for all bodies. That’s a fact. And ignoring that fact in the name of “adventure” is dangerous and irresponsible.

But here’s the other truth: If you let fear completely win - if you stop looking at anything, stop noticing anything, stop being curious about anything because the world is hostile - the Rut wins too.

And the Rut doesn’t care about your safety. The Rut just wants you numb.



The Dangerous Middle Ground

There’s a dangerous middle ground that many marginalized people end up in:

You’re too aware of danger to relax completely, but too exhausted by hypervigilance to keep fighting for moments of beauty.

So you stop going outside except when necessary. You stop looking around when you do go out. You keep your head down, your headphones in, your eyes on your phone. You get from Point A to Point B and you notice nothing.

You survive. But you don’t live.

And the Rut loves this. Because you’re doing its job for it. You’re keeping yourself numb, keeping yourself small, keeping yourself disconnected from the world.

Not because you’re weak. Because you’re exhausted from being vigilant.




What the Rut Wants You to Believe

The Rut whispers:

	∙	“The world isn’t safe for you, so don’t bother looking.”

	∙	“Curiosity is a luxury for people with privilege.”

	∙	“You don’t get to notice beauty - you’re too busy surviving.”

And if you believe that, you’ve handed the Rut exactly what it wants: your attention, your aliveness, your connection to the world.

This chapter is about refusing that binary.

You refuse to pretend the world is safe when it isn’t.

AND you refuse to let danger consume 100% of your awareness 100% of the time.

You acknowledge: “Yes, I need to be careful. AND I’m allowed to notice the sky while I’m being careful.”

You’re allowed to:

	∙	Walk to the corner during daylight and count front doors

	∙	Sit on your porch and watch the wind move through the trees

	∙	Look out the window and notice how the light changes throughout the day

	∙	Use Google Earth to explore places your body can’t safely go

You’re allowed to steal moments of curiosity from the cracks between survival tasks.

Not because you’re naive. Because you’re refusing to give the Rut everything.

The tragedy isn’t that you have to be hypervigilant. The tragedy is if hypervigilance becomes so totalizing that you forget you’re allowed to look at the moon.

You don’t need to risk your safety to notice beauty. You just need to find the moments when your nervous system says “safe enough” and trust those moments when they come.

The Compass doesn’t ask you to be reckless. It asks you to be awake within the bounds of what keeps you alive.

That’s the line. Stay on the safe side of it. But don’t let the line become a wall that blocks out everything.

You deserve to be curious. You deserve to notice the world. Not after you’re safe. Not when the world changes. Now. Today. From wherever you are.

You don’t need the world to be safe. You just need to find your Safe Zone and look closely at what’s there.





That’s the safety constraint. But there’s one more category of barrier that doesn’t show up on a map or depend on your demographics. It’s not about where you can go or what the world does to you. It’s about how your brain processes the world itself, and how the world wasn’t designed for the way your brain works.

If you’re neurodivergent, the barriers aren’t just external. They’re internal - but that doesn’t make them any less real.

The Compass says “just go for a walk.” Your brain says “I’ve been trying to stand up for twenty minutes and my body won’t cooperate.”

The Compass says “engage your senses.” Your brain says “I’m already drowning in sensory input and you want me to notice more?”

The Compass says “write the Souvenir when you get home.” Your brain says “I forgot the entire adventure three minutes after it ended.”

These aren’t character flaws. These are neurological differences. And they require different tools.

So let’s talk about what happens when the constraint isn’t the territory, it’s the brain trying to navigate it.








Chapter 11C Summary: The Safe Compass


What We Learned:

Safety Trumps Scenery: The Wonder Compass is a tool for flourishing, not survival. If your nervous system detects a threat (bad vibe, dark street, hostility), you can’t feel awe because your amygdala overrides your curiosity. If it feels unsafe, leave. That’s the rule.

The Privilege of Wandering: "Getting lost" is dangerous advice for marginalized groups (women, POC, queer individuals) in hostile environments. We replaced "wandering" with "The Safe Zone."

Social Camouflage: Using props (phones, headphones, notebooks) or specific behaviors ("The Waiting Stance") allows you to observe the world without drawing unwanted attention.

The Porch Radius: If the world is too chaotic, shrink the territory. Your front porch or balcony is a valid "wilderness" where you control the boundaries.

Digital Wonder as Refuge: When physical safety is compromised, digital exploration (Google Earth, games) is a legitimate and neurologically effective way to access novelty without risk.



Quick Win:

The Vibe Check.

Go to a window or step onto your porch/balcony (only if safe).

Look at the street. Don't look for beauty. Look for energy. Is the street calm? Busy? Hostile? Welcoming?

Identify one "Safety Anchor" - a streetlight, a neighbor's house you trust, a tree that feels sturdy.

Say to yourself: "That [object] is safe."

You’ve just mapped your environment for safety, which is the prerequisite for wonder. You don't have to go out there. Just knowing where the safe spots are calms the nervous system.
