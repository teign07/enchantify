Chapter 18: Around Town




You know your town. You know exactly how long the red light takes at the big intersection. You know how the grocery carts wheels squeak. You know the exact number of steps from your front door to your mailbox.

You know it so well that you’ve stopped living in it.

But the mailbox stands at the end of your driveway, mouth open all day and all night. It gets stuffed with junk and bills and catalogs it never asked for, holding everything until you finally show up to empty it out. The stop sign at the corner has been holding up its hand for twenty years, and not once has it lowered it. The world hasn’t gone silent - you’ve just stopped listening.

When you run errands or do chores, you’re essentially "fast-traveling" in a video game. You teleport from "House" to "Store" to "Home." The space in between, the air, the light, the ground, the people - is rendered by your brain as a low-resolution gray blur.

You’ve driven past the same coffee shop 500 times without ever wondering who painted that mural on the side. You’ve walked by your neighbor’s garden for three years without noticing they grow roses. The world has become wallpaper, and you’ve become a ghost walking through it.

This is like a sort of Trance of the Familiar. It’s efficient, but it’s deadening. It reinforces the feeling that your life is small, contained, and known.

But here’s the truth: The most boring places in your life are actually the most dense with wonder, if you know how to adjust your focal length.

In the introduction to this book, I made you a promise. I told you I would try to teach you how to find wonder in a walk to the mailbox and in a grocery store parking lot.

In this chapter, we’re going to cash that check.

We’re going to take the three dullest moments of your week - walking down the driveway, shopping for soap, and sitting in a car - and turn them into an expedition. We aren't going to visit new places; we’re going to visit the old places with a new lens.




Level 1: The Infinite Driveway (The Mailbox Walk) The Symptom: The "Autopilot Shuffle"

The Feeling: "I’m just getting the mail. I’m just taking out the trash. It’s a chore."

We tend to treat the space between our front door and the street as "dead space." It’s just distance to be crossed. But biologically, that thirty feet of concrete or grass is a teeming, violent, beautiful ecosystem.

To find wonder here, we have to change the Scale. We have to stop being a Commuter and start being a Micro-Biologist.


The Recipe (N-E-S-W) NORTH = NOTICE (The Spark)
The spark is to realize that you are a giant walking over a universe.

Spark: "I wonder how much life is happening in the one foot of grass next to the driveway?"

EAST = EMBARK (The Plan)

Destination: The Mailbox (or the Dumpster/End of the Driveway).

Delight: Leave your phone inside. The delight here is "Hands-Free Freedom."

Definition: "I won’t return to the house until I’ve found something that’s alive."



SOUTH = SENSE (The Playful Mission)

The Mission: The 10-Second Halt.

Walk halfway to the mailbox. Then, stop. Complete stillness.

Don’t look at the mailbox. Look down.

Imagine you’re looking at a National Geographic documentary, but the camera is pointed at your lawn.

The Jungle: Crouch down (yes, do it). Look at the grass stems. Notice how they aren't just green; they’re yellow, brown, purple at the base. They’re a forest. Each blade of grass is a tiny tower, holding itself up against gravity. Some of them are bent, leaning on each other like they gave up halfway. They’ve been growing all week, drinking rain, stretching toward sun. They want light. That’s all they know how to want.

The Residents: Find an ant. That ant has a destination and it’s not messing around. It’s been hauling and navigating all day, following a chemical map you can’t see, moving with more certainty than you had driving here this morning. Find a beetle. A spider. Don't just glance; watch it. Where is it going? It has a mission. It’s hunting, or building, or fleeing. You’re witnessing a drama as real as any on TV, happening right by your left sneaker.

The Survivor: Find a weed growing through a crack in the asphalt. Touch it. Feel the grit of the concrete and the softness of the leaf. Acknowledge the sheer biological force required to burst through stone. This weed isn’t supposed to be here. The asphalt tried to kill it. The heat, the lack of soil, the weight of tires - none of it worked. The weed just kept growing, stubborn and green, refusing to die. It’s been fighting this battle for weeks, and so far it’s winning. It doesn’t know it’s a weed. It doesn’t know the asphalt is trying to kill it. It just knows one thing: grow toward light. That’s the only instruction it’s ever had, and it’s been following it with a kind of dumb, perfect faith.


WEST = WRITE (The Souvenir)

Capture the vitality.

Example: "Watched a red ant carry a crumb twice its size across the Mount Everest of my driveway cracks; the sheer effort of it made me tired."

Example: "I realized the dandelion growing out of the drainpipe is working harder than I have all week."

Why This Works:

It breaks the "Functionality" of the walk. The walk is no longer about the mail (the utility). It’s about the life (the wonder). You realize that you aren't alone on your property; you’re  cohabitating with millions of tiny lives.


You’ve cracked the code on your driveway, turning 30 feet of concrete into a safari. But what about the places that feel genuinely soulless? The fluorescent-lit aisles where everything is designed to make you buy things you don’t need?




Level 2: The Typography Hunter (Inside the Store) 

The Symptom: The "Big Box" Numbness.

The Feeling: "I hate this place. It’s loud, bright, and soulless."

You’ve made it to the store. The lights are humming. The music is designed to make you buy things. Your brain wants to shut down and go into "Zombie Shopper" mode.

To fight this, you need to stop being a "Consumer" and start being a "Critic."

Every single box, can, and bag in this store was designed by a human being. Someone chose that color. Someone chose that font. Someone wrote that weird description on the back of the shampoo bottle. When you realize this, the store stops being a monolith and starts being a gallery of human choices.


The Recipe (N-E-S-W) 


NORTH = NOTICE (The Spark)

The spark is judgment. (Yes, you’re allowed to be judgmental here).

Spark: "I wonder who decided that this was the right font for this box of crackers?"


EAST = EMBARK (The Plan)

Destination: Aisle 4 (or wherever the boring stuﬀ is). The fluorescent lights overhead have been humming the same note all day, a flat buzz that gets inside your skull if you let it. They’re bright and tired at the same time, working overtime to make everything look the same shade of nowhere.

Delight: A podcast in one earbud (leaving the other ear open for safety). This creates a cinematic soundtrack for your mundane shop.

Definition: "I will play the game until I reach the checkout." The shopping cart you grabbed has a squeaky wheel - front left.  It’s been rolling through this store for months with that same complaint, turning left when you want to go straight, announcing your location to everyone in the cereal aisle.


SOUTH = SENSE (The Playful Mission)

The Mission: The Design Critic.

Your job is to find the specific design choices in the aisle.

The "Bad" Font: Find a product that looks like it hasn't changed its packaging since 1990. (Look for generic brands, cleaning supplies, or obscure condiments). Why does it look like that? Does it make you trust it more or less?

The Color Quest: Pick a rare color (like "Electric Purple" or "Safety Orange") and count how many times it appears in the cereal aisle vs. the detergent aisle. That neon purple detergent bottle has been screaming at you from the shelf for months, begging to be noticed. It’s been sitting there between the Tide and the Gain, desperate and garish, hoping someone - anyone - will think it’s special enough to take home.

The "Copywriter" Hunt: Read the back of a shampoo bottle or a box of tea. Someone was paid to write that flowery description. Critique it. Is it convincing? Is it ridiculous? That copywriter was sitting at a desk somewhere, probably tired, probably caffeinated, trying to make ‘lavender essence’ sound like a spiritual experience. They picked those words. They debated whether to say ‘calming’ or ‘soothing.’ The bottle has been carrying their sentences around the country for months now, quietly hauling those words from warehouse to truck to shelf, delivering this tiny piece of someone’s Wednesday afternoon to strangers in fluorescent aisles. And now you’re holding it. You’re the reason that sentence exists today. You picked it up. You read it. For just a second, that tired copywriter’s work landed. You’re standing in a Walmart, and you just completed a transaction that had nothing to do with money.


WEST = WRITE (The Souvenir)

Capture the absurdity.

Example: "The 'Relaxing Lavender' detergent bottle is a screaming neon purple that makes me feel anxious just looking at it."

Example: “Found a box of tea that promised ‘Zen Tranquility’ in Comic Sans font - the designer was either a genius or gave up entirely; I decided to believe it was genius, and it made me laugh out loud in Aisle 4.”

Why This Works:

It turns the "soulless" environment into a museum of commerce. You stop being a victim of the marketing and start being a student of it. You’re  engaged, you’re critical, and you’re  awake.




You’ve survived the store, turned the aisles into a gallery, the packaging into art. Now you walk outside. You load the groceries. And you’re about to make the biggest mistake: starting the engine and leaving immediately. Because you’re standing in the middle of an accidental observatory.





Level 3: The Asphalt Observatory (The Parking Lot) 

The Symptom: The "Getaway Driver" Anxiety.

The Feeling: "I need to load the car and get out of here." 

You survived the shop. Now you’re in the parking lot. It smells like exhaust. It’s ugly.

But it has a secret feature: It is the widest open space in your town. Because parking lots have no trees and low buildings, they are accidental observatories. While everyone else is looking at fenders and shopping carts, the biggest show on earth is happening overhead.


The Recipe (N-E-S-W) 


NORTH = NOTICE (The Spark)

The spark is "Contrast." We’re going to look for the collision between the ugly (asphalt) and the sublime (the atmosphere).

Spark: "I wonder what the sky is doing right now while everyone is looking at their receipts?"


EAST = EMBARK (The Plan)

Destination: The Driver’s Seat.

Delight: The car seat itself. You”ve loaded the groceries. You’re sitting down. Do not start the engine.

Definition: "I will sit here for 60 seconds before I put the key in the ignition."


SOUTH = SENSE (The Playful Mission)

The Mission: The Windshield Cinema.

Lean your seat back. Your windshield is a frame. It crops out the cars, the pavement, and the store. It leaves only the sky.

The Cloud Zoo: Watch the clouds. If they’re moving fast, there’s a massive wind current up there that you can't feel down here. You’re watching physics in action. Those clouds have traveled hundreds of miles to be here right now, above this Kroger, above your windshield. They’re not from here - they’re just passing through, like you. In ten minutes they’ll be gone, over the next town, and you’ll never see these exact clouds again.

The Light Show: If it’s evening, watch the "Gradient." Find the exact line where the blue fades into pink. That color didn't exist ten minutes ago.

The Bird Commute: Look for birds. Crows, seagulls, hawks. They love parking lot thermals. Watch them glide. They’re surfing invisible waves of heat rising oﬀ the blacktop. That crow has been working the parking lot all day, hunting for dropped fries and sandwich crusts. It knows this place better than you do - it knows which cars leave food, which times bring the most suckers with McDonald’s bags, which puddles are good for bathing. It’s been making a living here while you were inside debating between lavender and eucalyptus.

The “Nothing” Discovery: If the sky is gray, flat, and “boring,” that’s fine. Even the gray sky is doing something. It’s holding water, maybe, or light that’s trying to break through. It’s not dead, it’s just quiet. It’s been up there all day doing sky work - holding moisture, blocking sun, threatening rain it might not deliver, moving so slowly you can’t see it happening until it’s already somewhere else. Your mission becomes: “How many shades of gray can I actually see?” You’ll realize there’s no such thing as “just gray” - there’s steel, dove, ash, fog, smoke. Even the boring sky is actually 30 colors pretending to be one.

The Parking Lot Sound:

Roll down your window. Don’t listen for anything specific, just let the sound come in. The hum of the highway a mile away. A cart rattling. A car door slamming three rows over. A crow cawing.

All of it is arriving at your ear at the speed of sound - about 767 miles per hour. That crow’s call left its throat a fraction of a second ago and traveled through the air to find you. The sound is gone now. It will never exist again. You were the only person in this exact spot who heard that exact sound. The parking lot has been catching and throwing sounds all day - doors slamming, engines revving, cart wheels squeaking - bouncing them off the asphalt and sending them out into the air where they disappear forever.


WEST = WRITE (The Souvenir)

Capture the vastness.

Example: "Sat in the Kroger lot and watched a storm front roll in; for a minute, the car felt like a spaceship cockpit facing the edge of the world."

Why This Works:

It utilizes "Shadow Wonder." The parking lot is objectively ugly. The sky is objectively beautiful. By framing them together, you create a moment of profound contrast. You realize that even in the middle of your errands, you’re sitting on a planet spinning through space. You aren't just a shopper; you’re an astronaut in a Honda Civic.


Now, I know what some of you are thinking: ‘I’m not crouching down in my driveway. I’m not staring at the sky in a Walmart parking lot. People will think I’m insane, or worse, they’ll think I’m dangerous.’ Fair. Let’s talk about camouflage.

The Stealth Playful Missions from Chapter 8A are relevant to this discussion, but here’s a bonus technique:

The "Earbud" Prop

Wearing headphones signals "I am in my own world." It discourages people from interrupting you, giving you a bubble of privacy in a public space.

You’ve turned the grocery aisle into an art gallery.

You’ve turned the parking lot into an observatory. You’ve proven that the ‘boring’ parts of your day were never boring - you were just moving too fast to see them.

Your Guide to Everyday Expeditions




Chapter 18 Summary

What We Learned:

The Trance of the Familiar: We stop seeing our own towns because we know them too well. We treat errands like "Fast Travel" in a video game - teleporting from point A to point B without seeing the world in between.

The Solution: You don't need a new location; you need a new focal length.

Three Levels of Town Wonder:

Level 1: The Driveway (Scale): Shift from Commuter to Micro-Biologist. The cracks in your driveway contain a violent, beautiful ecosystem if you're willing to crouch down to see it.

Level 2: The Store (Judgment): Shift from Zombie Consumer to Design Critic. Every boring box was designed by a human. Critique the fonts, the colors, and the absurdity of the marketing. It's not just a store; it's a gallery of human choices.

Level 3: The Parking Lot (Contrast): Shift from Driver to Astronaut. The parking lot is an accidental observatory. Use your windshield to frame the sky and ignore the asphalt.

Stealth Mode: If you're feeling self-conscious stopping to look at things, use "The Waiting Ruse" (check your watch) or "The Photographer Shield" (hold up your phone) to create social camouflage.



Town Compass (N-E-S-W):

North: Notice the details you usually ignore. "I wonder who designed this cereal box?" / "I wonder what's living in that patch of grass?"

East: Embark with a restriction. Leave the phone inside for the driveway walk; use earbuds for a cinematic soundtrack in the store.

South: Sense the environment using a specific mission (The 10-Second Halt, The Cloud Zoo, The Typography Hunt).

West: Write a Souvenir that captures the vitality (nature), the absurdity (commerce), or the vastness (sky).

Quick Win:
 The Threshold Pause.
 The very next time you walk out your front door - whether it's to check the mail, walk the dog, or get in your car - stop. Don't take a step oﬀ your porch or stoop for ten full seconds. Look down at the ground near your feet. Find one single thing that's alive (an ant, a weed, a spiderweb). That ant has been working all morning, hauling and hunting. The weed has been growing all week, pushing up through the crack. The spiderweb was built overnight while you slept, thin and sticky and positioned perfectly to catch whatever flies by for breakfast. 

You've just broken the "Fast Travel" spell.
