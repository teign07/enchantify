Chapter 9: West = Write




There’s a specific tragedy that happens to almost all of us.

You have a genuinely good Saturday. You go to the park, or you visit a new town, or you just have a really great cup of tea on the porch. You feel light. You feel human.

Then comes Sunday night. The "Scaries" set in. You start thinking about the meeting on Monday morning. You start worrying about the grocery list. By Tuesday afternoon, you’re sitting at your desk or standing in your kitchen, and that beautiful Saturday feels like it happened to someone else. You try to remember what you did. You know you went somewhere. You can almost see the shape of it, like trying to remember a dream three seconds after waking. There was… a park? Or a shop? The edges are already dissolving. By Wednesday, it’ll be gone completely, like it never happened at all. It feels like a dream you had three years ago. The feeling is gone. The texture of the joy has evaporated. The grayness has rushed back in to fill the space, and you think, Did I even do anything this weekend?

This is the Emotional Amnesia we discussed earlier. And it’s not your fault. It’s your biology.

Here’s how brutal the biology actually is: In 1885, German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus discovered what’s now called the Forgetting Curve. He found that within 20 minutes of an experience, you’ve already lost 40% of the details. Within 24 hours, you’ve lost two-thirds. By the end of a week, 75% is gone.

That beautiful Saturday you had? By Tuesday afternoon, your brain has already deleted most of it. The smell of the coffee, the way the light looked, the specific joke that made you laugh - gone. Not because you’re broken. Because your brain is running an efficiency algorithm: ‘If this didn’t threaten survival, don’t waste storage space on it.’

The brain treats joy like RAM (temporary memory) and treats danger like a hard drive (permanent storage). Unless you manually hit ‘Save,’ the good stuff gets overwritten by the next day’s stress.

This is why people can remember every detail of a car accident from 20 years ago but can’t remember what they did last Saturday. The traumatic memory got saved automatically. The joyful memory? You had to save it yourself. And you didn’t know how.

We talked about how psychologists have a concept called the Negativity Bias. To keep our ancestors alive, the human brain evolved to treat danger and stress like Velcro, but to treat joy and peace like Teflon.

If you touch a hot stove, your brain records that data instantly and forever. Danger. Do not touch. It prioritizes that memory because it’s essential for survival.

But if you smell a beautiful rose? Your brain thinks, That’s nice, but it won’t save my life. So it doesn't bother to hit the "Save" button. It lets the moment slide right oﬀ the Teflon surface of your short-term memory.

The Rut thrives on this amnesia. The Rut wants you to believe that your life is nothing but a string of obligations interrupted by sleep. It wants you to forget the magic.

To defeat the Rut, we have to hack the filing system. We have to manually tell the brain: “Hey! This moment right here? This is important. Do not delete this.”

We do that by heading West. We do that by Writing.





The Problem with "Dear Diary"

Wait!

When I say "Write," I can feel you tense up.

I know what you’re thinking. You’re picturing a leather-bound journal with blank, judgmental pages. You’re thinking about "Morning Pages" or gratitude lists. You’re thinking, “I don’t have time to write a page a day. I’m not a writer. This feels like homework.”

The Caveman loves those excuses. He loves it when you think writing has to be a Big Deal, because then you won’t do it.

So, let’s be clear: We are not journaling. We are not processing our trauma. We aren’t writing a novel.

We are stealing.

We’re stealing a moment from time before it dissolves.

Here’s how.





The Tool: The One-Sentence Souvenir

The tool for West is incredibly specific. It is a constraint designed to lower the barrier to entry so far that you can’t trip over it.

You’re going to write One Sentence.

Not a paragraph. Not a page. One sentence.

Why one? Because anyone can write one sentence while waiting for the kettle to boil, or in the notes app of your phone while sitting in the car. It requires zero activation energy.

But what do you write?

You write the single best moment.

You scan the entire adventure - whether it was a trip to Quebec or just ten minutes in your backyard - and you ask yourself: What was the highlight? What was the moment that felt the most like ‘me’?


The "Specific is Terrific" Rule

There’s only one way to maybe mess this up, and that’s to be so vague it won’t trigger memories.

If you write, “We went to the diner and it was fun,” you haven’t really been specific enough to lock the memory in. That’s a label. That’s the autopilot talking. That sentence contains no sensory data, so your brain won’t retain it.

But you don't have to be a poet.

If your sentence is "The rock was smooth and I liked it," that counts; smooth is sensory and you don’t find a rock you like every day. If your sentence is "Saw a weird dog," that also counts; weird is weird, it sticks out. You aren't writing for an audience. You're writing for your future self, who will know exactly what "weird dog" means.

The Souvenir is best when it captures a sensory detail. It doesn't have to be complicated or flowery. It doesn't have to be smart. It just has to be specific.

Here’s how the "Souvenir" looks across the different adventures we’ve talked about in this book.


At Home

The Context: You spent 20 minutes doing a diamond painting or knitting while listening to a podcast.

Vague (Don't do this): "I worked on my craft; it was relaxing." (This sentence will be deleted by your brain within 48 hours. It has no hook, no texture, nothing for your neurons to grab onto.)

Specific (Do this): "The tiny 'click' sound of the pink gem sticking to the canvas scratched an itch in my brain I didn't know I had."


In the Kitchen

The Context: You cooked a simple pasta dish, but you paid attention to the smells.

Vague: "Dinner was good." (Your brain files this under “dinner” and throws away the key. There’s nothing specific enough to survive.)

Specific: "The smell of the garlic hitting the hot oil made the whole kitchen feel safe and warm, like my grandmother’s house."


Around Town

The Context: You went to the grocery store, but you looked for "Blue things" (your Playful Mission).

Vague: "Got groceries and saw some blue stuﬀ." (This is a label, not a memory. Labels evaporate.)

Specific: "I saw a little girl in a blue tutu dancing in the cereal aisle, and she didn't care who was watching." (The dancing girl in the tutu? That stays forever.)


In Nature

The Context: You sat on your back porch for ten minutes with your coffee.

Vague: "Nice bird."

Specific: "The blue jay was so bright against the gray sky it looked like a piece of candy."


The Road Trip

The Context: You drove an hour to a bakery, but it was closed. You pivoted and ate snacks in the car.

Vague: "Bakery was closed, Bummer."

Specific: "Sitting in the car eating Funyuns because the bakery was closed was actually the funniest part of the week - we laughed until we cried."


Here’s the trick: Close your eyes and replay the moment. What’s the first thing that comes back? Not the label (“bird,” “food,” “diner”), but the image. The blue against gray. The butter smell. The kid’s tutu. That’s your detail. That’s what your brain actually recorded. Write that.

See the difference? You aren't trying to win a Pulitzer Prize. You’re just trying to freeze-frame the texture of the feeling so you can keep it.

Why be specific? It helps retain the memory, but also because generalities are insults. To say "The flower was pretty" is lazy. To say "The flower looked like a bruised peach" is an act of attention. When you hunt for the specific word, you’re honoring the specific reality of that flower. You’re telling it: "I see you exactly as you are."






Level 2: The Alchemist’s Pen

Six months from now, you’re going to open your souvenir jar on a bad day. You’ll pull out a folded note that says “Had coffee on the porch, it was nice.” And you’ll feel nothing. Just words. You’ll almost throw it away.

Then you’ll pull out another one. It says: “The coffee tasted like a slow Sunday and the steam reached up toward the fog like it was trying to go home.”

And you’re back on the porch. You can feel the mug warming both palms. You can see the fog sitting in the yard like it moved in overnight. One sentence was a receipt. The other was a door. You walked right through it and sat back down in that morning.

Both sentences describe the same moment. The difference is that the second one is alive. And you can learn to write sentences like that. It’s not talent. It’s two simple tricks.


Technique A: Cross the Wires

Your brain keeps the senses in separate lanes. Sight here. Sound there. Taste over there. Neat, efficient, forgettable. That’s the Caveman filing system. Label it, box it, move on.

But when you describe one sense using the language of another sense, the Caveman can’t file it. There’s no box for “the fire sounded orange.” He has to actually stop and process it. And that confusion is exactly what burns the memory in deep.

Children do this naturally. “That song is red.” “This rock tastes angry. (Kids, right?)” They’re not being cute. That’s what full-spectrum perception actually sounds like before adults teach them to sort everything into separate drawers.

David Abram argues in The Spell of the Sensuous that our ancestors perceived this way too, felt the world with their eyes, heard it with their skin. The labeling came later. The boxes came later. You’re just taking the lids back off.

Here’s what it looks like:

“The wind was cold” → “The wind tasted like a rusted nail.”

“The fire was warm and crackly” → “The fire sounded orange.”

“The lemon was sour” → “The lemon tasted like a trumpet.”

“The library was quiet” → “The library was so quiet it was soft, like the whole room was lined in felt.”

“The rain smelled fresh” → “The rain smelled green and silver, like the sky had been keeping a secret all day.”

The first version in each pair is accurate. The second is alive. And six months from now, “the rain smelled green and silver” will put you right back on that sidewalk with your hood up, grinning. “The rain smelled fresh” will do nothing for you. It’s already gone.

You don’t have to be good at this. “The soup smelled loud” is a perfectly ridiculous sentence that will make you laugh when you read it in January - and laughing, you’ll remember the whole kitchen. The crossed wire is the hook. The memory hangs from it.





Technique B: Give the World the Pen

Most souvenirs put you at the center of the sentence.

“I saw the mountain.”

“I found a stone.”

“I sat on the bench.”

“I ate the pie.”

Fine. Accurate. Dead on the page.

Now flip the grammar. Make the world the one doing something. Give the object the verb. Let it act on you instead of the other way around.


“I saw the mountain” → “The mountain grabbed me by the chin and tilted my head up.”

“I found a stone” → “The stone had been in that river for a thousand years, getting ready, and I was the one who finally showed up.”

“I sat on the bench” → “The bench caught my weight like it had been saving that spot all afternoon.”

“I ate the pie” → “The pie had been sitting in the case since 6 AM watching people pick chocolate, and I was the one who finally said ‘you’.”


When you write “the sun hit my face,” that’s physics.

When you write “the sun found me through the clouds,” that’s a relationship.

That’s the shift. You go from recording the world like a security camera to writing about it like someone who’s in a conversation with it. The mountain isn’t scenery. It demanded something from you. The bench isn’t furniture. It did you a favor. The pie was waiting. You arrived.



The Alchemist’s Formula

Once both techniques feel natural, combine them. Cross a sense and let the object act in the same sentence.

“The old book smelled like vanilla and whispered when I turned the page.”

“The dock groaned under my weight and the lake smelled like cold iron.”

“The traffic was thick and gray but the radio fought through it with a song that tasted like July.”

You aren’t writing poetry. You’re writing proof that the world talked to you and you were awake enough to hear it.



Permission Slip

These techniques are upgrades, not requirements. “The rock was smooth and I liked it” is still a perfect souvenir. On tired days, capture the rock and move on.

But on the days when you’ve got a little more energy, when the moment feels bigger than a label can hold, cross a wire. Give the object a voice. Let the sentence breathe.

You’ll know it’s working when your souvenirs stop reading like a logbook and start reading like tiny spells. Like you’re not just remembering your life, but casting it in amber.

And on some gray Tuesday in November, you’ll pull a note from the jar that says “the fire sounded orange,” and you won’t just remember the fire.

You’ll feel warm.








However, a final permission slip:

If you’re too tired for details, or if a simple fact is all you have, write it anyway. "I went outside" is better than a blank page. The goal is to capture the diamond; but if you can only capture a rock, capture the rock. The habit matters more than the poetry.



That’s the technique. Now let me show you what’s actually happening when you write these sentences, because it’s more profound than just “keeping memories.”



Hack Your Brain

The Theory of Ink (Attention is Authorship)

Imagine you’re 80 years old. Someone hands you the book of your life. You open it, excited to relive your days. But page after page is marked ‘Ibid’ - Latin for ‘see previous.’ Same as before. Nothing new. The Tuesday you can’t remember looks just like the Tuesday before it, which looks just like the one before that. Entire years are just one line: ‘See 2024.’

But then, scattered throughout, you find pages with ink. Real ink. ‘The blue jay like candy.’ ‘The kid dancing in the cereal aisle.’ ‘Laughing over Funyuns.’ Those pages are alive. Those are the pages you’ll read over and over.

Here’s the truth: Your brain doesn’t record every day. It records the days you notice. Attention is the ink.

I want you to think of your life as a book. A literal, bound volume of 30,000 days.

When you’re in the Rut, you assume that the book is writing itself. You assume that because you were alive on a Thursday, the Thursday was recorded.

But neuroscience tells us that’s not true.

Your brain is an efficiency machine, not a biographer. If a day is repetitive, if it looks like every other day, your brain doesn't write a new page. It marks the page "Ibid" - see previous. It leaves the page blank.

This is why ten years can disappear in a blink. You weren't writing. You were skimming.
Here’s the radical truth: Attention is the ink.

You don’t write the story of your life with a pen. You write it with your eyes, your hands, your tongue.

Whatever you pay attention to is what gets written in the book.

If you pay attention to the worry, you write a tragedy.

If you pay attention to the lack, you write a grievance.

If you pay attention to the phone, you write nothing at all. You leave the page blank.

But when you use the Compass - when you Notice (North) and Sense (South) - you’re dipping the quill into the inkwell. You’re choosing to put text on the page.

And when you Write the Souvenir (West), you’re letting the ink dry.

You’re proving that this day wasn't just a placeholder between birth and death. You’re proving that you were here, you were awake, and you saw something. Think of it this way: If you don’t write it down, the moment is written in disappearing ink. It looks solid for a few hours - you can still see it, still feel it - but by morning, the page is blank again. Writing is how you make the ink permanent. It’s how you prove to yourself that today wasn’t just another “Ibid.” Today had a sentence that belonged only to you.

You’re switching from being the Reader of your life to being the Author.

When you open a notebook or a blank digital page, you aren't just facing emptiness. You’re facing a hungry space. The page wants your story. It’s tired of staying blank. When you write a sentence about the smell of rain, you’re feeding the page. You’re giving it a purpose. You’re turning pulp and pixels into a vessel for your life.

But there’s one thing that’ll sabotage this entire process. And it’s sitting in your pocket right now, buzzing with notifications.




The Trap: Capture vs. Broadcast

Now, we need to address the elephant in your pocket. The smartphone.

When you’re out there - finding the weird mailbox, sitting in the "Shadow Wonder" diner, or staring at the moss - you’re going to feel an impulse. It’s a modern, biological reflex almost as strong as the urge to sneeze.

You’ll want to take a picture and post it to Instagram/Facebook/TikTok immediately. You’ll want to caption it: "Living my best life!" or "Found this cool spot!"

Don’t do it.

I’m not telling you to be a Luddite. I’m not telling you to delete your accounts. I’m telling you that if you post that picture while you’re still inside the adventure, you’ve killed the Wonder. Think about the last vacation photo you posted. Right now, without looking, try to remember: What did the air feel like? What did it smell like? You can’t remember, can you? But you can remember exactly how many likes it got. That’s what happens when you broadcast before you absorb. The likes replace the memory.

Here’s why: The human brain has two distinct modes for dealing with information.

Input Mode (Sensing): "I am feeling this sun on my face. I am smelling the rain."

Output Mode (Performing): "How does this look to others? Is the lighting right? What is a witty caption?"

You can’t be in Input and Output mode at the same time.

The exact second you decide to "Share" the moment, your brain stops experiencing the moment and starts packaging it. You stop being the Protagonist of your life and you become the Documentarian. You step out of the frame to look at the frame.

The dopamine shifts, too. You switch from the internal satisfaction of discovery (Serotonin/ Wonder) to the anxious anticipation of validation (External Dopamine). Instead of looking at the tree, you’1re looking at your screen, waiting for the first "Like" to roll in.

You’ve leaked all the energy out of the container. It’s like trying to taste wine while describing it to a critic. You’re so busy thinking about how to describe it that you never actually taste it. The wine becomes a concept instead of an experience. The moment you start composing the caption, the tree stops being your tree and becomes their tree - and you’re just the photographer who happened to be there.


The Rule: The 24-Hour Embargo

To protect your Souvenir, you need to institute a strict law: The 24-Hour Embargo. Here’s the protocol:

Capture is Okay: Go ahead. Take the photo. Snap the weird mailbox. Taking a photo is fine; it helps you see details.

Write the Souvenir: Write your one sentence in your notebook or your phone notes.

The Freeze: Put the phone away. Don’t open social media.

The Wait: You’re forbidden from posting that image for 24 hours.

Let the Concrete Cure

Think of your memory like wet concrete. When you first experience something, the memory is soft and malleable. If you "walk on it" immediately by inviting 500 followers to comment on it, the impression gets messy. You start remembering the comments ("Wow, cool!") rather than the feeling (The smell of the rain).

By waiting 24 hours, you let the concrete harden. You allow the memory to become fully yours before you give it to them.


The Secret Power

There’s a profound power in having a secret. Walking around for a day knowing you saw something beautiful, and knowing that nobody else knows you saw it, builds a specific kind of internal pressure. It makes the memory stickier. It reinforces the idea that you’re doing this for you, not for an audience.

Carrying a secret wonder is a physical sensation. It feels like walking around with a glowing ember in your pocket. You know it’s there. It warms you. The rest of the world sees a normal person walking down the street, but you know you’re carrying a piece of magic that hasn't been diluted by a single "Like." It belongs entirely to you.

Tomorrow at work, someone will complain about the weather or the traffic. And you’ll smile, just a little, because you know something they don’t. You saw the light hit the water in a way that looked like hammered gold. You tasted a croissant that was better than it had any right to be. The secret sits in your chest like a smooth stone in your pocket. Nobody knows it’s there but you.

So, take the picture. Write the line. But keep it in the vault until tomorrow.

If, after 24 hours, you still want to post it? Go ahead.

So that’s when to share. But there’s one more practical question: Where do you keep these sentences? What’s the actual container?


Protect Your Memories



Choose Your Vessel

The Caveman will try one last trick. He’ll ask, "Where do I put this sentence? If I lose it, what's the point?"

You need a Vessel. But keep it simple. Don’t go buy a $40 journal that you’re too afraid to ruin with bad handwriting. Unless you want to!

The Digital Vessel: Open the 'Notes' app on your phone. Create a folder called 'Souvenirs.' Your phone has been serving you distractions all day - now give it something worth holding. Dictate the sentence while you drive home. It takes four seconds.

The Analog Vessel: Get a mason jar and a stack of post-it notes. Write the sentence, fold it up, and drop it in the jar.

The Social Vessel: Text the sentence to your partner or a friend. The text thread becomes the logbook.

The container doesn't matter. What matters is that you hit "Save."

That jar of notes isn't just paper. It’s a battery. It’s stored sunlight. When you unscrew the lid, the paper smells faintly like the pen ink and the coffee you were drinking when you wrote them. Each folded note is a tiny time capsule. Some of them are from summer - you can tell by the handwriting, looser and slower. Some are from hard days - you can see where you pressed too hard with the pen. But all of them are proof. Proof that you were awake. Proof that you chose to look. On the dark days, when the Rut’s whispering that nothing matters, you can unscrew the lid and let the light out. You can recharge your soul on the surplus joy of your past self.

But don’t just take my word for it. Let me show you the science behind why one sentence can change your brain.



The Science: Why This Actually Works

The data on this is overwhelming. It’s not just a nice idea; it’s cognitive rewiring.


     1. The "Peak-End" Rule

Nobel Prize-winning psychologist Daniel Kahneman discovered that our brains don’t remember an experience as a whole. We remember it based on the "Peak" (the most intense moment) and the "End." By writing down your Souvenir at the end of the adventure, you’re actively curating the "End." You’re forcing your brain to define the experience by its best moment, ensuring that the positive memory wins out over the stress of the drive home.

That’s the immediate effect, shaping how you remember the adventure. But the benefits compound over time.


     2. The Forgetting Curve (The 24-Hour Window)

But there’s a deeper reason why writing matters, and it has to do with timing.

Remember the Forgetting Curve from earlier in this chapter? Here’s why timing matters: In the first 24 hours after an experience, you lose approximately 66% of the details. By day 7, you’ve lost 75%. By day 30, the memory is effectively gone unless it’s been reinforced.

This is why the 24-Hour Embargo isn’t just about protecting the memory from social media - it’s about catching the memory before it evaporates.

When you write the Souvenir within 24 hours, you’re hitting the ‘Save’ button before the decay accelerates. You’re capturing the sensory details while they’re still accessible. Wait three days? Most of the texture is already gone. You’ll remember ‘we went somewhere’ but you won’t remember the smell of the rain or the specific shade of blue.

But here’s the magic: the act of writing doesn’t just capture the memory - it strengthens it.

This is called the Testing Effect. Research by Roediger and Karpicke (2006) shows that retrieving a memory (which is what you’re doing when you ask ‘What was the best part?’) strengthens the neural pathway more effectively than passively reviewing it.

When you write ‘The blue jay was so bright against the gray sky it looked like a piece of candy,’ you’re not just recording that image, you’re etching it deeper into your long-term storage.

You’re taking a fading photograph and turning it into a permanent engraving.

That’s why the Souvenir works. You’re not just fighting the Forgetting Curve - you’re reversing it.


     3. The Depression Antidote (The 35% Shift)

As we discussed earlier, in landmark studies by Dr. Martin Seligman, the father of Positive Psychology, participants were asked to write down "Three Good Things" that happened to them each day. It took less than five minutes.

The results were staggering. Participants showed significantly higher levels of happiness and lower levels of depressive symptoms for six months after the exercise. Just one week of writing changed their neural pathways.

And the mechanism isn’t just emotional, it’s neurological.


     4. Memory Consolidation

When you hunt for the right word - was the gem pink or purple? Was the chip salty or stale? - you’re forcing your neurons to fire repeatedly around that specific image. You’re moving the memory from short-term RAM to long-term storage. You’re manually applying the Velcro we talked about earlier.


But the most powerful use of these Souvenirs isn’t in the moment you write them. It’s weeks or months later, on the days when you need them most.





The "Bad Day" Protocol

Here’s the secret engine of the Compass. The "West" step doesn't just end the current adventure; it creates the fuel for the next one.

When you’re in the Rut, you don't go on adventures because you don't believe they’re worth the effort. Your brain tells you: "It’s just going to be tiring. It won't be fun. Stay on the couch."

But when you have a list of Souvenirs, you have The Bad Day Protocol.

Picture this: It is a rainy Monday in November. You’re tired. You feel like your life is just work and sleep. You feel the gray fog rolling in.

Open your phone. Open the jar. Read five sentences.

"The smell of garlic..."

"The blue jay like candy..."

"Laughing over Funyuns..."

Suddenly, you have Proof. You have undeniable, written evidence that your life isn’t just gray. You see that your life is actually speckled with gold, but you had just forgotten. You don’t need fifty souvenirs to feel the shift. Three is enough. Three specific moments proving your life isn’t just gray. That’s all it takes to crack the Rut open.

This creates a Positive Feedback Loop.

You Write the Souvenir (creating proof of joy).

You Read it on a bad day (breaking the amnesia).

That relief lowers the barrier to Notice (North) a new spark.

You have more momentum to Embark (East) again.

Every Souvenir you write creates kinetic energy for the next trip. You aren't just capturing memories; you’re building a case against the Rut. You’re proving to yourself, one sentence at a time, that your life is worth living.


Six months from now, you’ll be deep in the Rut again. The gray will be back. You’ll feel like you’ve never been awake, like you imagined the whole thing. And then you’ll open your notes and read: ‘The garbage truck rumbled past like a dragon clearing its throat.’ And you’ll remember. You’ll remember that on a random Tuesday in March, you were standing on a sidewalk outside your home at 5 AM, and the world was alive, and you were there. And for a second, the gray will crack. That’s what the souvenir does. It’s not just a memory. It’s a light you carry into dark places.




The Ripple: Breaking the "Fine" Loop

There is a final, magical benefit to the Souvenir. It solves the problem of "having nothing to say."

We all know the "Fine" Loop. You sit down for dinner with your spouse or meet a friend for a drink.

"How was your day?" "Fine. Busy. Yours?" "Fine."

This is the death rattle of connection. It’s data exchange, not emotional exchange. It creates Social Loneliness even when you’re sitting right next to someone.

But if you’ve used the Compass, and you have your Souvenir, you have a gift to oﬀer.

When they ask what you did, you don't say, "Nothing." You say: "I saw the funniest thing at the grocery store - a kid dancing in the aisle like she was on Broadway."

Watch what happens to their face. They were braced for another round of “Fine, busy, you?” But you just handed them a picture. Their eyes light up. They lean forward. “Wait, really? Like full-on Broadway?” Now you’re not exchanging status updates, you’re sharing a moment. You’ve turned a transaction into a connection. And it cost you exactly one sentence.

Watch what happens to the conversation.

The other person will smile. They’ll relate. They’ll tell you about something funny they saw. You’ve offered them a specific image. You’ve broken the script of small talk. You’ve invited them into a moment of human connection.

This is The Ripple.

Wonder is contagious. When you share your Souvenir, whether it’s telling your spouse over dinner, texting it to a friend, or posting it in our Clubhouse community, you aren’t just preserving the memory for yourself. You’re passing the spark to someone else.

You become a person who Notices. You become a person who has stories. You become a person who brings color into the room.

The Souvenir Method



The Practice

Here’s your assignment for West.

The next time you use the Compass, whether it’s a big trip or just noticing the light in your kitchen, don’t let the moment end until you’ve captured it.

Stop. Pull out your phone, or a scrap of paper, or the back of a receipt. Ask yourself: What was the single best feeling from the last hour?

Boil it down. Distill it. Catch it in one sentence.

Then, put it in your pocket.

Time is a dragon that eats everything. It eats your Tuesdays and your weekends. It eats your memories. But the pen is a weapon. When you write a Souvenir, you’re slicing open the belly of the dragon and rescuing the moment. You’re saying: "No. You don't get to have this one. This one is mine."

The dragon is huge and ancient and it never stops eating. But you have a knife now. And every time you write a sentence, you carve out one more moment the dragon can’t have. One more piece of your life that survives. The dragon doesn’t care. It has plenty to eat. But you care. Because that sentence is the proof that you were here, that you looked up, that you chose to be awake for one more minute of your one wild, short, precious life.

So steal that diamond from the dragon of time. You have the proof. Now, let’s use that energy to find the Center = Rest.

The Tool: The One-Sentence Souvenir. Not journaling, not a novel, just one sentence to capture the highlight.

Specific is Terrific: Avoid vague labels ("It was fun"). Capture sensory details ("The click of the gem," "The smell of garlic") to lock the memory in.

The 24-Hour Embargo: You can’t be in Input Mode (Sensing) and Output Mode (Performing) simultaneously. Wait one day before posting to social media so the "wet concrete" of the memory can cure.

The Science: Utilizes the Peak-End Rule (Kahneman) and acts as a depression antidote by rewiring neural pathways.

The Bad Day Protocol: Your collection of Souvenirs serves as proof on gray days that your life is speckled with gold.

The Ripple: Sharing specific Souvenirs breaks the "Fine Loop" of small talk and creates real connection.


Quick Win:
 Open the "Notes" app on your phone right now. Scan the last 24 hours of your life. Find one small moment that felt good - a sip of coffee, a funny text, a cool cloud. Write one sentence about it that includes a sight, sound, or smell. Do not post it. Just keep it. Don’t show anyone yet. Let it sit there in your Notes app like a secret. Tomorrow, add another one. Then another. You’re not keeping a diary. You’re not being productive. You’re robbing the dragon. One sentence at a time, you’re stealing your life back from the jaws of forgetting.
