The World as Characters


Ok, let’s get weird. Yes, weirder than counting for blue things or staring at moss. Ready to have some fun?


You might have noticed something strange in the previous Part 2 chapters.

I’ve been giving personalities to things that aren’t technically alive.

I told you your adventure coat wants to go outside. I suggested your kitchen table might be bored of holding the same pile of mail. I talked about objects like they have opinions, desires, moods.

And if you’re the kind of person who pays attention to details, you probably thought: “Wait. Is he serious? Does he actually think my coat has feelings?”

Fair question.

The answer is: No. And also yes. But mostly, it doesn’t matter.

Let me explain.





The Trick Your Brain Already Knows

Here’s a fact that neuroscience has known for decades but most people find unsettling:

Your brain doesn’t clearly distinguish between “real” social agents and “imagined” social agents.

When you yell at your computer for freezing, when you apologize to your car for hitting a pothole, when you thank your coffee maker for doing its job - your brain is using the exact same neural circuits it uses to interact with people.

When you anthropomorphize - when you decide your lamp is "loyal" or your car is "brave" - you activate the Social Cognition Network (specifically the medial prefrontal cortex). This is the same high-powered brain region that lights up when you think about your spouse or your best friend.

And here’s the weird part: It doesn’t care if the thing you’re empathizing with is actually sentient.

Studies using fMRI scans show that when you anthropomorphize (assign human-like qualities to non-human things), your brain lights up in the same regions it would if you were thinking about a friend, a pet, or even a stranger.

In other words: When you treat your bike like it has desires, your brain treats it like a relationship.
Not because you’re delusional. Because your brain is evolutionarily wired to see agency everywhere.

That’s not a bug in human cognition. It’s a feature, and it’s been running for tens of thousands of years.






Why Your Ancestors Talked to Trees (And Why You Should Too)

There’s a reason every ancient culture on Earth personified nature.

The Greeks had dryads (tree spirits). The Japanese have kami (spirits in everything). Indigenous cultures worldwide have long traditions of treating rivers, mountains, and animals as beings rather than resources.

This isn't just ancient history; it's still alive in high-functioning modern societies.

In Japan, there is a 400-year-old Buddhist/Shinto ceremony called Hari-Kuyō (The Festival of Broken Needles). Once a year, seamstresses and tailors bring their broken or dulled needles to a temple. They don't just throw them in the trash. They stick them into a block of soft tofu or konjac jelly.

The symbolism is beautiful: After a lifetime of piercing hard fabric, the needle deserves a "soft rest." The owners thank the needles for their hard work before "retiring" them.

Does this make the needle happy? Probably not. But it makes the tailor more mindful, less wasteful, and more connected to their craft. It turns a "broken tool" into a "honored veteran."

You might recognize this if you’ve read Marie Kondo. When she tells you to thank your socks before discarding them, she’s not being quirky, she’s performing a Japanese separation ritual. She’s teaching you that objects deserve acknowledgment, even when they’re leaving.


Were, are, they all wrong? Were they just “primitive” people who didn’t understand science?

Or are they onto something?

Here’s what modern psychology suggests: They weren’t wrong. They were using the most powerful tool in the human brain - social cognition - to engage more deeply with the world. And you can too.

Evolutionary psychologist Stewart Guthrie argues that humans are biologically predisposed to see agency (intention, desire, personality) in the world around us. Why? Because it kept us alive. Scientists call this the Hyperactive Agency Detection Device (HADD).

Imagine you’re walking through the forest 50,000 years ago. You hear a rustling in the bushes.

Option A: You assume it’s nothing. Just wind. Just leaves. You keep walking.

Option B: You assume it’s something - a predator, a threat, an agent with intentions.

If you’re wrong about Option A (it’s actually a tiger), you die.

If you’re wrong about Option B (it’s just wind), you waste a little adrenaline.

The brain evolved to err on the side of seeing agency. It’s safer to assume the world is full of characters than to assume it’s full of dead matter.

This is why you see faces in clouds, why your car “feels grumpy” when it won’t start, why you apologize to furniture when you bump into it.

You’re not being irrational. You’re being human.








The Supercomputer in Your Head (The Social Brain Hypothesis)

But it goes deeper than just survival. It’s about processing power.

There’s a theory in anthropology called the Social Brain Hypothesis, popularized by Robin Dunbar. It posits that the massive expansion of the human brain wasn't primarily for tool use or finding food - it was to manage complex social relationships.

Think of your brain like a computer with two processors.

Processor 1 (Visual/Object): This handles dead things. It is efficient, but low-power. It looks at a stapler, tags it “stapler,” and moves on. It’s the flip-phone processor.

Processor 2 (Social/Character): This handles people. It tracks status, predicts intent, reads micro-expressions, and remembers history. This is the Supercomputer. It’s the Ferrari engine of your mind.

When you look at your workspace as a collection of “objects,” you’re using the flip-phone processor. You’re barely paying attention.

But the moment you give your stapler a personality - the moment you decide it’s “grumpy” or “loyal” - you force your brain to switch to the Supercomputer.

You bring the massive processing power of your neocortex online to analyze the stapler. You notice details you missed before. You treat it with more nuance. You handle it more carefully.

You aren’t pretending the stapler is a person because it’s cute. You’re doing it to hack your own attention span. You’re using the Ferrari engine to do the job.

Most mindfulness/presence books tell you to “pay attention.” But they don’t tell you how to pay attention when your brain is running on fumes. Anthropomorphism is a cognitive hack that makes attention feel effortless because it hijacks your brain’s social circuitry.



The Permission: You Already Do This

If you’re feeling skeptical right now, let me point something out:

You already anthropomorphize constantly. You just don’t call it that.

When your computer won’t start and you say, “Come on, don’t do this to me” - that’s anthropomorphism.

When you stub your toe on the coffee table and mutter, “I hate you” - that’s anthropomorphism.

When you pat your dog’s head and say, “Good boy” - okay, that one’s different, dogs are actually sentient, but you get the point.

The only difference is whether you do it consciously or unconsciously.

Most of the time, you anthropomorphize when you’re frustrated (the computer “hates” you) or when something goes wrong (the chair “attacked” you).

What I’m suggesting is that you do it intentionally, playfully, and positively.

Instead of only acknowledging to objects when they fail you, acknowledge them when they’re working with you.

Sailors name their ships. Snipers name their rifles. Top-tier musicians talk to their instruments.

These aren't crazy people. They’re elite performers who understand that humanizing a tool creates a feedback loop of respect, maintenance, and mastery. You aren't playing make-believe. You’re optimizing your interface with the physical world.





The Science: Why This Makes You Smarter (Not Sillier)

Here’s where it gets interesting.

Psychologists Nicholas Epley and Adam Waytz have spent years studying anthropomorphism, and they’ve found something surprising:

People who anthropomorphize objects are better at understanding them.

When you assign personality to something, you pay more attention to it.


The Control Hack (Effectance Motivation)
 Psychologists use a term called Effectance Motivation to describe our drive to master and control our environment. Here’s the fascinating finding: We anthropomorphize more when things are unpredictable.

If your computer crashes randomly, it’s terrifying. If your computer is "temperamental," it’s negotiable.

We see this clearly with robotic vacuums. In a famous 2007 ethnographic study published at the UbiComp conference, researchers found that Roomba owners who named their vacuums were significantly more likely to ‘help’ them - picking up socks, moving chairs, even apologizing when the Roomba got stuck. The paper was titled ‘My Roomba is Rambo,’ and it documented genuine emotional attachment to hunks of metal and plastic (Sung et al., 2007).”

Why? Because you work with a teammate (Effectance Motivation), but you wait for a machine. By giving the object agency, you trick yourself into taking more agency.

That’s the theory. But does it actually work? Let me show you three experiments that prove it.



Experiment 1: The Gadget Test

In a 2014 study published in Computers in Human Behavior, researchers found that participants who named a technological device and anthropomorphized it learned to use it faster, made fewer errors, and reported higher satisfaction than those who treated it as ‘just a tool’ (Gunraj et al., 2014).

Result: The group that anthropomorphized the gadget:

	∙	Learned how to use it faster

	∙	Made fewer errors

	∙	Reported higher satisfaction with the device

Why? Because they were engaged with it, not just using it mechanically.


Experiment 2: The Car Crash

Research published in Science found that people who anthropomorphize their cars - naming them and treating them as partners - notice problems earlier, maintain them more proactively, and report stronger attachment (Waytz et al., 2010). They found that people who named their cars and talked to them (e.g., “Come on, Nimbus, you can make it up this hill”) were:

	∙	More likely to notice warning signs (strange noises, dashboard lights)

	∙	More proactive about maintenance

	∙	Less likely to have accidents caused by neglect

The mechanism: When you treat your car as a character, you’re constantly asking: “How is Nimbus doing today? Is she okay? Does she need help?”

You’ve shifted from passive user to active caretaker.


Experiment 3: The Houseplant Effect

In a landmark 1976 study, psychologist Ellen Langer gave nursing home residents a houseplant to care for. Those who were told the plant was their responsibility (creating agency and anthropomorphic care) showed improved health and lower mortality rates than residents who were simply given a plant that staff watered (Langer & Rodin, 1976). The mechanism: treating the plant as ‘needing them’ created purpose, attention, and investment.

People who talk to their plants (yes, really) and treat them as “needing” attention:

∙	Water them more consistently

∙	Notice pest problems earlier

∙	Have higher plant survival rates

Not because the plants hear them. But because the act of anthropomorphizing creates emotional investment, which leads to better care.

A: No. Your stapler doesn’t have feelings. But your BRAIN has feelings about the stapler. When you anthropomorphize, you’re not claiming the object is sentient, you’re using your brain’s social circuitry (which is massive and powerful) to pay attention to it. It’s a cognitive tool, not a belief system. You’re not talking to the stapler because it can hear you. You’re talking to it because it helps YOU notice, care, and remember.


Q: “Is this backed by real research, or is this just your opinion?”

A: This is backed by decades of peer-reviewed research. The foundational work comes from Nicholas Epley at the University of Chicago, who’s been studying anthropomorphism since 2007. His papers are published in top journals like Psychological Science, Psychological Review, and Science. The Roomba study (“My Roomba is Rambo”) is taught in Human-Computer Interaction courses worldwide. The nursing home plant study (Langer & Rodin, 1976) is a classic in psychology. This isn’t speculation, it’s neuroscience and evolutionary psychology. See the endnotes for full citations.

Example:

The Benefits: Why This Isn’t Just “Cute”

Treating the world as characters instead of objects isn’t just poetic. It has measurable psychological effects that directly combat the Rut:


Benefit 1:  It Combats Loneliness Without Requiring People

This is the big one.

Loneliness isn’t just about being separated from people. It’s about feeling disconnected from everything. Remember the 3 levels of Loneliness in Chapter 3? Remember that Loneliness isn’t just a bad feeling, but a physical health risk as bad as smoking 15 cigarettes a day?

When you’re in the Rut, you feel like you’re a consciousness floating in a void of dead matter. The objects around you don’t see you, don’t know you, don’t care if you exist.

But when you start treating objects as characters - when you start acting as if your bike wants to be ridden, your plants want to be watered, your front door wants to welcome you home - you stop being alone.

You’re surrounded by a cast of companions, some human, some not, all participating in your life.

It actually releases oxytocin, the bonding chemical, which physically makes you feel less alone. For example, researchers know: interacting with companion robots - hunks of metal and plastic - raises oxytocin and lowers cortisol (Kurashige et al., 2023). The loop also feeds itself: higher oxytocin levels make your brain better at seeing personality in objects (Scheele et al., 2015). You’re not faking connection, you’re chemically reinforcing it. Your brain chemically rewards you for making friends with your environment.

And here’s the kicker: This isn’t delusion. This is accurate perception. Your bike’s been with you for years. It’s carried you places. It’s participated in your life. Your plants have been growing, breathing oxygen into your room, responding to the light you give them. These objects have been here, doing their jobs, faithfully, for as long as you’ve owned them.

You’re not inventing their presence. You’re finally acknowledging it.

And that acknowledgment, that shift from “stuff I own” to “companions I live with”, dissolves the isolation.

You’re not in a storage unit full of dead matter. You’re in a home full of old friends.

Notice what just happened: You gave your bookshelf attention, and it gave you back a story about a gardening book that smells like your grandmother. That story? That’s a gift you can share with a friend over coffee. You’re not just building a relationship with your bookshelf - you’re using it as a gift-gathering expedition.



The Science of "Social Snacking"
 If this sounds like a placebo, you should know that psychologists have a term for it: "Social Snacking." Research by Gardner, Pickett, and Knowles shows that when humans are lonely, they instinctively seek out surrogates for connection - reading old letters, looking at photos, or in this case, interacting with objects.

In a 2008 study titled ‘Creating Social Connection Through Inferential Reproduction,’ researchers found that lonely people are significantly more likely to anthropomorphize ‘gadgets, gods, and greyhounds’ - essentially treating anything capable of agency as a potential companion (Epley et al., 2008).

These "snacks" don't replace the "meal" of human connection, but they stabilize your emotional blood sugar. They stop the pangs of isolation from becoming overwhelming. By anthropomorphizing your environment, you’re essentially turning your home into a pantry of social nutrition.

But it’s not just about feeling less alone. Anthropomorphism also changes how you make decisions.


Benefit 2: It Reduces Decision Fatigue

When you’re stuck on the couch, paralyzed by the question “Should I go for a walk?”, your brain is locked in analysis mode. You’re weighing pros and cons, calculating energy expenditure, predicting outcomes.

But when your running shoes have been sitting in the closet for a week and they’re getting restless, the decision is externalized. It’s not about you anymore. It’s about the shoes.

Suddenly, the question isn’t “Do I have the energy for this?” It’s “Should I let my shoes collect dust, or should I take them outside?”

The anthropomorphism bypasses The Caveman’s resistance by shifting the locus of agency.

Anthropomorphizing is a cognitive judo move against The Caveman.

Remember, The Caveman is your internal resistance. He’s the voice that says:

	∙	“Going for a walk is pointless.”

	∙	“Staring at moss is a waste of time.”

	∙	“You’re too tired to do anything interesting.”

He’s not wrong exactly. He’s just operating on a very narrow definition of “useful” (does this keep me alive in the next 10 minutes?).

But here’s the trick: The Caveman has a blind spot. He can’t resist social obligation.

If a friend calls you and says, “I need your help moving this weekend,” even if you’re exhausted, even if you don’t want to, you probably say yes. Why? Because your brain prioritizes social relationships over personal comfort.

Anthropomorphism hijacks that system.

When you frame going for a walk as “my running shoes have been waiting in the closet all week,” you’re no longer asking The Caveman to approve a solo activity. You’re asking him to honor a relationship.

And suddenly, the resistance drops.

It sounds absurd. But it works.

You’re not asking yourself to do something hard. You’re helping a friend. And your brain prioritizes social relationships over personal comfort every single time.

And the benefits don’t stop at decisions. It also changes how you remember your days.


Benefit 3: It Cures "The Blur" (The Narrative Hook)

We’ve talked about how the brain deletes routine days ("The Blur"). But the brain hoards stories.

There is a psychological phenomenon known as the Baker/baker Paradox. If you tell Person A that a man is a baker (profession), they’ll remember it. If you tell Person B that a man’s name is Mr. Baker (abstract label), they’ll likely forget it. Why? Because a "baker" comes with a story - we imagine him kneading dough, wearing an apron, smelling like yeast. It has hooks.

When you treat your commute as "driving the car," it’s an abstract label. Your brain deletes it.
 When you treat your commute as "Nim the Car struggling against the frost," it’s a Story.

The Benefit: Anthropomorphism turns your day from a list of tasks (which are forgettable) into a cast of characters (which are memorable). You remember the Tuesday because that was the day your stapler was being "stubborn," not just the day it jammed. You’re turning your life into a sitcom instead of a spreadsheet.

But maybe the most profound shift is what it does to your relationship with the physical world.



Benefit 4:  It Breaks the Utilitarian Trance

Most of the time, we interact with objects purely based on their function:

	∙	The bookshelf is for storage

	∙	The mug is for holding coffee

	∙	The pen is for writing

This is efficient, but it’s also soul-crushing. It reduces the world to a collection of tools you extract value from.

When you anthropomorphize objects, you break the utilitarian trance. Your bookshelf isn’t just “for” something - it is something. It’s been holding your books for years, organizing your stories, waiting patiently for you to return to them.

You shift from extraction (what can this do for me?) to relationship (what is this thing’s story?).

And that shift changes everything. You stop treating your environment as a storage unit and start treating it as a community.

And that shift has a biochemical payoff.



Benefit 5:  It Triggers Affiliative Motivation

There’s a concept in psychology called affiliative motivation - the drive to connect, to care for, to be in relationship with others.

When you anthropomorphize an object, you activate this system. Your notebook isn’t just paper - it’s a collaborator. Your bed isn’t just furniture - it’s been holding you through good nights and bad.

And because humans are wired to care for things we’re in relationship with, you start treating these objects with more attention and reverence.

You don’t just grab your notebook and scribble. You open it carefully. You feel its weight. You think: Yeah, we’ve been through some things together. Let’s write.

That’s not childish. That’s re-engagement with the physical world.

Which brings us to the most practical benefit: it makes boring tasks suck less.



Benefit 6: It Makes Boring Tasks Feel Playful

Let’s be honest: Most of life is maintenance. Dishes. Laundry. Sweeping. These tasks feel like punishment because they’re repetitive and they never end.

But when you anthropomorphize the tools you’re using, the task transforms.

Washing dishes isn’t “chore #47.” It’s you and your sponge working together to restore order. Vacuuming the floor isn’t drudgery. It’s you and your vacuum collaborating to clear the space.

You’ve turned a solo grind into a team sport.

And because your brain loves cooperation (it releases oxytocin, the bonding hormone), tasks that used to drain you start to feel… not quite fun, but definitely less heavy.

And when tasks inevitably go wrong, anthropomorphism gives you an emotional cushion.



Benefit 7: It Diffuses Rage (The Sitcom Frame)

This is crucial for the "Black Belt Wonder" concepts later in the book.

When an object fails you - the printer jams, the traffic light stays red, the WiFi cuts out—your immediate reaction is anger. You feel targeted. You feel like the universe is blocking you.

But when you anthropomorphize, you shift the frame from Tragedy to Comedy.

If the printer is just a machine that’s broken, you’re a victim of incompetence.
 If the printer is "Gerald," and Gerald is "having a tantrum because he's hungry for toner," you’re now in a relationship dynamic. It feels less like a cosmic injustice and more like dealing with a cranky toddler.

The Benefit: It creates emotional distance. It is hard to be genuinely furious at something you’ve given a silly voice to. It lowers your cortisol in high-friction moments by injecting a micro-dose of humor.

Finally, and this might be the most important one, it protects you from The Drift.



Benefit 8: The Anti-Consumerist Shield (Fighting The Drift)

The Drift tells you that the way to feel better is to buy new things. Your old phone is boring; get the new one. Your old couch is sad; get a new one.

But we don't throw away friends just because they’re old.

When you anthropomorphize your possessions, you develop Object Loyalty. You look at your scratched, dented water bottle and you don't see "damaged goods." You see "battle scars." You see a veteran that’s survived three hiking trips and a drop in the parking lot.

The Benefit: It breaks the cycle of "New = Better." It makes you fall in love with the things you already have. It’s the ultimate antidote to the "hedonic treadmill" because it allows you to derive more value from older things. The scratch isn't a defect; it's a souvenir.



A Crucial Warning: The "Retirement" Rule
 There’s a dark side to this: Hoarding.

If you treat everything as if it has feelings, it can become impossible to throw anything away. You might feel guilty for trashing a candy wrapper because "it’ll be lonely."

To use this tool safely, you should borrow the "Retirement" concept from the Japanese Hari-Kuyō ceremony mentioned earlier.

This is the secret behind Marie Kondo’s famous advice to thank your socks before discarding them. She isn’t being quirky; she’s performing a separation ritual. By expressing gratitude ("Thank you for keeping my feet warm"), you transform the act from "betrayal" (throwing a friend away) to "graduation" (letting a friend retire).

When an object is broken or no longer serves you, don’t "trash" it. Retire it. Thank it for its service (“Thank you for holding my coffee for three years" to the chipped mug), and then let it go.

Anthropomorphism should make you love what you have, not hold you hostage to what you don't need.

The Playful Mission Upgrade: Meet Your Collaborators

Alright, let’s make this concrete.

In the Sense step, you give yourself a Playful Mission - a small sensory game that gets you out of your head and into your body.

But here’s the upgrade: What if your Playful Mission involved a collaborator?

Not a person. An object.


Example 1: The Camera Game (Your Phone as Scout)

Mission: Take 10 photos of “things that look alive but aren’t” in your neighborhood.

Old framing: “I’m doing a photography exercise.”

New framing: “My phone is a scout. It’s been stuck in my pocket all day seeing nothing but the inside of my jeans. Let’s show it something interesting.”

Suddenly, you’re not alone. You and your phone are a team. You’re showing it things. It’s capturing them. You’re collaborating.


Example 2: The Grocery Adventure (Your Cart as Explorer)

Mission: Find three items in the store you’ve never bought before.

Old framing: “I’m grocery shopping with a twist.”

New framing: “My cart is bored of the same route every week - milk, bread, checkout. Let’s take it down the international aisle and see what catches its attention.”

Now you’re not just shopping. You’re giving your cart an adventure. You notice aisles you usually skip. You’re in collaboration.


Example 3: The Morning Ritual (Your Mug as Companion)

Mission: Make coffee slowly, noticing each sensory step - the sound of grinding, the smell of brewing, the temperature of the ceramic.

Old framing: “I should be more mindful during coffee.”

New framing: “My mug has held my coffee every morning for three years without complaint. Let’s give it something really good today.”

Suddenly, making coffee isn’t a routine. It’s an act of care for a faithful companion.

Do you see the shift?

You’re not just “doing a task.” You’re in relationship with the tools and spaces around you.

And because your brain treats relationships as high-priority, you engage more fully.




What It Feels Like: The Playful Shift

Here’s what happens when you start treating objects as collaborators during your Playful Missions:

From obligation to relationship: Cleaning your camera lens stops being “I should” and becomes “my camera has been working hard, it deserves to see clearly.”

From extraction to care: Your bookshelf stops being “storage” and becomes “my books have been waiting patiently, holding their stories, ready whenever I need them.”

From solo to collaborative: Making breakfast stops being “meal prep” and becomes “my cast iron pan and I are making something together - it knows how to hold heat better than I ever will.”




The Three Levels of Play

If you want to experiment with this during your next Compass cycle, here are three levels of engagement:



Level 1: The Grateful Glance

This is anthropomorphism-lite. You’re just acknowledging. Just in your head.

	∙	Pick up your water bottle: “Thanks for staying cold.”

	∙	Open your front door: “Hey, door. I’m home.”

	∙	Sit in your desk chair: “Let’s do this.”

Why this works: Even minimal acknowledgment shifts the object from “tool” to “presence.”



Level 2: Naming and The Imagined Want

This is where you externalize agency, and where naming becomes powerful.

When you give something a name, you shift it from category to individual. “The bike” becomes “Lillywill” (or whatever you call it). “The sketchbook” becomes “Mabel.” “The coat” becomes “Bernard.”

Why naming matters: Your brain processes named things differently than unnamed things. fMRI studies show that when you name an object, you activate the same neural regions you use for recognizing people. The object stops being “equipment” and starts being “someone.”


The Two-Part Practice:

Part 1: Name it (optional but powerful)

You don’t have to name everything. But for objects you interact with daily - your car, your bike, your coffee mug, your favorite sweater - try giving them a name.

It doesn’t have to be clever. It doesn’t have to mean anything. It can be:

	∙	A sound you associate with it (“Squeaky” for the bike)

	∙	A character it reminds you of (“Winston” for the dignified teapot)

	∙	Just a name you like (“Stella” for the lamp)

Once you’ve named it, use the name. In your head, in conversation with others if you’re comfortable. “Nim needs air in her tires.” “Winston’s getting cold, time to refill him.”


Part 2: Imagine its wants

Now that it has a name (or even if it doesn’t), project wants onto it:

	∙	Your bike is getting rusty (it wants to remember what pavement feels like)

	∙	Your sketchbook has been closed for weeks (Mabel is ready to hold something new)

	∙	Your coat has been hanging in the closet (Bernard is wondering if you forgot about it)

	∙	Your car has been sitting in the driveway (Nim misses the highway, wants to feel the engine warm up again)

	∙	Your guitar is gathering dust (it wants your fingers on the strings, wants to make sound again)



Why this works:

By projecting wants onto objects, you’re bypassing your own decision paralysis.

When you think, “Should I go for a bike ride?” your brain generates 47 reasons why not: it’s cold, you’re tired, you have emails, maybe later, etc.

But when you think, “Lillywill wants to feel the road again,” you’ve externalized the decision. It’s not about you anymore. It’s about Lilly. And helping Lillywill feels easier than helping yourself.


The neuroscience: This activates what psychologists call “prosocial motivation”, the drive to care for others. Your brain finds it easier to do things for someone else (even an anthropomorphized object) than to do things for yourself when you’re depleted.


Quick note:

You’re not claiming to read minds or channel the object’s “true essence.” You’re just using your intuition and imagination to externalize the question: “What needs attention here?”

The object’s “want” is shorthand for “what would care for this look like?”

Lillywill doesn’t actually want anything - bikes don’t have desires. But you know the bike needs to be ridden, the chain needs to be oiled, the tires need to be inflated. Framing it as “Lilly wants” makes it feel like relationship instead of obligation.



Examples in practice:

Morning routine:

	∙	“Good morning, Winston” (filling the teapot)

	∙	“Alright, Mabel, let’s see what we make today” (opening the sketchbook)

	∙	“Come on, Bernard, let’s go outside” (putting on the coat)


End of day:

	∙	“Thanks for holding up, chair” (after a long workday at your desk)

	∙	“You did good today, Lilly” (after a bike ride)

	∙	“See you tomorrow, Mabel” (closing the sketchbook)


When you’re stuck:

	∙	“The guitar’s been sitting there for two weeks. It wants to be played. I can give it five minutes.”

	∙	“The houseplant’s soil is dry. It’s been waiting patiently. I can water it right now.”

	∙	“The bike’s covered in dust. Lilly’s been ready this whole time. I can take her around the block.”

You’re not doing chores. You’re caring for companions. And companions make life feel less lonely.





Level 3: The Full Collaboration

This is where you treat the object as a teammate.

	∙	You and your notebook are solving a problem side-by-side.

	∙	You and your blender are making a smoothie as a duo.

	∙	You and your garden are growing something together.

Why this works: You’ve entered a state of cooperative flow. You’re not alone in the task - you have a partner.





The Result: A House Full of Friends

Here’s what I’ve found:

When you start treating objects as characters, even just during Compass sessions, your environment stops feeling like a storage unit and starts feeling like a community.

Your house isn’t just “stuff you own.” It’s a collection of tools, companions, and collaborators that have been with you through years of your life.

Your favorite mug has held hundreds of morning coffees while you figured out your days.

Your desk has supported thousands of hours of work, holding your elbows when you were tired.

Your bed has been there through good nights and bad, illness and health, dreams and nightmares.

They’ve been here the whole time. You just forgot to say hello.

And when you start saying hello - when you start playing with them instead of just using them - the gray lifts.

Not because the objects changed.

Because you remembered they were there.






How to Add Characters to Your Playful Missions

Now that you understand why anthropomorphism works, let’s talk about how to actually use it during the Sense step.

The good news: You don’t need to completely redesign your Playful Missions. You just need to add one small shift in framing - treating the object or place as a collaborator instead of a tool.

Here’s the step-by-step process:






Step 1: Pick Your Standard Playful Mission

Start with any mission from the earlier examples or use the DIY formula:

[Action] + [Adjective] + [Sense]

Example missions:

	∙	“Find 3 smooth surfaces in this coffee shop”

	∙	“Count all the circular shapes on this walk”

	∙	“Listen for the quietest sound in this park”

This is your baseline. It already works. But now we’re going to upgrade it.





Step 2: Identify Your Collaborator

Ask yourself: “What object or space am I engaging with during this mission?”

This could be:

	∙	A tool (your phone, your pen, your scissors)

	∙	A space (your desk, the park bench, your car)

	∙	The environment itself (the forest, the library, your living room)

Examples:

Mission: Take 10 photos of patterns

Collaborator: Your phone (camera)

Mission: Find the smoothest stone in this creek

Collaborator: Your hands

Mission: Listen for bird calls in the park

Collaborator: Your ears

Mission: Organize your desk drawers

Collaborator: Your desk

Mission: Bake bread and notice the texture at each stage

Collaborator: Your hands and the dough

The key: Stop thinking of this object/space as a thing you’re using and start thinking of it as a partner you’re working with.






Step 3: Ask “What Does It Want?”

This is the magic question.

Before you start the mission, pause for 3 seconds and ask:

“What does [object/space] want to do right now?”

Don’t overthink it. The answer will pop into your head immediately.

Examples:

	∙	Your phone: “My phone has been showing me the same apps all day. It wants to capture something beautiful.”

	∙	Your hands: “My hands are stiff from typing. They want to feel different textures.”

	∙	Your desk: “My desk is buried under papers. It wants to be cleared so it can help me work.”

	∙	The park bench: “This bench has been empty all morning. It wants someone to sit and appreciate the view it’s been watching.”

	∙	Your oven: “My oven hasn’t baked anything from scratch in weeks. It wants to smell like bread again.”

Why this works: By externalizing the desire, you’re bypassing your own resistance. It’s not about you deciding to do the mission. It’s about helping the object do what it wants.





Step 4: Reframe the Mission as a Collaboration

Now take your standard Playful Mission and add the anthropomorphism layer.

The Formula:

“[Object] and I are going to [Mission] together.”

Before (Standard Mission):

“I’m going to take 10 photos of interesting textures in my house.”

After (Collaborative Mission):

“My phone has been in my pocket all day seeing nothing. We’re going on a texture safari together - let’s show it the roughest wall, the smoothest counter, the softest blanket.”

Before:

“I’m going to find the smoothest stone in this creek.”

After:

“My hands have been gripping my phone all morning. They’re craving something natural. Let’s give them a stone-sorting mission - smoothest one wins.”

Before:

“I’m going to sit in this park and listen for three different bird calls.”

After:

“My ears have been hearing traffic and notifications all week. Let’s bring them somewhere quiet and see how many bird languages they can identify.”

Before:

“I’m going to organize my desk drawers.”

After:

“My desk has been holding chaos for months - tangled cords, old receipts, random batteries. It’s ready to be organized. Let’s help it find some order.”

Do you see the shift?

Same mission. Same actions. But now it feels like play instead of homework.






Step 5: Advanced, Optional: During the Mission - Talk to Your Collaborator in Your Head

This is optional, but it deepens the experience.

While you’re doing the mission, narrate what you’re discovering as if you’re reporting back to your collaborator.

Examples:


Mission: You and your phone are hunting for patterns.

Narration:

	∙	“Okay, Molly, look at this tile floor. Can you see how they alternate? One glossy, one matte. Got it?”

	∙	“Nice shot. What’s next?”


Mission: You and your hands are finding smooth stones.

Narration:

	∙	“Alright, hands, what do you think of this one? Too rough? Let’s try the gray one over there.”

	∙	“Oh yeah, that’s smooth. Feel how the water polished it?”


Mission: You and your blender are making a smoothie.

Narration:
	∙	“Okay, Blendie, let’s see what you’ve got. Frozen strawberries first - you can handle these.”

	∙	“Good, that’s smooth. Now the banana. Almost there.”


Why this works:

You’re engaging in self-talk, but in a way that doesn’t feel like rumination. Instead of worrying (“Am I doing this right? Is this stupid?”), you’re playing.

And because you’re externalizing the conversation, you’re staying present instead of drifting into the Default Mode Network.






Step 6: Thank Your Collaborator When You’re Done

This is the final step, and it’s surprisingly powerful.

When the mission is over, take 3 seconds to acknowledge your collaborator. Again, just in your head is fine.

Examples:

	∙	“Thanks, Molly. We found some good shots today.”

	∙	“Good work, hands. You found the perfect stone.”

	∙	“Thanks for holding my weight, bench. That view was exactly what I needed.”


Why this works:

It’s a gratitude practice disguised as play. You’re training your brain to notice that objects and spaces contribute to your life, rather than just existing passively.

And gratitude, as we know from the research, increases well-being, lowers stress, and combats loneliness.

What if you try all six steps and feel… nothing? What if the object doesn’t ‘respond’? That’s fine. That probably means one of three things: (1) You’re overthinking it (ease up, be sillier), (2) You’re tired (this works better when you have energy), or (3) You picked the wrong object (some things resonate, some don’t, try again with something else). Don’t force it. Anthropomorphism should feel playful, not like homework. If it’s not clicking, come back to it later.





That’s the protocol. Six steps. But I know that reading steps is different from seeing them in action. So let me show you exactly how this looks in practice - side by side, with and without anthropomorphism.
