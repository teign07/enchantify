Chapter 11D: The Neurodivergent Compass





If this book doesn’t out me as neurospicy, nothing will. So this chapter is for my people. 😊

You know that feeling when a productivity book tells you to “just build a habit” and you want to throw the book across the room?

You’ve tried building habits. You’ve tried bullet journals and apps and sticky notes plastered on every surface. You’ve tried accountability partners and motivational quotes and waking up at 5 AM. You’ve tried setting seventeen alarms with increasingly aggressive titles: “GET UP NOW,” “YOU SAID YOU’D DO THIS,” “WHY ARE YOU LIKE THIS.”

It doesn’t work. Not because you’re not trying hard enough. Because your brain’s hardware doesn’t run the software those books were written for.

The book says “Just decide to do it, then do it.” And you think: I did decide. I decided three hours ago. My brain and my body just aren’t speaking to each other right now.

The book says “Just focus on one thing at a time.” And you think: I’m trying. But my brain is a browser with 47 tabs open and three of them are playing music and I don’t know which ones.

The book says “Just calm down.” And you think: Oh wow, I hadn’t thought of that. Just calm down. Revolutionary.

And then you open a book like this one. A book that promises to help you “escape the Rut” and “rediscover wonder.” You read the instructions:

“Go for a walk and notice things”

“Write a Souvenir when you get home”

“Engage all your senses”

“Just be present”

And your brain laughs. Because your brain knows:

	∙	Going for a walk requires getting off the couch, which requires an executive function you don’t currently have access to

	∙	Writing when you get home means remembering you were supposed to write something, which… good luck with that

	∙	Engaging all your senses sounds like a nightmare when you’re already drowning in sensory input

	∙	“Just be present” is what people say when they have no idea how loud your brain is

You feel that familiar sinking sensation. The one where you think: Oh. This book is for people whose brains work normally. Not for me.

You start to close it.

Stop. You’re my people and I won’t leave you behind.

Don’t close it yet.


If you’re neurodivergent - ADHD, autistic, anxious, dealing with sensory processing differences, or any combination - you’ve probably been told your whole life to:

“Just focus.”

“Just calm down.”

“Just be normal.”

And if I tell you “just use the Compass” without acknowledging that your brain works differently, I’m doing the same thing those people did.

So let’s be specific about what’s hard and what actually helps.





What Neurotypical Brains Take for Granted:

Initiation: Deciding to do something and then… doing it.

If you have ADHD, you know this isn’t how it works. Your brain says “yes, let’s go for a walk” and your body says “no, we’re staying on the couch forever.” The couch has been holding you hostage for three hours. It knows you wanted to leave at 2 PM. It doesn’t care. The couch wants you to stay, and it’s very persuasive. Your brain and your body are having two completely different conversations, and your body is winning.

Filtering: Being in a busy environment and only noticing relevant things.

If you’re autistic or have sensory processing issues, this is a joke. You can’t filter. You notice everything. The hum of the fluorescent lights. The tag in your shirt. The smell of someone’s perfume three aisles over. The flicker in the corner of your vision. Your brain is a radio picking up every single station at once, and someone’s asking you to “just focus on the music.”

Social ease: Doing something unusual in public without crippling self-consciousness.

If you’re autistic or anxious, the idea of stopping in the middle of a sidewalk to look at moss sounds like volunteering for public execution. Everyone will stare. Everyone will judge. Everyone will know you’re weird. (They won’t. But your brain doesn’t believe that.)

Memory: Remembering to do the thing you planned to do.

If you have ADHD, you know that “I’ll write the Souvenir when I get home” is a lie you tell yourself. By the time you get home, the entire adventure has evaporated from your brain like it never happened. The memory tried to hold on. It was right there, waiting for you to write it down. But then you saw your phone, checked a text, thought about dinner, and the memory just… gave up. It’s gone now. It tried.

Completion: Starting a task and finishing it.

If you have ADHD, you know that starting the Compass loop is hard, but finishing it is harder. You get distracted halfway through. You start noticing something, then your brain says “hey, remember that thing from 2019 you need to worry about?” and you’re gone. The mission sits there, half-finished, confused about where you went.

If any of these are hard for you, the standard Compass instructions won’t work. You need modifications.



The Science: Why Standard Instructions Don’t Work

Let’s be clear about what we’re talking about here.

When I say “the standard Compass instructions won’t work,” I don’t mean you’re not trying hard enough. I mean the instructions were written for brains that have certain baseline capacities that your brain doesn’t have.

Not because your brain is broken. Because your brain is wired differently.

Here’s what the research actually shows.




The Prevalence (You’re Not Alone)

First, let’s establish scale. Neurodivergence isn’t a rare edge case.

According to the CDC and recent research:

	∙	6-7% of children and adults have ADHD (that’s roughly 15-18 million Americans)

	∙	1-2% of the population is autistic (though many researchers believe the actual number is much higher due to underdiagnosis, especially in women and adults)

	∙	Anxiety disorders affect 31% of adults at some point in their lives

	∙	Sensory processing differences are estimated to affect 5-16% of children, with many carrying into adulthood

And these conditions frequently overlap. Many people have ADHD and anxiety. Or autism and ADHD. Or all three.

This isn’t a footnote in a self-help book. This is a massive portion of the population for whom standard “just do it” advice doesn’t work.

And yet almost every productivity, mindfulness, or adventure book assumes:

	∙	You can decide to do something and then do it

	∙	You can filter sensory input and focus on what’s relevant

	∙	You’ll remember to write things down later

	∙	You won’t be paralyzed by the fear of doing it wrong

Those assumptions are wrong for tens of millions of people.






The Neuroscience of Executive Dysfunction (Why “Just Do It” Doesn’t Work)

Dr. Russell Barkley, one of the leading ADHD researchers in the world, has spent decades studying executive function. His findings are brutal and liberating:

ADHD is not a deficit of knowing what to do. It’s a deficit of doing what you know.

Here’s what that means neurologically:

In a neurotypical brain, when you decide “I’m going to walk to the mailbox,” a chain of executive functions activates:

	1.	Intention formation: “I want to do this”

	2.	Planning: “I need to stand up, put on shoes, walk to the door”

	3.	Initiation: “Okay, standing up now”

	4.	Sustained attention: “Still walking to mailbox, not getting distracted”

	5.	Task completion: “I did the thing”


In an ADHD brain, step 3 (Initiation) frequently fails. Not because you don’t want to do it. Not because you forgot. But because the neural pathway between “I’ve decided to do this” and “my body is now doing this” is unreliable.

Barkley calls this “time blindness” - the inability to use past and future to guide present behavior. Your brain exists in an eternal now. The intention you had three hours ago? That’s not accessible right now. The consequence you’ll face later if you don’t do it? That’s not real yet.

The implication: Willpower doesn’t fix this. External scaffolding does. That’s why the 2-Minute Contract works - it removes the need for sustained intention. You only have to hold the intention for two minutes.





Working Memory Deficit (Why You Forget Immediately)

Working memory is your brain’s RAM - the temporary storage space where you hold information while you use it.

Research shows that people with ADHD have significantly impaired working memory capacity. You can hold about 2-3 items in working memory at once, compared to 5-7 for neurotypical people.

Here’s what this means practically:

You have a great adventure. You notice the moss, the light, the texture of the bark. You think “I should write this down when I get home.”

But between the adventure and home, your working memory has to process:

	∙	“Did I lock the door?”

	∙	“I need to text Sarah back”

	∙	“What’s for dinner?”

	∙	“Oh, there’s a bird”

	∙	“My shoe is untied”

	∙	“I forgot to pay that bill”

By the time you walk in the door, the memory of the moss is gone. Not because it wasn’t important. Because your working memory can’t hold 47 things at once, and the moss got pushed out by more urgent survival tasks.

The implication: You can’t “just remember better.” You need external memory systems. That’s why Voice Memos work - you offload the memory to your phone immediately, before your working memory dumps it.




Sensory Processing Differences (Why “Engage All Senses” Is Torture)

For many autistic people and people with sensory processing disorder, the problem isn’t that you notice too little. It’s that you notice too much.

Most brains have a sensory “gate” that filters out irrelevant stimuli. You can sit in a cafe and focus on your conversation because your brain automatically filters out:

	∙	The hum of the espresso machine

	∙	The texture of your jeans on your legs

	∙	The flicker of the overhead lights

	∙	The smell of someone’s perfume three tables over

Autistic brains (and some ADHD brains) don’t have that gate. Or the gate is broken. You notice everything at full volume.

This is sometimes called the “Intense World Theory” - the idea that autistic people aren’t less sensitive to the world, but more sensitive. The world is louder, brighter, more textured, more overwhelming.

When a typical adventure book says “engage all five senses,” it assumes you can voluntarily increase your sensory awareness. But if your baseline is already sensory overload, “engage more” means “trigger shutdown.”

The implication: Single-sense focus isn’t a compromise. It’s the only way to actually notice anything without drowning. Noise-canceling headphones aren’t “cheating” - they’re bringing your sensory input down to a manageable level so your brain can process visual information.







The Hyperfocus Paradox (ADHD Isn’t “Can’t Focus”)

Here’s the thing about ADHD that most people get wrong:

ADHD is not an attention deficit. It’s an attention regulation deficit.

You can’t focus on boring tasks (like taxes or chores), but you can hyperfocus on interesting tasks for hours without realizing time has passed.

This is why people with ADHD can:

	∙	Spend six hours building a Minecraft world but can’t spend six minutes folding laundry

	∙	Read an entire fantasy novel in one sitting but can’t read one page of a textbook

	∙	Watch ants for 90 minutes but can’t sit through a 10-minute meeting

It’s not laziness. It’s not lack of discipline. It’s that ADHD brains only have two modes:

	1.	“Not interesting enough to register” (no focus possible)

	2.	“Extremely interesting” (hyperfocus activated, time ceases to exist)

The implication: When the Compass triggers hyperfocus on moss or ants or clouds, that’s not a problem. That’s your brain doing exactly what it’s supposed to do. Let it. The hyperfocus on wonder is the entire point.






Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria (Why Fear of Failure Is Neurological)

Many people with ADHD experience something called Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria (RSD) - an extreme emotional response to perceived criticism or failure.

This isn’t “being sensitive.” This is a neurological response where perceived rejection triggers a pain response in the brain similar to physical pain.

When someone with RSD thinks “What if I do this wrong?” their brain doesn’t generate mild anxiety. It generates panic-level threat response. The fear of doing it wrong feels physically dangerous.

This is why perfectionism and procrastination often co-occur in ADHD:

	∙	You want to do the thing

	∙	But your brain screams that if you do it wrong, the emotional pain will be unbearable

	∙	So you don’t start, because not trying feels safer than trying and failing

The implication: “Just try anyway” doesn’t work. You need explicit permission that there is no wrong. The Compass doesn’t grade. There’s no rubric. If you noticed one thing, you succeeded. That’s not coddling. That’s removing a neurological barrier.






The Bottom Line

Your brain isn’t broken. It’s running different software.

Neurotypical instructions assume:

	∙	You can initiate tasks without external structure

	∙	You can filter sensory input

	∙	You can hold memories in working storage until you write them down

	∙	You can regulate focus voluntarily

	∙	Fear of failure is mild and manageable

Your brain can’t reliably do these things. Not because you’re not trying. Because the neural infrastructure for these things works differently.

The adaptations in this chapter aren’t “accommodations” in the sense of making things easier. They’re necessary modifications to make the tool work with your actual hardware.

You’re not doing it wrong. You’re running the right tool on different hardware, and it needs different inputs.






Adaptation 1: For ADHD (Executive Dysfunction)

The Problem:

You want to do the adventure. You’ve read the chapter. You’ve picked a Spark. You’re excited about it.

But when it’s time to actually leave the house, your body won’t move.

You’re stuck on the couch. Not because you don’t want to go. Not because you’re lazy. But because the part of your brain that says “stand up and walk to the door” has gone offline. The start button is broken. The couch is holding you. Your phone is in your hand. The front door is twelve feet away and it might as well be twelve miles.

This is executive dysfunction, and it’s not a character flaw. It’s a neurological issue where the brain’s “initiation system” misfires.


The Fix: The 2-Minute Contract

Don’t commit to the full adventure. Commit to 2 minutes.

“I’ll walk to the mailbox. That’s it. 2 minutes. Then I can come back.”

That’s the deal. The mailbox is right there. It’s been waiting for you all day, mouth closed, hoping you’ll show up. It doesn’t care if you stay for two minutes or twenty. It just wants you to visit. And here’s the trick: once you’re at the mailbox, once you’re already outside, once your body is in motion, your brain often says “okay, fine, we’re out here, let’s keep going.”

But you don’t have to. The 2-minute deal is real. If you get to the mailbox and want to go back inside, that’s success. You moved. You left the couch. The couch lost.


Why this works:

ADHD brains struggle with big commitments but handle tiny ones. “Go for a 30-minute walk” feels impossible. “Walk to the mailbox” feels doable. And once you’re moving, Newton’s First Law takes over: objects in motion stay in motion.



The Fix: Body Doubling

Do the Compass with another person. Not “with” like they’re participating. “With” like they’re just… there. This is parallel play, and we’ll talk more about it in the next chapter.

Examples:

	∙	Call a friend and say “I’m going to walk around the block. Just stay on the phone with me. You don’t have to talk.”

	∙	Join a virtual co-working session (like Focusmate) and announce “I’m doing a 10-minute Compass loop.” They’ll be on screen doing their own thing. That’s enough.

	∙	Ask your partner/roommate to sit in the same room while you do a Rug Adventure (Chapter 17). They can read. You can lie on the floor and look at the ceiling. You’re body-doubling.


Why this works:

ADHD brains initiate better when there’s another human in the equation. It’s not about accountability or pressure. It’s about the presence of another person creating just enough social scaffolding that your brain says “okay, I guess we’re doing this now.”

The other person doesn’t have to care. They don’t have to watch. They just have to exist near you. Your brain is weird. This works.



The Fix: Attach to Existing Routine

Don’t create a new adventure. Attach the Compass to something you’re already doing.

You’re already walking to the mailbox (because bills). That’s North. Notice one thing on the way.

You’re already going to the grocery store (because food). That’s East. Add one micro-mission: find the ugliest product design in Aisle 4.

You’re already taking the dog out (because the dog has been giving you the Look for ten minutes and refuses to be ignored). That’s your adventure. The dog doesn’t care if you call it a “Playful Mission.” The dog just wants to pee and sniff things. Go with the dog. Notice what the dog notices.


Why this works:

ADHD brains resist starting new things but can modify existing routines. You’re not adding a task. You’re adding a lens to a task that’s already happening.





Adaptation 2: For Autism (Sensory Overwhelm)

The Problem:

The Compass says “engage all your senses.” The book is full of instructions like “listen to the sounds, feel the textures, smell the air, notice the colors.”

But when you try, the world becomes too much.

The sounds are too loud. The smells are too strong. The textures are wrong. The lights are too bright. Your brain isn’t filtering, it’s drowning. You’re not experiencing wonder. You’re experiencing overload. And then you shut down.

This isn’t you “doing it wrong.” This is your nervous system protecting you from sensory assault.


The Fix: Single-Sense Missions

Pick ONE sense. Just one. Ignore the others.

Visual only: Look at textures, colors, patterns, shadows. Wear earplugs or noise-canceling headphones if sound is overwhelming. Your eyes can handle this. Your ears can sit this one out. The world doesn’t need to be loud for you to see it.

Auditory only: Close your eyes. Sit somewhere safe (a park bench, your car, your porch). Listen to one sound at a time. The hum of the highway. The rustle of leaves. The beep of a truck backing up. You’re not trying to hear everything. You’re trying to isolate one thing. The rest of the noise can wait outside. You’re only letting in one guest at a time.

Tactile only: Touch one object. A tree trunk (if the bark texture is okay for you). A smooth metal railing. A cold glass window. Your own jacket (familiar texture, safe). You’re not trying to explore the world. You’re trying to feel one thing that doesn’t make you want to crawl out of your skin.


Why this works:

Autistic brains process sensory input intensely. One sense at a time is manageable. Five senses at once is torture.

The Compass doesn’t require you to use all your senses. It just requires you to use one sense intentionally instead of defensively.



The Fix: Safe Materials Only

If certain textures make you want to scream (rough tree bark, sticky leaves, wet grass), don’t touch them.

Touch only safe materials:

	∙	Smooth metal (railings, poles, door handles)

	∙	Glass (windows, smooth stones)

	∙	Your own clothing (you already know this texture is okay)

	∙	Cool concrete (if that’s a good texture for you)

The tree bark can do its job without you touching it. The tree doesn’t care. It’s been growing bark for thirty years without asking for your input. You’re allowed to look at the tree and skip the touching part.


Why this works:

Wonder doesn’t require discomfort. You can notice the way light reflects in a window without forcing yourself to touch something that feels Bad.



The Fix: Noise-Canceling Headphones Are Mandatory

If you own noise-canceling headphones, wear them during outdoor Compass missions. This isn’t “cheating.” This is adaptive equipment.

The world is too loud. The headphones turn the volume down so your brain can actually process what you’re seeing.

If anyone asks why you’re wearing headphones in a park, you don’t owe them an explanation. But if you want one: “I’m listening to something.” (You’re listening to the absence of noise. That counts.)


Why this works:

Reducing auditory input frees up cognitive bandwidth for visual or tactile input. You can’t notice the details of the moss if your brain is screaming about the sound of a lawnmower three blocks away.




Adaptation 3: For Social Anxiety / Masking

The Problem:

Every time you think about stopping in public to look at something, your brain generates a horror movie:

Everyone will stare.

They’ll think you’re weird.

They’ll know you don’t belong here.

Someone will say something.

You’ll be exposed.

And because your brain is so loud about this, you don’t stop. You keep walking. You miss the moss, the mailbox, the weird sign, the cloud formation. You miss everything because standing still feels like volunteering for public humiliation.


The Fix: Mandatory Props

Never, ever do a public Compass mission without a prop. Remember chapter 8A?

Phone: Hold it up like you’re taking a photo or reading a map. Now you’re not “staring at a building like a weirdo.” You’re “a person using their phone.” Totally normal. The phone doesn’t care if you’re actually taking a photo or just pretending. The phone is happy to be your shield. It’s been protecting you from awkward social situations for years. This is just another one.

Notebook: Hold it like you’re sketching or taking notes. Now you’re “an artist” or “a student.” You have a reason to look. The notebook has been waiting in your bag for weeks, hoping you’d give it a job. Today, its job is to make you look purposeful.

Sunglasses: Wear them even on cloudy days if you’re brave enough. Now no one can see where you’re looking. You can stare at the moss, the mailbox, the person’s weird shoes, whatever. Your eyes are hidden. The sunglasses are your invisibility cloak. They’re not blocking the sun, they’re blocking judgment.


Why this works:

Props give you a “reason” to look, which short-circuits the anxiety loop. You’re not weird. You’re documenting. You’re navigating. You’re creating. The prop tells the story for you.



The Fix: Off-Peak Hours

Do Compass missions when fewer people are around.

Early morning walks (6 AM): Most people are still asleep. The streets are yours. The world is quieter, emptier, safer.

Weekday afternoons (2 PM): Most people are at work. Parks, trails, neighborhoods—they’re all less crowded.

Late evening walks (dusk): People are inside making dinner. You have the sidewalks to yourself.


Why this works:

Fewer people = less masking required = more cognitive bandwidth for actually noticing things.
The moss doesn’t care what time you visit. It’s been growing at the same rate whether it’s 6 AM or 6 PM. It’s just happy someone finally looked.



The Fix: Permission to Flee

If you start a Compass mission and the anxiety spikes - if your chest tightens, if your brain starts screaming, if you feel like you’re about to cry or bolt - you’re allowed to leave immediately.

No explanation. No shame. No “but I said I’d do this.”

Exiting is success, not failure.

You tried. You showed up. Your nervous system said “this is too much right now.” That’s data. That’s your body protecting you. Respect it.

The mailbox will still be there tomorrow. The moss will still be growing. The adventure can wait.


Why this works:

Knowing you can leave reduces the pressure. And reducing the pressure often makes it easier to stay.

But if you need to leave, leave. The Compass isn’t worth a panic attack.






Adaptation 4: For Memory Issues (ADHD/Anxiety)

The Problem:

You had a great Compass loop. You noticed the moss. You felt the texture of the tree bark. You saw the way the light hit the sidewalk. You were present. You were awake.

But by the time you get home, by the time you’ve checked your phone, answered a text, thought about dinner, taken off your shoes, the entire adventure has evaporated from your brain.

The Souvenir never gets written. The memory is gone. It’s like it never happened.

The memory tried to stay. It was sitting there in your short-term storage, waiting for you to write it down. But you got distracted, and the memory got pushed out by “did I lock the door?” and “I need to email Sarah” and “what’s for dinner?” and seventeen other thoughts that all arrived at once, loud and urgent.

By the time you remember you were supposed to write something, the memory is gone. It tried.



The Fix: Voice Memo in the Moment

Don’t wait until you get home. Don’t wait until you’re “done” with the adventure.

The second you notice something worth capturing, pull out your phone and record a 10-second voice memo:

“The moss on the fence was so green it looked fake.”

“The tree bark felt like scales.”

“The cloud looked like it was being pulled apart by invisible hands.”

That’s it. You don’t have to transcribe it later. You don’t have to turn it into a beautiful sentence. The voice memo is the Souvenir.

Your phone has been holding your thoughts for years. It’s used to this job. It doesn’t judge your sentence structure. It doesn’t care if you sound stupid. It just records and waits.


Why this works:

Voice memos require almost zero executive function. You press one button. You talk for ten seconds. You’re done.

Writing requires: finding the Notes app, opening it, typing, editing, deciding if the sentence is good enough. That’s five decisions. Your ADHD brain hates five decisions.

Voice memo: one decision. Your ADHD brain can handle one decision.



The Fix: Recurring Alarm

Set a phone alarm for 8 PM every day. Title it: “What did I notice today?”

When it goes off, you have two options:

Option 1: Write one sentence in your Notes app.

Option 2: Record a 10-second voice memo.

That’s it. If you can’t think of anything, write “I forgot” or “Nothing today.” That counts. You showed up to the alarm. That’s the practice.


Why this works:

External reminders compensate for internal memory failures. Your brain can’t remember to write the Souvenir on its own. That’s fine. Your phone remembers for you.

The alarm doesn’t judge you. It just asks the question. You answer however you can.




Adaptation 5: For Rejection Sensitivity (ADHD/Anxiety)

The Problem:

You want to try the Compass. But your brain is terrified of doing it wrong.

What if my Souvenir is stupid?

What if I missed the “real” wonder?

What if I’m bad at this?

What if I try and fail?

This is rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD) if you have ADHD, or perfectionism, if you have anxiety. Either way, the fear of failure is so loud that it prevents you from trying.


The Fix: There Is No Wrong

Let me be very, very clear:

The Compass doesn’t grade you.

There is no quiz.

There is no “correct” Souvenir.

There is no way to fail this.

If you noticed one thing, just one thing, you succeeded.


Examples of “successful” Souvenirs:

“I walked to the mailbox.”

“I looked at a rock for 30 seconds.”

“I tried to notice things but my brain was too loud today.”

“I saw a dog, the dog was brown.”

“The sky was gray. That’s all I remember.”

All of these count. Every single one.

The Compass doesn’t care if your Souvenir is poetic. It doesn’t care if it’s profound. It doesn’t care if it’s “good enough.”

It just cares that you showed up.



The Fix: This Is For You, Not Instagram

You’re not creating content.

You’re not trying to impress anyone.

You’re not writing for an audience.

You’re creating presence. For yourself. That’s it.

The Souvenir doesn’t have to be shareable. It doesn’t have to be beautiful. It just has to be true.

If your Souvenir is “I walked outside and hated it,” that’s a perfect Souvenir. It’s honest. It’s real. It counts.



The Fix: Celebrate Attempts, Not Outcomes

If you tried to use the Compass and it didn’t “work” - if you felt nothing, noticed nothing, gave up halfway, hated every second - that’s not failure.

That’s data.

You showed up. You tried. That’s the win.

Some days, the Compass works. Some days, it doesn’t. Both are fine.

The only failure is not trying because you’re afraid of failing. And even that’s not really failure, it’s just your brain protecting you from perceived danger (judgment, rejection, inadequacy). Your brain is doing its job. It’s just a little overzealous.

Example “Failed” Compass Loop (that actually counts as success):

“Tried to do a Compass walk today, got to the end of the driveway and felt stupid, so came back inside; at least I left the house, that’s more than yesterday.”

That’s a win. You moved. The couch didn’t win. You did.




The System: The Adaptive Compass

You’ve just read five different adaptations for five different neurodivergent challenges. Maybe you have one of these. Maybe you have all five. Maybe you’re not even sure which ones apply to you.

This is the Adaptive Compass Protocol - a meta-framework for figuring out which adaptations you specifically need and how to use them.


Step 1: Identify Your Specific Barriers

The first step is diagnosis. Not medical diagnosis - functional diagnosis. What specifically goes wrong when you try to use the Compass?

Ask yourself:

Do I struggle to start?

	∙	You read the chapter, you picked a Spark, you know what you want to do

	∙	But when it’s time to actually leave the house, you can’t

	∙	You’re stuck on the couch for hours

If yes → You need Initiation support (Adaptation 1: ADHD)


Do I get overwhelmed by sensory input?

	∙	When you go outside, the world is too loud, too bright, too much

	∙	You can’t focus on one thing because you’re noticing everything at full volume

	∙	You shut down or melt down

If yes → You need Sensory regulation (Adaptation 2: Autism)


Do I feel watched/judged when I do unusual things in public?

	∙	The idea of stopping to look at moss makes you panic

	∙	You’re convinced everyone is staring at you

	∙	You can’t relax enough to notice anything because you’re too busy managing how you appear

If yes → You need Social camouflage (Adaptation 3: Anxiety/Masking)


Do I forget the adventure by the time I get home?

	∙	You had a great experience, but by the time you’re ready to write it down, it’s gone

	∙	You can’t remember what you noticed

	∙	The Souvenir never gets written

If yes → You need External memory systems (Adaptation 4: ADHD/Anxiety)


Am I terrified of doing it wrong?

	∙	You want to try, but the fear of failure is so loud you can’t start

	∙	You’re convinced your Souvenir will be stupid or inadequate

	∙	The fear of judgment (real or imagined) prevents you from trying

If yes → You need Permission and structure (Adaptation 5: RSD/Perfectionism)


Most neurodivergent people will identify with 2-4 of these. That’s normal. That’s why the next step matters.


Step 2: Stack Your Accommodations

Here’s the secret: You can use multiple adaptations at the same time. In fact, you should use multiple adaptations at the same time.

Example Stack for someone with ADHD + Social Anxiety:

Problem: Can’t initiate, and when I do go out, I feel watched.

Solution Stack:

	∙	Use the 2-Minute Contract (initiation support)

	∙	Bring a prop (phone or notebook) for social camouflage

	∙	Record a voice memo immediately (external memory)

	∙	Go during off-peak hours (fewer people = less anxiety)

That’s four adaptations working together. Not overkill. Just matching the tool to your actual barriers.


Example Stack for someone with Autism + ADHD:

Problem: Can’t start, and when I do, sensory input overwhelms me.

Solution Stack:

	∙	Attach to existing routine (don’t create new task)

	∙	Wear noise-canceling headphones (reduce auditory input)

	∙	Use single-sense mission (visual only)

	∙	Set recurring alarm for 8 PM to write Souvenir (external memory)


Example Stack for someone with severe anxiety:

Problem: Terrified of doing it wrong and being judged.

Solution Stack:

	∙	Use Spark Menu (don’t have to generate your own)

	∙	Bring multiple props (phone, notebook, sunglasses)

	∙	Go during daylight, high-traffic times (blend in)

	∙	Give yourself permission to bail at any time (reduces pressure)

	∙	Write Souvenir as “I tried, left early, that counts”


The more barriers you have, the more adaptations you stack. That’s not weakness. That’s engineering.


Step 3: Fail Fast, Iterate

Here’s the most important part of the protocol: If one adaptation doesn’t work, try a different one.

This isn’t a test. There’s no “correct” adaptation. There’s only “does this one work for my specific brain today?”


Example iteration:

Attempt 1: 2-Minute Contract (walk to mailbox)

Result: Still can’t get off couch

Diagnosis: Initiation barrier is too high even for 2 minutes

Attempt 2: Body Doubling (call a friend, stay on phone while walking)

Result: Got to mailbox, felt good

Diagnosis: I need the social scaffolding, not just the time limit

Learning: My specific executive dysfunction responds better to body doubling than time contracts. Use body doubling going forward.


Or:


Attempt 1: Single-sense mission (visual only, headphones on)

Result: Still overwhelmed

Diagnosis: Even visual input alone is too much today

Attempt 2: Stay inside, look out window (controlled sensory environment)

Result: Noticed the tree, felt calm

Diagnosis: I need more control over sensory input than “just one sense.” Window is better.

Learning: On high-sensory days, skip outdoor missions. Window is enough.

This is troubleshooting, not failing. Every attempt gives you data about what your specific brain needs.


Step 4: The Baseline vs. Flare-Up Protocol

Your brain doesn’t work the same way every day. Some days you have more executive function, more sensory tolerance, more social bandwidth. Some days you don’t.

This is the Baseline vs. Flare-Up distinction:


Baseline Days (You’re functioning at your normal capacity):

Use your standard stack. The adaptations you’ve identified that usually work.

Example:

	∙	2-Minute Contract + Body Doubling + Voice Memo

	∙	Walk to mailbox with friend on phone, record what I notice


Flare-Up Days (ADHD symptoms worse, sensory overwhelm higher, anxiety spiking):

Use the minimum viable version. The smallest possible Compass loop.

Example:

	∙	Don’t leave the house

	∙	Look out window for 30 seconds

	∙	Notice one thing (color of the sky)

	∙	Don’t even write it down, just think it

Both are valid. Both count. The goal isn’t consistency in output. The goal is staying in relationship with wonder at whatever scale your brain allows today.


Step 5: Give Yourself Meta-Permission

The final step is this:

You’re allowed to modify the modifications.

If the 2-Minute Contract feels too long, make it 30 seconds.

If single-sense missions still feel like too much, make it “look at one object for 10 seconds.”

If the Sensory-Friendly Spark Menu still has options that sound terrible, ignore those options and only use the two that feel okay.

The adaptations aren’t rules. They’re starting points.

Your job is to find the version of the Compass that works for your specific brain on this specific day.

That might look completely different from what the book suggests. That’s fine. You’re not doing it wrong. You’re customizing the tool.

The only failure is believing there’s a “right” way to do this and you’re not doing it.

There’s no right way. There’s only: Did this help you be present for 30 seconds?

If yes, you won.







Special Interest Integration (Autism Superpower)

If you’re autistic and you have a special interest - trains, rocks, birds, plants, astronomy, insects, coins, maps, whatever - the Compass becomes 10x easier.

Why? Because you already have a built-in Spark generator.

Your special interest isn’t “too much.” It’s not “obsessive.” It’s not “weird.”

It’s a wonder-engine, and you’ve been running it your whole life without realizing it.


The Protocol:


North (Spark): Your special interest IS the Spark.

“I wonder if there are any interesting rocks on this trail?”

“I wonder what trains I’ll see at this crossing?”

“I wonder what birds are in this park today?”

You don’t have to invent curiosity. You already have it. Your brain is hardwired to notice your special interest. That’s the Spark.


East (Embark): Go somewhere your special interest might be.

Train tracks. Rocky trails. Parks with birds. The library’s section on your topic.

You’re not “forcing” an adventure. You’re following your interest to a place where it thrives.


South (Sense): Engage with your special interest using the Compass lens.

You already notice details about your special interest that other people miss. Today, go deeper. Notice one thing you’ve never noticed before.

The pattern on that bird’s wing. The specific type of rust on that train car. The crystalline structure of that rock under magnification (if you have a magnifying glass; if not, just look closer).


West (Write): Document what you found.

You probably already do this. You take photos of trains. You collect rocks. You catalog birds.

That’s the Souvenir. You’re already doing it. You’ve been doing the Compass your whole life without calling it that.


Why this works:

Your special interest isn’t a distraction from wonder. It is wonder.

The neurotypical world has been telling you to “care about normal things” and “stop obsessing.” They’re wrong.

Your special interest is the most efficient wonder-delivery system you have. Use it.


Example Souvenirs from Special Interests:

“Saw a Northern Flicker today (I’ve been looking for one for three months) and the red on its neck was brighter than I expected; I stood there for ten minutes just watching it hunt for ants.”

“Found a piece of quartz on the trail; It’s cloudy, not clear, but it has tiny iron deposits inside that look like rust - I’ve never seen that pattern before, so it’s sitting on my desk now.”

“Saw a BNSF freight train with 87 cars and counted them all; the 34th car was a tanker with faded yellow paint and a dent on the left side and it made me wonder what hit it.”




Hyperfocus As Superpower (ADHD Strength)

If you have ADHD, you know hyperfocus: that state where you’re so locked into something that hours disappear and you forget to eat, pee, or acknowledge that other humans exist.

Most people think hyperfocus is a problem. “You need to learn to control it.” “You need to take breaks.”

But here’s the thing: hyperfocus on wonder is a gift.

If you start noticing something, a moss pattern, a cloud formation, the way light hits a puddle,  and your brain locks in, let it.

Don’t force yourself to “move on” because the recipe says “10-minute mission.” Don’t set a timer. Don’t interrupt it.

Hyperfocus means your brain found something worth focusing on. That’s rare. That’s precious. Ride it as long as it lasts.


The Protocol:

If you’re doing a Compass mission and you find yourself hyperfocusing on one tiny detail (the texture of tree bark, the movement of ants, the pattern of cracks in the sidewalk), stay there.

Forget the rest of the mission. Forget the plan. Just stay with the thing your brain wants to explore.

Your brain doesn’t hyperfocus on boring things. It only hyperfocuses on things it finds genuinely interesting. Trust it.


Why this works:

Hyperfocus is your brain’s way of saying “this matters to me.” It’s not a bug. It’s a feature.

When you hyperfocus on wonder, you’re doing the Compass at expert level. You’re not skimming the surface. You’re going deep. That’s mastery.


Example Hyperfocus Souvenir:

“Spent 90 minutes watching ants carry a dead beetle across the driveway and noticed they worked in shifts: Some pulled, Some pushed; they stopped twice to regroup and figure out a new route when they hit an obstacle (a stick) - I forgot I was supposed to be walking to the mailbox, the ants were more interesting.”

That’s not failure. That’s success. Your brain found wonder and stayed with it. That’s the whole point.




The Sensory-Friendly Spark Menu

If you’re autistic or have sensory processing issues, the regular Spark Menu in the Appendix might have options that sound terrible to you. (“Touch a tree” = nope, bark texture is Bad. “Listen to bird calls” = nope, too many sounds at once.)

So here’s a sensory-friendly Spark Menu. All of these are low-stimulation, single-sense, and designed for brains that need gentle inputs.


Visual Sparks (no touching, no loud noises):

“I wonder what shapes the clouds are making?”

“I wonder how many different shades of green I can see in this one tree?”

“I wonder what the light looks like through that window?”

“I wonder if any of the cars parked on this street are the same color as mine?”


Tactile Sparks (safe textures only):

“I wonder what this smooth rock feels like compared to that smooth rock?”

“I wonder if the railing is warmer or cooler than the bench?”

“I wonder what my jacket feels like if I pay attention to it?”


Auditory Sparks (single sounds, not ambient chaos):

“I wonder if I can hear a bird call if I close my eyes and ignore all the other noise?”

“I wonder what sound my shoes make on different surfaces (concrete vs. grass vs. gravel)?”

“I wonder how long it takes the wind chimes to stop moving after the wind dies down?”


Movement Sparks (proprioception-friendly):

“I wonder how it feels to walk backwards for 10 steps?”

“I wonder if I can balance on the curb without falling off?”

“I wonder how many steps it takes to get from my front door to the mailbox?”


Safe-Space Sparks (for when going outside is too much):

“I wonder what I can see from my window if I look for 60 seconds?”

“I wonder how the light changes in my room between morning and afternoon?”

“I wonder what’s the most interesting object on my desk if I study it closely?”


Pick one. That’s your Spark. You don’t have to generate your own. You definitely don’t have to pick the ones that sound overwhelming.




The Neurodivergent Permission Slip

You are allowed to:

Stay inside and look out the window (that counts)

There is no “correct” way to do this. There’s only: Did this help you be present for 2 minutes?
If yes, you succeeded.

If no, that’s data. Try a different adaptation tomorrow.

The Compass isn’t a test. It’s a tool. And tools are meant to be modified for the person using them.

Your brain is different. That’s not a flaw. That’s a variable.

Not because the world beat you. Because you beat yourself.





The Tragedy of Internalized Ableism

You have a brain that works differently. That brain needs different tools. That’s not a character flaw. That’s just… hardware.

But the world is built for neurotypical brains. Productivity systems, social norms, self-help books - all designed for brains that can:

	∙	Initiate tasks without external structure

	∙	Filter sensory input automatically

A Mac program doesn’t run on a PC. That doesn’t mean the PC is broken. It means the software isn’t compatible.

But if you spend your whole life believing that needing different software means you’re defective, you’ll never look for the software that actually works.

You’ll just keep trying to run Mac programs on your PC and wondering why you keep crashing.
