Chapter 6A: North = Notice








Stop reading. Look up. Find the nearest door.

Now: Is the doorknob on the left or the right?

If you had to guess - and you do, because you don’t actually know - which way would you say?

Now go look.

You’ve walked through that door six hundred times. You’ve never seen it.

This is what the Rut does. It doesn’t just make the world boring. It makes you blind to it.


There’s a famous psychological study from Harvard University, conducted by Christopher Chabris and Daniel Simons in 1999, that effectively ruins everyone’s confidence in their own eyes.

Researchers asked participants to watch a short video of two teams of people, one in white shirts, one in black, passing basketballs around. The participants were given one specific, high-focus job: Count the passes made by the team in white.

It requires focus. You have to watch closely. The balls move fast. One pass, two passes, three passes…

Halfway through the video, a person in a full-body gorilla suit walks into the center of the frame, faces the camera, beats their chest, and walks oﬀ. The gorilla is on screen for a full nine seconds. It’s massive, hairy, and impossible to miss.

Except, when the video ended and the researchers asked, "Did you see anything unusual?",
50% of the participants said no.

They never saw the gorilla.

These people weren't stupid. They weren't blind. They were just looking for basketballs. Because their brains were hyper-focused on the task at hand (efficiency), they became legally blind to the absurdity right in front of them.

This is the scariest and most liberating fact about your brain, and the foundational principle of finding North: You don’t see what’s there; you see what you’re looking for.

If you’re looking for "tasks," "mess," and "problems," that’s literally all you’ll see. You’ll miss the gorilla. You’ll miss the sunset. You will miss your life.


The Science: Your Brain’s Spam Filter

Why does this happen? It isn't a glitch; it's a feature.

Right now, your socks are touching your ankles. The refrigerator is humming. There’s a tiny draft coming from somewhere you can’t identify. The light has a subtle flicker you’ve been ignoring for three months.

Your brain deleted all of this until I just mentioned it. Now you can’t unfeel your socks.

That’s your RAS - your brain’s spam filter. It decides what’s “important” and deletes the rest. And right now, it thinks “same old stuff” is spam.

The Wonder Compass is how you change the filter settings.

Neuroscientists estimate that your senses are bombarded with approximately 11 million bits of information every single second. The light hitting your retinas, the hum of the refrigerator, the pressure of your socks against your ankles, the smell of old coffee.

However, your conscious mind can only process about 40 to 50 bits per second.

That’s a massive data gap. If your brain tried to process everything, you would overheat and crash in less than a minute. So, you have a filter. It sits at the base of your brainstem, and it’s called the Reticular Activating System (RAS).

The RAS is the Caveman’s favorite tool. It acts like a spam filter for your reality. It deletes 99.999% of the world before it ever reaches your conscious mind. It only lets in things that are "Dangerous," "Urgent," or "Important."

When you’re in the Rut, your RAS decides that "Same Old Stuﬀ" is spam.

The route you drive to work? Spam. Deleted. (This is why you can drive home and not remember a single turn).

The clutter on the dining table? Spam. Deleted.

Your spouse’s face while they tell a story you’ve heard before? Spam. Deleted.

I’ve called this Monotony Blindness earlier in the book. You aren't bored because the world is boring. You’re bored because you’re technically blind to it.

The RAS doesn't just filter; it confirms bias. If you believe the world is awful, the RAS only lets in awful news to prove you right.


To find North, to Notice, we don't need to change the world. We just need to update the search settings on your RAS. But updating the settings isn’t just a fun trick. It’s clinically proven medicine. Let me show you the data.
Why You Missed the Gorilla


The ROI of Noticing: Why This Is Medicine

Now, The Caveman will try to tell you that "Noticing" is cute. He’ll say it’s a nice little hobby for people who have too much time, or for poets who don't have real jobs and get eaten.

He’s wrong. The clinical data suggests that the simple act of Noticing is one of the most potent mental health interventions available.

We aren't guessing here. Let’s look at the receipts.


The "Awe Walk" Effect (The UCSF Study)

In a groundbreaking 2020 study led by Dr. Virginia Sturm at UCSF, researchers studied older adults for eight weeks. One group took a normal weekly walk. The other group took a 15-minute "Awe Walk," where they were instructed to actively look for things that amazed them.

The Result: The "Awe" group reported significantly higher positive emotions and lower distress in their daily lives than the walkers who just exercised.

The Selfie Metric: Interestingly, the researchers analyzed the selfies participants took. Over the 8 weeks, the "Awe" group literally made themselves smaller in the frame to show more of the background. Their egos shrank, and their smiles grew measurably wider.

That’s the long-term emotional benefit. But what about immediate, daily relief?


The Curiosity Dividend (28% Boost)

As we discussed in Chapter 4, research by Dr. Todd Kashdan, a leading expert on curiosity, found that actively cultivating curiosity, the core mechanism of "Noticing", doesn't just make you smarter; it makes you happier. In studies using the Curiosity and Exploration Inventory, high curiosity was linked to a 28% increase in overall well-being and a massive reduction in anxiety. When you’re curious, your brain is too busy asking questions to be anxious.

So curiosity makes you happier. But it also changes how your brain rewards you in real-time.


The "Three Good Things" Persistence

The father of Positive Psychology, Martin Seligman, tested an intervention called "Three Good Things," which is essentially a Noticing drill. Participants wrote down three things that went well each day for just one week.

The Result: Happiness increased and depressive symptoms decreased not just for that week, but for six months afterward.

Why? Because 7 days of training was enough to permanently recalibrate their RAS. They taught their brains to hunt for the good, and the brain kept doing it on autopilot.


The Dopamine of Novelty

When you notice something new, a "Prediction Error", your brain activates the Substantia Nigra/Ventral Tegmental Area (SN/VTA). This releases a rush of dopamine. This isn't just a "reward"; it is the biological signal for motivation.

In the Rut, your dopamine is flat.

When you Notice, you’re self-administering a micro-dose of motivation. This helps with the next step of the Compass, Embarking or starting.

So, when you pause to look at a weird shadow or a new town name, tell The Caveman: "I’m not wasting time. I’m increasing my psychological well-being by 28% and recalibrating my dopamine receptors."

And that dopamine hit doesn’t just feel good, it makes you magnetically attractive to others.


      5. The Curiosity Attraction

If you're worried that asking questions makes you look ignorant or annoying, the science says the opposite. Research by Dr. Todd Kashdan found that curious people aren't just happier; they’re magnetic.

In studies where strangers were paired up for conversation, people with high curiosity were consistently rated as more socially attractive and more interesting by their partners. Think about that. They weren't rated as interesting because they said interesting things. They were rated as interesting because they were interested.

When you use the Compass to Notice someone, when you genuinely wonder "What’s their story?", you aren't just gathering data. You’re signaling presence. And in a world of distracted people, presence is the most attractive quality you can have.

But beyond the chemistry and the social science, there’s one more thing Noticing does that no study can measure.


6. The Reality Snap

This benefit isn't in the charts. It’s the snap of connection.

When you truly lock eyes with a bird, or run your hand over rough brick, or let the smell of rain on asphalt hit your lungs, you aren't just recording the world. You’re colliding with it. You’re completing a circuit.

The world isn't just a backdrop anymore; it’s alive, and it presses back against your senses. In that split second of true attention, you and the object create a third thing: a moment of undeniable reality. You stop being a ghost passing through walls, and you become solid. The world becomes real because you witnessed it, and you become real because you felt it.

That’s the promise. But how do you actually do it? How do you reprogram a filter you didn’t know was running?

You ask a question.


The ROI of Noticing


The Tool: "I Wonder..."

The RAS is programmable. You can change its settings instantly by asking a question.

You’ve experienced this phenomenon before. It’s called the Frequency Illusion (or the Baader-Meinhof phenomenon). You decide to buy a Chevy Trailblazer. Suddenly, you see Chevy Trailblazers everywhere. They were always there, but yesterday they were spam. Today, because you tagged them as "Important," your RAS is letting them through.

To trigger this effect on demand, we use the phrase: "I wonder..."

Psychologist George Loewenstein coined the term "The Curiosity Gap." He described curiosity as an itch, a gap between what you know and what you want to know. When you say "I wonder” and run the Compass, you scratch that itch.

This phrase is the manual override code. It switches your brain from "Passive Consumer" to "Active Hunter." It tells the Caveman: "Hey, let that gorilla in. I want to see it."

But what exactly are we looking for? We’re looking for a Spark.

A Spark is a specific target. Crucially, the Spark provides the Goal of your adventure.

If you’re just "looking," that’s passive mindfulness (still good!). But the Wonder Compass is about active adventure. So, when you Notice something, you’re actually identifying the mission.

If you Notice a "Diamond Painting Kit" at the craft store, the Goal is to buy it and open it.

If you Notice a weird trail, the Goal is to walk to the end of it.

And this works for beauty, yes - but also for darkness. Wonder isn’t just rainbows. It’s rust. It’s decay. It’s the strange sadness of an abandoned building. We’ll talk about that in a moment.


Let’s prove it works. Right now.

Pick a color - electric blue, burnt orange, whatever. Look around your current room and count how many times you see it.

Go.




How many did you find? Three? Seven? Here’s the thing: they were always there. You just weren’t looking for them.

Your RAS just woke up.


Case Study: The Waterville Mission

Let me give you a real-life example from our own lives that we’ve already gone over so you get a deeper understanding.

One Sunday, we felt the itch to get out of the house. We knew there was a town nearby called Waterville.

At first, the "Spark" was too vague: "I wonder what’s in Waterville to see?"

That’s a start, but it’s weak. Vague plans get crushed by The Caveman because they feel aimless. "Just driving around" sounds exhausting. So, we refined the Spark. We used Google Maps and Yelp to get specific.

Spark 1: "I wonder if Erika’s is as good as Yelp seems to say for brunch?"

Spark 2: "I wonder if we can find those generic Block Tech Lego succulents at the 5 Below store?"

Notice the shift. We weren't just "going to Waterville" anymore. We had Targets. We had a mission: Secure the brunch, secure the succulents.

This transformed a vague, meandering day trip into a structured adventure with clear win conditions. The "I Wonder" question gave us the map “X”.

I need to address something before we go further. Because not every Spark is going to be a sunny diner or a cute succulent. Sometimes the Compass points toward rust, decay, and sadness. And that’s not a failure - it’s a different kind of wonder.

But what happens when you see something that isn’t "nice"?

What happens when your Compass points you toward a dead bird on the sidewalk, or a boarded-up storefront, or a pile of rusted scrap metal?

The instinct of the Efficiency Trap is to look away. “Ugly. Sad. Gross. Delete.” The Caveman in your brain wants to filter out the decay because it reminds us that things end.

But the Wonder Compass isn’t a filter for "Pretty Things." It’s a lens for Real Things.


Shadow Wonder (The Art of Mono no aware)

There’s a distinct flavor of wonder that doesn't feel like a sugar rush. It tastes more like a deep, dark red wine. I call it Shadow Wonder.

The Japanese have a beautiful phrase for this: Mono no aware (moh-no no ah-wah-ray). Roughly translated, it means "the pathos of things," or a sensitivity to the passing of time. It’s the bittersweet feeling you get when you see a flower falling apart, or an abandoned house being swallowed by ivy.

It isn't "happy," but it is deeply, profoundly beautiful.

If you only use the Compass to look for sunshine and rainbows, you’re missing half the picture. You’re also setting yourself up to fail on the days when you feel lousy. Sometimes, you don't want to look at a cheerful daffodil. Sometimes, your mood matches the gray sky.

Shadow Wonder gives you permission to harmonize with the gray.


How to Wonder in the Dark

When you encounter something broken, old, or decaying, don't avert your eyes. Lean in. Use the "I Wonder" tool to honor the history of the object.

The Dead Bird: Instead of "Gross," try: "I wonder what its last flight looked like? I wonder how long it will take for the earth to reclaim these feathers?"

The Closed Restaurant: Instead of "What an eyesore," try: "I wonder how many birthday dinners happened at those tables? I wonder who locked that door for the very last time, and what they felt when they walked away?"

The Rusted Car: "I wonder who drove this when it was brand new and shiny? I wonder where they were going?" When you wonder about the rusted car, you aren't just looking at junk. You’re looking at the ghost of a road trip. You’re witnessing the evidence that someone lived, traveled, and loved before you got here. The rust isn't a mistake; it's a memory made physical. You aren't alone in the timeline.


Do you feel the difference?

"Regular" Wonder expands you with joy. "Shadow" Wonder deepens you with empathy. It connects you to the cycle of life. It reminds you that everything is temporary, which makes this moment, right now, infinitely more valuable.

So, if you’re having a bad day, or if the world feels heavy, don't try to force a smile. Go find something broken. Go find a crack in the sidewalk or a tree that lost the fight with a storm. Stand there. Witness it. Wonder about it.

You aren't wallowing. You’re paying your respects to the passage of time. And that’s a hell of an adventure.


Turning Shadows into Journeys

Now, you might be thinking: "Okay, I’m wondering about this abandoned building. But where do I go? Do I just stand here and feel sad? That’s not an adventure."

Great point. Remember, North (Notice) is just the ignition. East (Embark) is the drive.

When your spark is "Shadow Wonder," your adventure could take one of many forms, for example, The Mystery or The Tribute.


The Mystery Mission (The Detective)

If you Notice something abandoned or forgotten, your adventure becomes finding out why.

The Spark: You wonder about the boarded-up storefront that used to be a bustling bakery.

The Adventure: Go to the local library (or use your phone on a nearby bench) to find an old photograph of this street from 50 years ago. Match the old photo to the current reality. See the ghosts of what used to be there.

The Destination: The Local History section, or a specific historical marker.


The Tribute Mission (The Time Traveler)

If you Notice something broken or "past its prime," your adventure is to find beauty in similar things.

The Spark: You wonder about a rusted, beat-up car from the 80s.

The Adventure: Go to a thrift store or antique shop. Your mission is to find one object that feels like it has a secret history. Hold it. Guess who owned it.

The Destination: The Goodwill, the cemetery (for a history walk), or an "Old Town" district.


The Mood Match (The Vibe Check)

Sometimes, the "Shadow" isn't an object; it's just the weather or your own gray mood.

The Spark: You wonder why the rain makes the streetlights look so cinematic.

The Adventure: Lean into the noir. Go to a diner or a coffee shop that feels "out of time", the kind with vinyl booths and old waitresses. Order black coffee. Listen to jazz or rain sounds.

Write your souvenir in a corner booth like a brooding poet.

The Destination: The "uncool" diner, the empty park bench, the back of the bus.


The "Shadow" Adventure Recipe:

When you plan this trip in the next step (East), your "Delight" probably won't be upbeat pop music or a sugary snack.

Destination: The Antique Store.

Delight: A thermos of hot tea and a melancholic playlist (Bon Iver, anyone?).

Definition: 45 minutes of browsing without buying.

See? You aren't wallowing. You’re investigating. You’re taking your sadness or your curiosity about the past and turning it into a quest for connection.


Summary of the Logic (For your own notes):

Notice (North): "I wonder who lived in that empty house?"

Embark (East): "I am going to walk to the cemetery/library/historical plaque to see the names of people who lived here in 1920."

Sense (South): "I am running my fingers over the moss on the stone/smelling the old paper."

You’ve learned how to reprogram your filter. You’ve learned to look for beauty in decay. Now let’s talk about where to actually find these Sparks, because the world is full of them, but they hide in two very different places.

You can find a Spark (and a Goal) Here (The Immediate World) and There (The Conceptual World).


Distance 1: The Microscope (Finding a Spark HERE)

The Caveman wants you to believe that adventure only happens in Italy. He wants you to believe that your current location, the grocery store, the office, the living room, is devoid of magic.

He’s lying. The world is fractal; there’s wonder at every scale, if you zoom in.

To find a Spark Here, try the 5-Foot Rule: What can I notice without moving my feet?

You’re looking for the "glitch in the matrix." You’re trying to achieve what organizational psychologist Adam Grant calls "Vuja De" - the opposite of Deja Vu. Deja Vu is when something new feels familiar. Vuja De is when something familiar suddenly feels new, like when you look at your kitchen and realize you’ve never actually looked at the light fixture. You want to look at something familiar and see it as if it were the first time.


At the Grocery Store (The $3 Trip Abroad)

Usually, you’re in "Hunter-Gatherer" mode. Get the milk, get the eggs, get out.

The Shift: Go to the "International" aisle or the deep produce section. This is a portal. You’re surrounded by labels in languages you don't speak and ingredients you've never tasted.

The Spark: You’re standing in the International aisle, surrounded by labels in languages you can’t read. There’s a jar of something yellow and wrinkled - pickled mango, maybe? The label has a picture of a grandmother stirring a pot. You wonder: What does this taste like? Who taught her to make this? How did it travel 8,000 miles to sit on this fluorescent shelf for $2.99? "I wonder what that jar of pickled mango actually tastes like?"

The Goal: Buy it and eat it.

Why it works: For $3, you’re engaging with a culture that’s thousands of miles away. You’ve broken the monotony of "Cheerios and Milk." It might taste terrible. That’s okay. The point isn't the flavor; the point is the surprise. Stop for a second and realize: That jar of pickled mango traveled 3,000 miles to be here. A farmer grew it. A truck driver hauled it. A ship carried it across an ocean. It’s a miracle of logistics sitting on a shelf for $3. When you look at it with Wonder, you aren't just seeing a condiment; you're seeing the fingerprints of a thousand strangers.


At Work (The Archaeology of the Office)

Office buildings are designed to be ignored. They’re beige for a reason. But people live there.

The Shift: Stop looking at the screens and look at the "artifacts." Look at the collection of mugs in the breakroom. Look at the photos on your coworker's desk (that you've walked past for three years and never actually seen).

The Spark: "I wonder why Bob has a bronze bowling pin on his desk that says Altoona Lanes 1987?”

The Goal: Ask Bob if he was a league champion or if he stole it from his dad’s basement.

Why it works: You’re turning your sterile environment into a museum of human behavior. But more than that, you’re breaking the "Professional Fourth Wall." That trophy isn’t office equipment; it’s proof that Bob had a moment of glory, that he has a pulse and a history outside of these spreadsheets. When you ask about the story, you stop seeing a "Co-Worker" avatar and you start seeing the human hiding inside the cubicle. You turn a transaction into a connection.


At Home (The Light Hunt)

This is the hardest place to Notice, because your Monotony Blindness is strongest here. You don't see your house; you just see your chores.

The Shift: Ignore the mess. Look at the light. Light is the only thing in your house that changes every hour.

The Spark: "I wonder why the sun hits the spine of that Terry Pratchett paperback at exactly 4:17 PM and turns it gold?"

The Goal: Sit in that specific patch of sun for five minutes.

Why it works: It forces you to interact with your home as a space, not a to-do list. But deeper than that, it connects you to the cosmos. That beam of light traveled 93 million miles through the vacuum of space just to hit your dusty bookshelf. It shifts every minute because the Earth is actually turning under your feet. For five minutes, your living room isn't just a messy box; it’s a sundial. You aren't watching dust; you’re watching the planet spin.

But sometimes the Spark isn’t in arm’s reach. Sometimes it’s 20 miles away, hiding in a town you’ve never heard of. That’s when you need to hunt at a different scale. Plus, it’s fun!


Distance 2: Digital Dowsing (Finding a Spark via Maps)

I told you that your phone is the enemy, a "Scroll of Numbness." And it can be. But it can also be a miraculous tool - if you use it as a tool, not a pacifier.

You can use Google Maps or Apple Maps to find a Spark in the wider world without leaving your couch. (You can also use travel websites, blogs, subreddits, yelp, etc.)


Method 1: The Green Blob Hunt


Open your map app. Zoom out slightly from your house, maybe a 5-mile radius. Look for the "Green Blobs." These represent parks, forests, conservation land, or cemeteries.

The Scan: Look for a green blob you’ve driven past 1,000 times but never stepped foot in. Look for the "dead space" on the map, the roads that just end.

The Spark: "I wonder if there are actual trails in that weird patch of conservation land behind Roosevelt High School, or if it’s just where kids go to smoke?"

Target Acquired: Drop a pin.


Method 2: The "Town Name" Zoom

Zoom out further, maybe a 30-minute drive radius. Scan the names of the towns or neighborhoods. Your brain usually filters these out as "places I drive through to get to Target."

The Scan: Look for a name that sounds weird, or a town you’ve never actually stopped in.

The Spark: "I wonder what downtown 'Pignut' actually looks like?" (Yes, that’s a real place). "I wonder if they have a coffee shop?"

Target Acquired: Drop a pin.


Method 3: The Adjective Search

Don't search for "Coffee" or "Gas." Those are utility searches. Search for intrigue. Type these words into the Google Maps search bar and see what pops up within 20 miles:

"Historic" / "Ruins" / "Oldest"

"Lookout" / "Scenic"

"Odd" / "Haunted"

The Spark: "I wonder what the Katahdin Iron Works Historic Site actually looks like - and why it has a 1.5-star review on Google Maps even though it’s rated at 4.6 stars?"


Method 4: The Satellite Scan

Switch your map to "Satellite View." Scroll over your own neighborhood or town. You’re looking for anomalies - weird shapes, bright colors, or hidden alleys.

The Scan: Look for a bright turquoise pool surrounded by pine trees (abandoned motel?), a perfect circular concrete pad in the middle of nowhere (old missile silo?), or a warehouse roof covered in what looks like a 40-foot mural of a dragon.

The Spark: "I wonder what that weird structure is behind the factory?"

The Tug: How to Choose

You’ve scanned the list. You have ten possible Sparks. How do you pick one?

You feel for The Tug.

Noticing isn't just an intellectual exercise; it’s a somatic (body-based) feeling. Neuroscientist Antonio Damasio calls this the "Somatic Marker Hypothesis." He argues that our bodies process emotional data faster than our conscious minds. Before you logically know why you like something, your gut has already decided.

When you look at that spiky fruit, or you see the town name "Mystic," pay attention to your body.

The Should: Feels heavy. It feels like "I should go to the museum because it's educational." (This is The Caveman).

The Tug: Feels light. It feels like a tiny internal "Huh?" or a smirk. It feels like a magnetic pull.

Close your eyes. Think about “doing laundry.” Notice the heaviness in your chest. That’s a Should.

Now think about “walking to the weird blue mailbox I’ve never really looked at.” Notice the tiny lift, the smirk trying to form. That’s the Tug.

The Tug feels like leaning forward. The Should feels like leaning back.

That’s your resonance. That’s your North. If the AI gives you five destinations for your trip to Waterville, and four feel boring, don't go. You’re hunting for the dopamine hit of curiosity. Trust the Tug.

You’ve found your Spark. You’ve felt the Tug. Now comes the hardest part: putting the phone away before it eats your adventure.
