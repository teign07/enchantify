Chapter 26: Conclusion




If you use the Wonder Compass - even imperfectly, even sporadically, even badly - you’ll collect moments.

Moments when you were awake instead of asleep. Moments when you saw the thing instead of the label. Moments when you felt your body instead of just living in your head.

And when you’re lying in bed at the end of your life, when you’re doing that final accounting of what you did with your one wild and precious existence, you won’t remember the emails you sent or the meetings you sat through or the hours you spent scrolling.

You’ll remember the moments.

Your kids leading you around the block. The weird mailbox. The sound of your partner laughing in the car. The smooth stone. Your friend gossiping. The way the light looked that one Tuesday. The taste of the donut. The cold wind on the bridge.

You’ll remember that you were here. That you paid attention. That you didn’t let the Rut win. That’s not everything. But it’s not nothing.

It’s proof you lived.

The smooth stone you picked up from the trail has been sitting on your desk for six months. It’s cold and heavy. When you finally pick it up again, your thumb finds the groove worn smooth by some river. The stone doesn’t hold the memory - you do. But touching it kicks everything loose: the cold wind, the sound of water, the exact moment you bent down and chose this one. The stone is just a stone. But it’s your stone now.

The compass isn’t a thing you hold. It’s a question you ask. And the question - I wonder? - has been in your chest since you were a kid, before you learned to ignore it. It’s still there. It never left. It’s just been buried under decades of ‘hurry up’ and ‘be realistic’ and ‘stop daydreaming.’ But it’s loud when you let it out. It wants to know things. It wants to look at stuff. It wants to poke around and find out what happens. Today’s the day you let it. The world is out there doing its thing. The trees are growing. The clouds are moving. Even the weird mailbox is standing there being weird. They’re not waiting for you. They’re just being themselves. And you’re ready to notice.

It’s not perfect. It’s not magic. It’s not a cure. It’s just a tool.

But tools work if you use them.

So use it. Break it. Modify it. Make it yours.

And when it works, when you’re standing in your kitchen on a random Thursday and you realize you just spent five minutes watching the steam rise from your coffee and you forgot to be anxious, capture it. Write it down. Send it to someone you trust. Post it in The Clubhouse. Because Wonder is contagious.

When you start doing this - when you start stopping for the moss, noticing the clouds, hunting for weird mailboxes - you’re not just changing your own life. You’re showing the people around you that it’s possible to be an adult and still be curious. That it’s possible to have responsibilities and still have a sense of play. That it’s possible to be tired and still be awake.

You become evidence that there’s another way to live.

You might not save the world, but you’ll save yourself.


I’ve spent this whole book telling you that the Compass is a tool for the present moment. I’ve told you it’s about surviving Tuesday. I’ve told you it’s about finding a moment of peace in the traffic jam.

And that’s true.

But there’s a secondary effect, a long-term side effect, that I haven’t mentioned yet. 

When you use the Compass for a week, you feel relief. When you use it for a month, you feel a rhythm. But when you use it for a year, you get something else entirely: Data.

Your notebook (or notes app) has been collecting these sentences for months - scribbled on napkins, typed into your phone at 2 AM, scratched in the margins of receipts. Some of them are profound. Most of them are just ‘the moss was really green today.’ The notebook doesn’t rank them. It just keeps them. And when you flip back through, you’ll see it: proof you were awake. Proof you were here.


Think about your Souvenirs. If you collect 100 sentences about what made you feel alive, you aren’t  guessing who you are. You have a dataset.

You might look back at your Souvenirs and realize:

“Weird. Every single time I felt a spark of joy, I was making something with my hands.” 

Or: “Every time I felt truly at peace, I was alone in the quiet, not at the loud parties I force myself to go to.”

Or: “Every time I wrote a souvenir about my job, it was about leaving it.”

The Rut thrives on vagueness. It convinces you that you’re generally unhappy for no specific reason. But the Compass creates specificity. It highlights the discrepancy between the life you’re living and the life that actually lights you up.

But the Souvenirs do something deeper than reveal preferences. They edit your story.

Narrative therapists have a term for this. They say we are the stories we tell about ourselves - and the Rut writes a very specific story. It says: I am tired. I am boring. Nothing interesting happens to me. I am the kind of person who watches life from the couch.

The Souvenirs contradict that story. Not with affirmations. Not with positive thinking. With evidence.

You can’t hold the belief “I am a boring person” and the sentence “Yesterday I spent ten minutes studying the gargoyles on the building I’ve walked past for two years” in the same hand. One of them has to go. And the sentence is written down. The belief is not.

After thirty Souvenirs, you don’t just have a collection of nice moments. You have irrefutable proof that you are a person who notices things. A person who stops and looks. A person who writes sentences about frost and bread and the way the light hit the counter on a Tuesday.

That’s not a memory aid. That’s a new identity, built one sentence at a time, in your own handwriting, in evidence you gathered yourself. The Rut can argue with your feelings. It can’t argue with your notebook.

Eventually, the friction becomes too loud to ignore.

You can’t keep noticing that you love silence and then keep working in a noisy factory forever. You can’t keep noticing that you love the woods and stay trapped in a concrete city forever.

The "Noticing" eventually demands action.

This is how the micro becomes the macro.

You don’t change your life by sitting on the couch and thinking really hard about your "Five Year Plan." You change your life by noticing what you love, thirty seconds at a time, until the evidence is so overwhelming that you have to move toward it.

The Compass starts as a survival tool. But if you keep spinning it, it becomes a navigation tool. It stops pointing to "The Pretty Leaf" and starts pointing to "The Life You Were Meant to Live." First, it wakes you up. Then, it walks you home.



That’s the long-term trajectory, from survival tool to navigation tool to life compass.

But there’s one more layer I haven’t told you about yet. A secret objective that’s been hidden in the design of this whole system.

The goal isn’t for you to use the Wonder Compass forever.

The goal’s for you to lose it.

Right now, you might be worried that I’m asking you to carry a clipboard around for the rest of your life. The Caveman is probably whispering, “Great, another chore. Another thing I have to maintain.”

But that’s not how neuroplasticity works. The Compass isn’t a lifestyle; it’s a set of training wheels. And if the science holds up, here is the exact trajectory of how this tool will grow with you - and eventually, into you.


Phase 1: The Prosthetic Phase (Weeks 1–4)

Right now, you’re in the manual phase. You’re relying 100% on the external brain of the book. You’re tired, your decision-making battery is dead, and you need the "Spark Menu" to tell you exactly what to do.

This feels mechanical. It feels like a game. It might even feel like a chore. That’s normal. You’re manually operating a system that’s supposed to be automatic. You’re bypassing decision fatigue by following a direct order (“Find a red object”), which jump-starts your Reticular Activating System (RAS) when it’s stuck in “ignore” mode.


Phase 2: The Internalization Phase (Months 2–6)

Somewhere around month two or three, a shift happens. You’ll be standing in line at the grocery store, and before you even reach for your phone, your brain will automatically ask: “I wonder what the oldest thing on this shelf is?”

You didn’t prompt it. You didn’t look at the worksheet. Your brain just… did it.

This is the wiring taking hold. Neurons that fire together, wire together. Because you’ve spent months manually strengthening the neural pathways for curiosity, your brain is starting to default to “Wonder” instead of “Worry.” The “West” step (writing the souvenir) has trained your hippocampus to tag positive sensory data as “important” for long-term storage. You aren’t doing the Compass anymore; you’re just being an observant person.


Phase 3: The Trait Phase (Year 1+)

Psychologists distinguish between State Curiosity (I am curious right now because I saw something weird) and Trait Curiosity (I am a curious person generally).

The ultimate goal of this book isn’t to give you State Curiosity. It’s to restore your Trait Curiosity.

I didn’t realize this had happened to me until I wrote this book.

I sat down over Thanksgiving weekend to put the Compass into words. I had an outline. I had my research. I had a plan to write a practical, science-backed guide with clear instructions and numbered steps.

And then I wrote about a coat that wants to go outside.

I wrote about moss that drinks rain. About a sourdough starter that doesn’t care about your Instagram metrics. About a mug holding onto heat like a memory. About a blue chair in a hospital room that holds you without asking questions.

I didn’t plan any of that. I wasn’t consulting the Compass worksheet. I wasn’t thinking “Now I’ll use the animistic voice technique from Chapter 8B.” I was just writing about the world the way I see it now. And the way I see it now is: everything is doing something. Everything has a quality of aliveness that I can’t not notice.

I wrote 80,000 words in four days. And somewhere around hour thirty, sitting in the recliner with Ron on my lap and Hermione judging me from the floor at my feet, I realized what had happened.

The Compass was gone.

Not lost. Not forgotten. Absorbed. It had sunk so far into the way I think and see and move through a room that I couldn’t find the edges of it anymore. I couldn’t tell where the tool ended and I began. The training wheels hadn’t just come off, they’d dissolved into my legs.

I was writing a book about how to see the world as alive, and I couldn’t stop seeing the world as alive long enough to write a single paragraph without giving a mailbox a personality. The tool had done its job so well that it had made itself invisible.

That’s Phase 3. Not a dramatic moment of enlightenment. Not a sunrise on a mountaintop. Just a guy in an armchair at 5:37 PM realizing that the thing he built to save himself had quietly become the way he sees everything - and that he’d been using it without knowing it for so long that the proof was 400 pages of evidence sitting in his lap.

The Compass is designed to make itself obsolete. It’s a bridge back to the person you were before the exhaustion took over. And once you’re across the bridge, you don’t need to carry it with you.

It’s been pointing you toward now this whole time - not toward some future perfect version of yourself, not toward Bali, just toward the kitchen window and the weird light on Saturday. And when you don’t need it anymore, when you’re just walking around noticing stuff automatically, the Compass will sit on your shelf collecting dust. And that’s fine. That’s the point. Tools don’t need applause. They just need to work.

That’s how the Compass disappears: you don’t need the map because you’ve become the kind of person who sees the territory.






Before we say goodbye, I want to leave you with one last piece of the science. The one that changed everything for me.

There’s a misconception that your eyes are cameras. We think we walk around recording a reality that’s objectively there, and if we’re bored, it’s because the movie playing in front of us is boring.

But neuroscience tells us that this is backward. And so is the boredom.

The world isn’t boring. It’s strange. Non-human. The tree outside your window is doing something genuinely alien, completely independent of whether you ever look at it. The reality is there. It was always there. But your brain doesn’t record it. It constructs your experience of it.

Neuroscientists call this Top-Down Processing. Your senses take in raw data (light, sound, pressure), but that data is noisy and incomplete. To make sense of it, your brain projects its own expectations, memories, and beliefs onto the raw data to create the image you “see.”

Leading neuroscientist Anil Seth calls this “Controlled Hallucination.” He argues that we’re always hallucinating our experience of reality, but when we agree on the hallucination, we call it “reality.”

Which means the tree is real. And your experience of it is constructed. Both things are true simultaneously. 

The Compass teaches you that you aren’t the camera. You’re the projector.

The beam of light is your attention. Wherever you point it, the world resolves into high definition. Point it at the worry, and the world becomes a cage. Point it at the moss, or the stranger, or the weird light on the wall, and the world becomes a gallery. Don’t point it at the tree, and that tree never exists for you, experientially; it’s not part of your movie.

You aren’t passively watching your life. You’re lighting it up, frame by frame, with the power of your own attention. You decide what makes the final cut.

We’re all constructing our experience of the same strange, real world. The question isn’t which construction is objectively true - the Rut or a living world. It’s which one does justice to what’s actually there. The evidence, scientific and lived, suggests: treating the world as alive does. That’s not metaphysics. That’s not even mysticism. 

It’s just a more accurate way to be human.

I didn’t discover it in a lab. I discovered it standing in a boatyard in January, looking at a patch of gravel where my home used to be.







I’m not a guru. I’m not a life coach. I’m not someone who’s “figured it out.” But I’ve figured out some.

I’m a 52-year-old man who used to live on a boat and now lives in an apartment with two cats and too much yarn. I still struggle. I still have bad weeks. I still take medication. I still see a therapist.

I didn’t write this book because I’m “fixed.” I wrote it because I found a tool that helps me stay unfixed without falling apart.

I’m not the destination. I’m just a guy who walked this territory before you and decided to make a tool so you wouldn’t get as lost as I did.

I should tell you how the story of Northern Light ended - or more specifically, how her story, and ours, didn’t end.

After Christmas, the boatyard hauled her out. We stood in the cold January air, looking at her hull in the harsh winter light. Her lines had snapped during the storm - she’d been thrown against the dock over and over, bucking in the wind. Her hull was cracked. Deep in her belly, a seacock had broken. A single valve, a small piece of bronze that was supposed to keep the ocean out. When it failed, the sea rushed in.

All it took was one small breach.

We couldn’t afford to fix her, so we gave her away. A man came with a cool plan - he was going to dig a hole in his side yard, lower Northern Light into it, and turn her into a studio apartment. Our home would become someone’s writing room, art studio, whatever he dreamed she could be.

You might think this was the saddest possible ending for a boat that had been sailing since the 1980’s.

I wasn’t there when they took her.

I was at work. It was a Tuesday, or a Wednesday, maybe - I don’t remember which, because it was the kind of day the Rut eats without chewing. I was at my station, doing my job, and somewhere across town a truck was backing up to the boatyard and a man was strapping Northern Light to a trailer.

Amanda told me that evening. “She’s gone,” she said. And I felt - I don’t know what I felt. I thought it would just be relief, but it was deeper than that. Not grief exactly. I’d already grieved her. More like the feeling of reaching for something on a shelf and finding the shelf empty. The shape of the thing still in your hand, but the thing itself, gone. 

We drove past the boatyard a few days later. I don’t know why. Maybe we wanted to see the empty space. And there it was, just a patch of gravel where a boat had sat for weeks, with a dark stain where her hull had rested. Oil, or bottom paint, or just the shadow a heavy thing leaves on the ground when it’s been there long enough. The boatyard didn’t look different. Nobody walking by would notice anything missing. But I stood there and I could feel the absence like a loose tooth.

She was already in the man’s yard by then. He’d told us his plan - dig a hole, lower her in, turn her hull into a room. A writing studio, maybe. An art space. He wasn’t sentimental about it. He just saw a good hull and had an idea.

And I realized, standing in that empty patch of gravel, that I would probably never see her again. I would never know what he made of her. I would never sit inside her hull and feel the curve of the cabin I used to bump my head on in the dark. I would never know if someone was in there right now, making something, the way Amanda and I used to make our strange little life inside those same walls.

But someone might be. That’s the thing.

Somewhere, Northern Light is buried up to her gunwales in earth. The hull that held our coffee and our quail and our Nintendo Switch and our marriage through five Maine winters is holding someone else’s afternoon. She’s still a room with walls. She’s still a vessel. Still doing the one thing boats and homes and chairs and blue mugs all do - holding whoever’s inside them, without complaint, for as long as they’re needed.

She’s just not mine anymore.

I didn’t get the farewell scene. I didn’t get to touch her hull one last time. I was at work, doing the ordinary thing, on an ordinary day. And she left.

But she didn’t end. She transformed. And I wouldn’t have known that was possible if I hadn’t already spent two years learning to see the world as a place where things don’t die, they just become something else. A boat becomes a studio. A sinking becomes a book. A tool becomes a person.


And isn’t that how the Rut works too?

We don’t fall into the gray because we’re broken or lazy or ungrateful. We fall because one small thing gives way - a stressful month, a loss, a seacock of the soul. And once the breach opens, the water rushes in fast.

But here’s what I learned standing in that boatyard: You don’t have to keep sailing the same vessel forever. You can haul yourself out, assess the damage, and decide what comes next. For Amanda and me, what came next was land.

We have the apartment now. We have jobs we actually enjoy - we work together again, our 9th job together, and it still delights us.

Amanda’s crafting supplies have taken over the bedroom. Yarn bulges bins. Fabric stacks teeter on shelves. She’s creating things that didn’t exist before - quail sculptures made of cotton and starch, paper fabrics, scarves, a bowl of tiny raviolis made from resin. She’s finding wonder in texture and color and the slow rhythm of making something. She lives in wonder and I’m jealous and inspired by her.

Her loom sits by my chair, yarn tangled over it. The bins are full of skeins waiting to become something - scarves, blankets, weird sculptures. Some of that yarn has been sitting there for years. When Amanda finally pulls it out and starts weaving, the yarn stretches tight, locks into place, becomes fabric. It’s in flow. Now it’s doing what thread does best: holding things together.

We have two cats - our little wizards, Ron and Hermione.  Annie, our beloved rabbit who survived the boat years, passed away two years ago. We still miss her. She’s buried in the side yard, and mint grows there now.

And me? I’m still in therapy, but only once a month. Progress. I’m still on medication. I’m not “cured.” I don’t think I ever will be. But I’m better than functional. I’m present. I’m steering. I’m engaged and paying attention to my life. For a person who’s routinely struggled with deep depression, it feels like a miracle.

Most weekends, we try to do a day trip or an adventure - unless winter freezes us in at home, which is also good. Sometimes it’s just driving to the next town over for a scone. Sometimes we’re too tired and we lie on the couch watching shows we’ve seen before.

Some weekends, the Rut wins.

But then Monday comes, and we try again.

We’re not living the “dream” anymore. We’re living the life. And it turns out, the life is great.


That’s my story. The boat that sank. The life we built on land. The Compass I made to keep myself afloat.

I hope it was useful. But it was never really the point. You’re holding this book right now. You’ve read this far. And that means something.





This book has been with you for however long - days, weeks, months sprawled on your nightstand, or on your Kindle. The pages are probably dog-eared by now. Maybe there’s a coffee ring on the cover. The book doesn’t care. It said what it needed to say. And when you shove it on the shelf, it’ll sit there, spine out, ready to be pulled down when you forget everything and need a reminder. Books are good at that. They don’t get offended when you ignore them.

It’s almost over. You’re about to close it, put it on a shelf, go back to your life. The Caveman is already warming up. He’s getting ready to tell you that this was “nice” but not “practical.” He’s going to remind you of the dishes, the emails, the errands. He’s going to try to put you back to sleep. Don’t let him.

You picked up this book for a reason. Some part of you, the part that hasn’t been crushed by the routine, the part that still remembers what it feels like to be curious, called out for help. That part is still here. It’s been here the whole time, waiting under the gray, under the exhaustion, under the years of being told to hurry up and get back to work. It’s the part of you that used to lie on your back and watch clouds. The part that used to catch fireflies. The part that used to ask “Why?” about everything until the adults got tired of answering.

That part of you isn’t dead. It’s just lost. You can use the Wonder Compass to find it.

Your front door is right there. It’s been opening and closing for you for years, thousands of times, exposed to every season, and it never once asked where you were going. It doesn’t know the difference between a Tuesday errand and the first day of the rest of your life. It does the same thing either way. It swings. That’s what doors do. They’re the most optimistic objects in any house - every single time you reach for the handle, the door assumes you’ve decided to go somewhere. It’s been assuming that, patiently, all day. Even while you were on the couch. Even while you were scrolling. The door has been right there, doing its door thing, ready to swing the moment you give it a reason.

It doesn’t need to know your reason. It just needs the push.

The world hasn’t been waiting for you, though. It’s been busy. The trees have been growing whether you looked or not. The clouds have been moving. The moss has been spreading across rocks, soaking up rain, going dormant when it’s dry. None of it needs you. But all of it’s here. And when you finally look up, it’s still doing its thing.

The adventure isn’t over there. It was never over there.

It’s here. In your kitchen. On your street. In the parking lot of the grocery store where you buy the same things every week.

It’s in the question: “I wonder…?” It’s in the walk to the mailbox. The texture of the tree bark. The sentence you write on the back of a receipt. It’s in the decision to stop drifting and start steering. The Compass doesn’t take you away from your life. It brings you back to it.

The compass is in your hand. The world is waiting.

Let’s do!
